# Glyph joints: construction, use and context

## What this study is trying to show

An L can be one stroke turning a corner, or two pieces attached together.
The finished drawing can be identical. A T can stop an arrival, pass it
straight through, turn it, or offer a choice. Those possibilities need actual
connections and laws; the silhouette does not choose among them.

This study builds supplied examples of all 26 capital letters and ten digits,
then changes their construction histories, junction uses and selected ground
contexts. It asks which differences are preserved by Gojo structure and which
disappear under a simpler read. It also names some possible uses of recurring
structures—bend, attachment, bridge, choice, merge, crossover, enclosure and
support—without making those names into primitive instructions.

Open the [interactive atlas](results/glyph-joints/index.html). Its
[Markdown equivalent](results/glyph-joints/README.md) records the constructions,
probes and limits for repository readers. The page begins with the glyphs;
it then shows flow experiments and contact-span examples. This is a local
report, not a newly published website page.

```powershell
dotnet run --project experiments/QualitativeFrieze -- --glyph-joints
```

## Three questions, three different reads

| Question | Connected evidence | What it does not establish |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What is joined to what? | Sections refer to actual endpoint sites; shared references form incidences. | Coincident pixels do not weld independent sites. |
| How was it drawn? | Ordered stroke groups contain ordered uses of those sections, including reversed uses. | Pen direction does not automatically become a one-way traffic rule. |
| What can use the junction? | Exposed ports, ordered routes, finite control laws and material receiver capacity. | A visible branch does not automatically select an outlet or copy material. |

Ground, load, frame, closure and selected context supply further reads. They
cannot be collapsed into the joint's degree or a glyph label.

## Teaching order and visible landmarks

All 26 capitals and ten digits have been reviewed against the supplied school
cards, the [Learning Without Tears capital formation chart](https://www.lwtears.com/sites/default/files/2021-12/HWT%20Capital%20Letter%20Formation%20Chart.pdf),
and the [Zaner-Bloser manuscript practice package](https://media.zaner-bloser.com/handwriting/pdfs/ZB_HW_Manuscript_Practice_Package.pdf).
The selected baselines follow the supplied cards where they give a convention;
the publisher guides fill the remaining cases. These are teaching conventions,
not universal handwriting orders. J and T retain bar-first baselines from the
cards and stem-first alternatives; G also has a separately attached-bar variant.
The baseline 4 has the card's diagonal first arm, and 5 has its continuous,
top-bar-first stroke. The publisher's alternatives are noted rather than silently
substituted.

The first stems of B, D, E, F, H, I, K, M, N, P and R descend. This does not mean
every later vertical movement must descend: the selected N returns to its top,
descends diagonally and then rises along its right stem. A uses two descending
legs from the apex; X uses two descending diagonals; O, Q and 0 start at the top
and travel leftwards around the contour. The figure-eight starts at its top,
not its waist.

The main drawing now shows green start rings, red stop dots, direction arrows
and dashed pen-lift relocations. Selecting a stroke leaves earlier ink visible
and fades later ink. Each stroke explains its evidence:

| Example | Construction read | Distinction preserved |
| --- | --- | --- |
| L corner | Continue down the stem and turn right without lifting. | A continuous turn. |
| F top bar | Return to the existing top endpoint, then leave rightwards. | A constructed endpoint attachment, not the L's continuous turn. |
| F middle bar | Return to an interior landmark and leave orthogonally. | A later outward branch or secondary route. |
| F incoming-bars variant | Approach the stem from outside and stop there. | Arrival and stop, rather than departure. |
| H second stem | Locate a new start relative to the first stem's top, then descend parallel to it. | A supplied offset reference, not a shared site. |
| H bridge | Start at one existing middle landmark and stop at the other. | A landmark-to-landmark join. |
| B bowls | Return to the top, meet the existing middle landmark, then continue to the bottom. | Meeting need not mean stopping. |
| 6 and 8 | Revisit a landmark already reached earlier in the same stroke. | Earlier evidence does not require a pen lift. |

The construction column is separate from final geometric labels such as
T-like or crossing-like. A construction stop means the pen stops there; it
does not install a barrier for a later material flow. Landmark availability
is inspected chronologically from connected visits, not inferred from the
finished silhouette or the letter's name. Midline sites are supplied in these
fixtures; this pass does not learn where a writer would estimate them.

## Native encoding

The study uses the existing native relation, curve, finite-law and packet
transport mechanisms. It does not implement the proposed consolidated
plane-law substrate or change the primitive ODV word.

For the corpus, the selected grammar is:

```text
glyph surface = (frame -> closed section inventory) -> ordered stroke groups
section       = (start site -> end site) -> (control words -> interpolation scheme)
stroke        = ordered uses of sections
use           = actual section -> ((chosen start -> chosen end) -> ordered control-word references)
placement     = ((target stroke -> earlier source stroke) ->
                 (earlier landmark site -> target start site)) -> offset word
construction input = glyph surface -> closed placement inventory
```

A straight section has two control words. Curved sections use the existing
connected interpolation scheme, with shared parameter and intermediate
outputs; they are not replaced with many little straight strokes. The chosen
control words are an authored realization in a shared, uncalibrated reach.
They are more specific than a bare pre-numeric shape. Their pixel positions
are a rendering, not a claim about the required proportions of a letter.

The order of stroke groups records pen lifts. Sharing a site across groups
preserves attachment in the finished object. Reusing a section in two groups
retains one source section with two uses; it does not silently double the
material or the source ink inventory. Reversing a use changes its ordered
frame without rewriting the source section.

Pen-stroke groups are historical uses, not a material-strength model. Calling
one an attachment does not supply a bolt, hinge, rigid bond or elastic law.
Those would require additional connected member/interface structure and
settlement rules. The present experiment preserves the construction distinction
and tests explicit passage laws; it does not infer mechanical properties from
pen lifts.

The construction reader retains actual previous sections, source strokes,
incoming and outgoing visits, and completed visit prefixes in native event
witnesses. The incoming section does not count as evidence that its destination
was already available before arrival. Future landmarks, foreign sites and
incompatible placement offsets remain unresolved. H's offset is an authored
realization of an estimated spacing, not a universal distance requirement.
`construction.gojo` preserves the glyph, supplied placements and read witnesses;
the labels can be discarded and the chronology read again.

The joint-use fixtures connect their own incident sections to exposed ports
and route witnesses. A control law selects route candidates. A material step
then asks the existing packet transport to accept those requests under the
connected receiver capacities. These are distinct stages: possible routes
are not already executed routes. Their retained native archives include both
the apparatus and its observations, so archive byte size is not the minimum
active-state size of a junction.

These passage steps are atomic transfers between exposed ports. The ordered
interior sites certify that the route is connected; they do not simulate travel
time, intermediate occupation or capacity at every position along it. A stop
probe holds its packet at the input port, rather than modeling motion up to
the crossbar and then impact with it.

ODV's V remains a magnitude read at the selected resolution. Selecting a
stroke, route or context does not write V=0 into excluded elements. Hiding
a stroke in this atlas changes the displayed selection; it does not remove
that stroke from the native source or install a gate on an actual passage.

## Important comparisons

The recorded run contains **36 glyphs, 51 constructions, 16 joint-use
machines, 21 route probes, and 12 contact contexts**. The constructions are
supplied examples; the joint, topology, route and contact answers are reads
of their connected native structure.

Even a site with two incidences is not one geometric concept. In this corpus,
L has a bend, O has tangent continuations, and the middle of 3 has a fold-back:
its two outward tangents point the same way. None of those geometric readings
installs a traffic law or a material coupling by itself.

- **L bend versus attachment:** compare the same sections and endpoint
  topology with different stroke groups. A later use law can allow the same
  continuation in either construction, or different ones.
- **F:** downward stem, endpoint attachment and outward interior branch make different
  local positions available. The intermediate site is a real incidence,
  not a decorative mark on an unsplit stroke.
- **T:** a stop, a permitted turn, a conditional choice and a receiving merge
  can share a projected outline. Their input/output relations differ.
- **H:** two downward stems precede their joining bar. The second stem's start
  references the first stem's top plus an offset. Drawing the connecting bar left to right does not establish the
  direction in which a later observer or material packet may cross it.
- **X:** two crossing strokes can remain independent. A shared centre is an
  additional topological commitment, not a consequence of drawing the same
  pixels.
- **Loops:** graph cycle rank is read from finite selected connectivity.
  It is not an enclosed area measurement, a typographic counter count in
  every font, or proof that a controller is repeatedly executing a loop.
- **A and V:** the support examples explicitly supply contacts and a query
  projection. A single contact cannot become a broad base simply because two
  arms meet there.
- **N and Z:** different baseline structure can give the same coarse
  contact-span answer. The remaining difference must be inspected at another
  read, rather than invented by this one.

## What a support read actually says

The context surface connects a frame, a declared similarity basis, selected
sections, an ordered ground reference, selected actual contact sites and a
load-position query. The reader validates site positions and contact
membership, projects onto the chosen reference, and compares the result
with the selected contact span.

Its answers are **inside**, **boundary**, **outside**, or **unresolved**.
They concern a supplied geometric condition. They do not solve forces,
stiffness, friction, mass distribution, tipping, balance through time or
material failure. In particular, a V on its point receives a boundary answer,
not a universal statement that the physical V must fall. Extra supports,
fasteners or active control could change the situation.

An empty selected contact set supplies no support answer. An open contact
inventory also remains unresolved: it would be incorrect to treat an
incomplete list as a complete list of all available supports. Position
agreement is only at the selected word resolution; it is not proof of an
infinitely precise physical zero.

## From glyphs to meanings

A bridge, switch, support or workspace is a useful candidate interpretation
when the corresponding relations and use laws are present. The study tests
specific affordances. It does not encode every possible use of a bridge, or
derive the meaning of a table from a T-shaped drawing.

A broader concept can include the object, its exposed surfaces, other
participants, actions, goals, outcomes and history. These can be connected
as further local contexts without forcing them all into one noun boundary.
The present transfer cards distinguish executed route mechanisms from
proposed mechanical or semantic uses. They are a small, supplied vocabulary,
not learned concept discovery or an exhaustive inventory of meaning.

## Verification and next experiments

The focused tests check native round trips, full glyph coverage, history
validity, actual shared sites, uncertain control, material accounting,
receiver contention, absent laws, and false contact reads. The report can be
regenerated from the fixture code; the tests do not infer success from a
recognizable picture.

Recorded verification on 9 November 2026: 78 focused glyph test cases passed;
`dotnet test Gojo.slnx` passed 6,943 tests across 18 suites. The focused checks
were repeated after the final 6-tail drawing correction and presentation
wording changes. Browser checks visited all 51 constructions and 93 strokes,
every route probe and context, checked start/stop markers against native
chronology, dropdown focus and click navigation, and found no script errors
or page overflow at 1440px and 360px widths. All 36 baseline renderings were
also inspected together.

The next useful experiment would select one of these structures under partial
evidence, try a connected use, retain its success or remaining tension, and
compare it with another construction that produces similar ink. That would
begin learning which interpretation is useful in a context. This pass builds
the inspectable alternatives and counterexamples needed for that experiment.
