# Three designs from each skeleton

Art direction for the full Bourgoin colour atlas. These are authored design
choices, not aesthetic theorems or new native geometry laws. Read with
[the Jones source notes](jones-colour-notes.md). The existing skeleton is kept
in all three designs; added native contours, branches and attachments change.

## Three distances, three decorations

- **Overall disposition:** broad connected bands, substantial compartment
  panels, nested outlines or a sparse large medallion establish the field.
  Smaller marks remain quiet. The large construction should read at thumbnail
  size before any flower is identified.
- **Intermediate motif:** a repeated palmette, leaf spray, rosette, fan or
  conventional blossom follows the source's actual local orientation. Its
  silhouette carries the design. The scaffold becomes quieter but remains
  visible. Use different silhouettes across families.
- **Fine ornament:** small paired buds, seeds, vein strokes, corner fleurons or
  edge marks enrich the same skeleton. Prefer details distributed along its
  flow to another large flower at every centre. Repetition makes a textile-like
  texture at a distance; close inspection reveals the individual marks.

The difference must survive rendering every line in the same ink: separate
native enrichment sets are required. Contrast and colour then clarify those
differences. All three can contain subsidiary marks; each needs one clearly
dominant scale.

These are starting directions. A good fitted alternative is preferable to
forcing a motif into a compartment too small for it. An empty gap is useful
counterform, not a mandatory place to add decoration.

## A small reusable ornament vocabulary

- An eight- or twelve-chord **lens leaf**, with a narrow neck, full middle and
  pointed tip. This reads less like a diamond than the earlier four-chord leaf.
- A **lanceolate leaf**, longer and narrower, useful on diagonal stems.
- A three-leaf **trefoil**, using one actual shared leaf prototype and three
  justified images in the selected local basis.
- A four-, six- or eight-part **rosette**, whose native turns follow the source
  family. A quiet centre prevents the petal junction from becoming a blot.
- A three- or five-lobed **palmette / fan**, useful where growth has a preferred
  direction rather than a radial centre.
- **Paired leaflets**, a bud, a seed and a short vein stroke, for fine work.
- A **nested contour**, for panels and large-scale emphasis when botanical
  decoration would obscure an already rich interlock.

Small finite chords conventionally suggest curves. These contours do not
claim an analytic curve, exact tangent, circle or equal-angle theorem. Native
source anchors and offset witnesses remain distinct from a physical join.

Broader filled silhouettes need a dark outer edge and a thinner pale or warm
separator. A warm accent should occupy considerably less area than its quiet
support. Change which role is broad between the three designs; the whole atlas
should not use one dark-ground formula repeatedly. A few near-monochrome
designs give the stronger combinations room to breathe.

Jones's practical lessons are hierarchy (proposition 7), balancing straight,
inclined and curved tendencies (10), visible growth from a parent (11–13),
colour distinguishing forms (14), strong accents over quiet masses (16), a
coherent distant effect (22), and separating edges (28–32). His historic
judgements and colour ratios are not modern psychophysical laws. Fitness here
is a visual review under those stated aims, not a solved aesthetic score.

## Visual review

1. Thumbnail: can the major disposition still be named?
2. Medium view: does a repeated ornament emerge with a convincing silhouette?
3. Close view: do details attach and flow, rather than float arbitrarily?
4. Compare all three in one ink: is the geometry actually different?
5. Check repeated boundaries: keep accidental rosette tangles, clipped tips
   and uneven intersections from masking the original structure.
6. Check white space: no need to fill every gap, but obvious large dead
   compartments and overloaded small ones should trigger reconsideration.
7. Preserve the source's distinctions: a detached end stays detached; a sliding
   row stays slid; a visual crossing does not silently acquire a join.

## A lesson from the first visual pass

Repeated fine details can become a larger pattern at shared landmarks. In the
small B16 triangular mesh, several pairs of short leaves accumulated around
each grid vertex and made broad mustard-coloured bands. The individual leaf
was small in its own basis, but the resulting visual read overwhelmed the
intended fine scale. The revised B15–B18 fine designs therefore use one small
corner kite and a seed in each selected compartment centre: two or three
introduced marks per macrocell. This is an authored density adjustment, not a
general automatic fitness law. The rejected pattern can still be interesting
as a different large-scale design.

Another useful distinction was to give the overall and intermediate designs
different main silhouettes. An overall polygonal panel, jewel or fan-shaped
mass now contrasts with an intermediate flower or leaf spray; merely adding a
stem to the same flower was too close to the earlier design.

The visual review covered three designs for each of the 36 retained sources.
It checked silhouettes, source counterforms, local attachments, apparent
intersections, palette roles and differences between the three readings. Small
details sometimes form a conspicuous larger repeat of their own, especially
in B20, B21, B23 and B27; these were retained as useful alternatives rather
than enforcing one universal degree of quietness. The proposed final fine
presentation also reduces the opacity of source-panel fills while retaining
their outlines. This separates display emphasis from native geometry.
