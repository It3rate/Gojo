# Detail studies that belong to their scaffold

Art direction for the next ornament round. These are authored proposals and
visual review criteria, not new Gojo laws or a solved aesthetic score. The
new studies should be readable as small reusable constructions before they
are repeated through an existing pattern.

## Why B02 and B05 need different details

The previous B02 fine design puts several angular leaflets at selected path
turns, with separate seeds in the rectangles. The chosen turns are real native
landmarks, but that alone does not make the resulting ornament follow the
rectangle's long axis. Some leaflets occupy the corners and square gaps, so
they read as objects placed on the lattice rather than as enriched woven
strips.

The previous B05 fine design similarly puts leaf pairs at hexagon vertices
and a three-part mark near the centre. Repetition makes these resemble local
clusters, but the clusters do not clearly describe either the sixfold hexagon
or the triangular counterfield. A more deliberate radial centre is a better
first integration.

The historical guidance supports this distinction. In Christopher Dresser's
[*Principles of Decorative Design*, chapters IV–V](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/39749/39749-h/39749-h.htm),
figures 64–79 compare lateral plant views with radial views from above. He
relates the former to directed sprays and the latter to centred ornaments.
Later in chapter V, he asks for an intelligible geometric arrangement with
additional detail visible at nearer distances. Here that suggests an axial
sprig inside each B02 strip, and a radial flower inside each B05 hexagon.
These are selected design principles, not universal restrictions on wallpapers
or a claim that bilateral floor designs are inherently wrong.

Jones's parent-stem, conventionalisation and hierarchy guidance remains useful;
see [the earlier primary-source notes](jones-colour-notes.md). A source anchor
and a visible flowing attachment answer different questions: preserve the
native witness, but also make the intended growth legible in the image.

## Ten proposed close-up studies

The names below identify studies for discussion. They are not native operation
tags. Each study should expose an actual stem, centre, spoke system, guide
rail or path-end relationship, with its embellishment connected through that
surface. Budgets are targets for introduced finite chords in one unit, before
field repetition; fewer good contours are preferable to dense hatch marks.

| D02 Three-leaf running vine | A finite, gently turning stem with three alternating leaves; entry and exit directions agree, so a longer vine can be composed. Avoid a leaf at every control point. | B08, B08.B, B15, B32, B33 | 35–60 |

The rosettes, palmette and lotus should have visibly different silhouettes.
Do not obtain the whole library by varying the number of equal diamonds.
Eight to twelve chords can suggest a curved leaf or petal at sufficient native
resolution. If the available reach is too coarse to distinguish shoulders
from a tip, choose a simpler pointed contour or a finer declared basis.
Increasing its footprint to hide the limitation would spoil its fit.

Useful image references are Dresser's plant-view figures 64–79 and
alternation figure 15 in the linked edition, and Jones's Moresque plates XLI–
XLIII and Celtic ribbon plates LXIII–LXIV in
[*The Grammar of Ornament*](https://library.si.edu/digital-library/book/grammarornament00jone).
The existing cached plate XLII was especially useful for separating broad
motif masses from smaller line and flower enrichment. The proposed bead-and-leaf
chain is our composite construction, not a claimed facsimile of one historic
figure. The ribbon plates inspire the plait's appearance; they do not prove
the native model has recovered their knot topology.

## Exact B02 placement advice

Current source construction: one rectangle with coarse bounds
`x=[-1,1], y=[-2,2]`, repeated twice upright and twice after an actual
quarter-related word correspondence. Its long edges already expose side
contacts. At four fine reaches per coarse reach, the four local centres are
`(0,0)`, `(12,0)`, `(12,12)` and `(0,12)`, with period words `(24,0)` and
`(0,24)`. Horizontal units use the same ornament transformed with their source
rectangle.

For an upright rectangle, retain the ornament inside
`x=[-2,2], y=[-6,6]` in that fine authoring basis. This leaves two fine reaches
to each side and short end of the original `8 by 16` rectangle. The square gap
between one set of neighbours is `x=[4,8], y=[4,8]`: keep it quiet in this
round. The gap remains an emergent counterform, not a square glyph to paint.

Three useful comparisons are:

1. **Axial sprig:** a stem from `(0,-6)` to `(0,6)`, two or three alternating
   leaves inside the envelope, and a terminal bud. Put a single fine vein in
   each leaf rather than several dense vein strokes. Quarter-transform the
   complete construction for the horizontal strips.
2. **Beaded bud / linked chain:** a short central chain occupies the same long
   axis. Two collar leaves frame its middle or terminal bud. Keep bead radii
   small enough that the chain remains elongated; it should not become three
   touching medallions filling the strip's width.
3. **Interrupted plait:** two slim strands occupy the inner strip and cross a
   small number of times. Reserve a clear space before both short ends. Do
   not continue them through the square gaps or join them to a neighbouring
   strip merely because their rendered strokes touch.

An actual source port at `(-4,-8)` can anchor the first sprig position
`(0,-6)` through the explicit offset `(4,2)`. These numbers count native
unmeasured reaches for authoring; they are not new measured carriers. Actual
ports, offset words and witnesses must remain in the saved construction.

Visible outline width and halos need their own display allowance inside this
margin. Centreline containment alone does not prove that all rendered ink is
contained, or that native occupied faces exist.

## Exact B05 placement advice

The source is one six-edge contour, not a separate hexagon glyph plus triangle
glyphs. At four fine reaches per coarse reach its local boundary vertices are
`(8,0), (0,8), (-8,8), (-8,0), (0,-8), (8,-8)`. The source's triangular
presentation basis interprets its two ordered directions as a triangular
frame. Keep the radial correspondence consistent with that basis.

Start with one **six-petal rosette at the actual local centre**. Use a finite
outer reach of five, or at most six, fine steps in its sixfold images. This
leaves a visible interval between the petal tips and the hexagon edges in the
selected display. A one-reach centre seed or small collar is enough. The
outer contour supplies the disposition; the flower supplies the intermediate
unit; thin veins or small internal seeds supply the close detail.

For example, the actual boundary port at `(8,0)` can be the source for the
explicit centre offset `(-8,0)`. Derive or retain the centre through that
correspondence; do not pretend that a bounding-box average is a new native
port. Replicate the complete rosette with the hexagon's disposition, rather
than independently scattering petals near its vertices.

First leave the triangular counterfield clear. A later comparison may add a
small three-part bud at a centre derived from the actual three bounding
participants. That would require its own landmark correspondence and fit
read. It is not the same construction as selecting the centre of one hexagon,
and it should not be simulated by placing a free triangle icon in the gap.

## Crossings, joins and interrupted strands

Keep three separate claims visible:

- Independent actual paths may cross in the selected display without sharing
  a native site.
- An authored interruption has actual terminal and start ports with a known
  nonzero displacement. The current `OrnamentStrandGap` contract can retain
  such a read; it does not join those ends.
- A continuously traversable knot with a specified over/under order needs
  additional structural evidence. Neither a gap witness nor renderer paint
  order establishes that topology, hidden continuity, clearance or passage.

For D09, prefer explicit native interrupted paths to a white rectangle drawn
over an otherwise claimed continuous native strand. Label it as an interrupted
plait and keep its gap endpoints inspectable. D10 instead retains a genuinely
closed native outer contour; its decorative loop does not carry over/under
order. Make any displayed interruption wide enough that dark edging and pale
keylines do not reconnect it by accident. Do not use the word
"accepted" in a gap witness as though it meant a strand transfer was accepted.

## Visual review before repeating

1. The close-up should make the stem, centre, rails or path-end arrangement
   obvious without reading its title.
2. Use one strong silhouette, one subsidiary articulation and only then small
   veins, beads or collars. A dark central blot is a failure of the read.
3. Compare a unit with its scaffold visible, then the repeated field with the
   scaffold quiet. Its neighbours should strengthen the intended rhythm.
4. In B02, verify every ornament rotates with its strip and the square gaps
   stay open. In B05, verify sixfold centres and clear triangular counterforms.
5. Test display widths as well as native paths. A valid offset witness is not
   an automatic visual fit or non-overlap theorem.
6. In plait studies, follow each coloured strand by eye and inspect all gap
   endpoints. Every apparent overpass must have an honest stated basis.
7. Keep the first repeated fields small. Ten rich single-unit studies plus a
   few selected integrations are more useful here than another large batch of
   almost identical decorations.

## Visual outcomes

The first native renders make D11 and D12 convincing B02 integrations. D11's
willow sprigs rotate with each strip; D12's large feather-veined leaf occupies
the same interior envelope. Leaves and veins follow the long axes rather than
the macrocell corners, and the emergent square gaps remain quiet. The actual
integration basis uses eight fine reaches per coarse reach, doubling the
four-reach authoring coordinates above.

D13's six-petal flower fits within the B05 hexagon and leaves a clear interval
at its tips. Its final four-by-four execution shows four visible flowers and
their triangular counterforms, in addition to the separate close-up. D14's
inward buds follow the same sixfold correspondence while leaving an open
hexagonal heart. The two designs give outward-growth and inward-growth reads
of the same outer scaffold.

Three first-pass adjustments were identified. D03's shared-origin petals
overlap slightly just outside the centre bead; moving their necks outward and
shortening their bodies keeps the same guide tips while separating the bases.
D10's original axial compartments protrude past the narrower loop waists;
diagonal compartments are a better fit to its actual diagonal lobes. D09's
continuous diagonal is present, but its quiet linework loses salience beside
large filled pearls. Stronger strand contrast and outline-only pearls improve
that read without changing its native gap.

The corrected D03 and D10 close-ups passed the visual check: D03's exposed
guide landmarks now supply separate petal necks, and all four small D10
compartments sit on actual diagonal guides within the lobes instead of
crossing the waists. The final D09 display makes its continuous lilac strand
easy to follow beside the thinner copper interrupted portions; outline
pearls remain secondary. Its native interruption is unchanged.

The final four-by-four B02 and B05 integrations retain their intended strip or
radial organisation, with no apparent unwanted neighbouring overlap. These
are checks of the shown finite designs; they do not establish a general
occupied-face clearance or aesthetic theorem.
