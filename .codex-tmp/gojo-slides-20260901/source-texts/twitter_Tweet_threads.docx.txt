[normal] The goal: Understanding our own thought mechanics so we can better emulate it, and ultimately interact with AI at a conceptual level. I think this is a computationally simpler problem than deep learning, and AI will be a combination of both eventually.
[normal] Maybe that seems hand-wavy. When we hear 'car' and get that concept, what is the concept made of? Is a car a whole bunch of mini-ideas, or a single one? Is it just a picture? How much overlap is there with 'truck', or is that totally different?
[normal] We can *quickly* tell the difference between "feeling the blues" and "feeling the oranges". What are those things made of that they can jump around so easily? If they're made of mini-concepts, what are the lowest level 'primitive' concepts there?
[normal] Even in single words, we can see many things going on. A concept like 'move' is very generic and simple. We append other concepts to it as it gets more specific: give, toss, send, deliver, distribute.
[normal] 'Distribute' has multiple copies of something going to multiple locations, what is our mental model of that, just words? A 2D image with moving shapes? Some specific thing actually getting distributed? How can we figure out what we're hallucinating to give us these ideas?
[normal] Can we reverse engineer our 'output' to figure out what is going on inside? Look at the simplest things like gestures, ornamentation, cartoons, sounds, glyphs, physics sense, or memes. Are these minimal concepts reduced to minimal representations?
[normal] In computer languages, there are things called terminals (primitives) in the grammar. These are things that can't be further reduced. Are all concepts made of primitives? Would our primitives be things like food, friend, danger? Maybe motion, energy, quantity, size? Emotions?
[normal] Are there a fixed number of these primitives that all thoughts are constructed out of? Are they constant? Inate? Universal? How many would there be -- 10s, 100s, 1000s, millions? Is there overlap? Does 'struck by a hand' and 'struck by an idea' reuse anything?
[normal] How to figure out these 'primitives'? What are the simplest gestures that convey big and small? The fewest lines that make happy and sad? The minimum sound to convey disappointment? The fewest strokes to capture a pattern?
[normal] At a higher level: words, sentences, stories, music, sports, numbers, logic, emotions, processes, organizations... These are complex, but there is something similar with a story arc, a gestural arc, a ball's arc, and the arc of a process. A goal, shit happens, a result, repeat.
[normal] Lastly, as we figure these things out, how do we encode it? An encoding exists that is low bandwidth enough for us to all sync on, because we do. Is this more dependent on how our brains are wired, or on the limitations of what the world can be?

[normal] Change is the basis for all perception and understanding, because without it we have nothing to compare.
[normal] When you dig right to the bottom, the thing that we lock onto for all perception is change. We track it at all scales relevant to us, and we are very attuned to the characteristics and patterns of changes. We easily transfer these patterns across domains.
[normal] At the lowest level there are sensory changes, changes over time (motion and scaling), and changes of focus. We are sensitive to slight changes in light, sound, texture or motion. We understand static photos through changes in light.
[normal] It is worth looking really really close at what change is then, as it is the basis for everything. When looking at these kinds of ‘mind primitives’, it is important to look with first principles. Our intuitions are mostly correct, but are far too embedded to be easily understood.
[normal] Most simply, change has a start, a duration, and an end. The start and end is chosen by the observer, as everything is always changing. The duration isn't just over time. For example, the change from a city to country happens over a distance.
[normal] This describes change in properties that can be measured. Things that can be counted (objects) are made of collections of these property ranges and their relations (definitions). More on those later.
[normal] The start and end points of a change are chosen, so they map to some definition of the chooser. Duration is the measure between them. The one rule of the duration of change is it can't be zero length.
[normal] This feels right and wrong to our intuition - something is moving or not moving, no in betweens. Yet if there is zero distance between those states, how can there be change? The confusion comes from how easily we can scale and ignore precision, our superpower.
[normal] So start, motion, end. There are two more obvious elements to add here. First, there must be potential for change at the start point, otherwise there would be no change. It follows that by the end, that potential has been reduced by the amount of change that happened.
[normal] Second, the potential for change doesn’t mean it’s certain - all transforms require work, and other things can affect that. A rock may never roll down a hill, or it may get pushed. It may hit a wall half way down, or might miss it. Interactions that assist or impede transformations are always part of our change calculus.
[normal] So select something with potential, define a start condition, duration with obstacles until some defined end condition. These are the primitives of how we perceive change. Is this because of how our brain works, or how change works?
[normal] We use 'change' as the encoding for everything. Not just motion or senses, but stories, music, sentences, equations, paths, emotions, plans. At all scales, and over moments or eons. Mini-stories are the predigested change-patterns running our conscious minds.
[normal] I’ll die on that hill. Maybe alone :).
[normal] Not only do our thoughts run on these change sequences, but reuse the change patterns themselves as the basis for all metaphor. You can drop a ball, then you can drop a call, a boyfriend, or just drop by. Something is held, then let go, ending on the ground.
[normal] The last part of change we care about, and the most important to us, is the predictability. We broadly divide predictability of change into animate and inanimate, because animate things are much harder to predict (and tend to be tasty and hungry).
[normal] To predict inanimate things, like the motion of a falling tree or a setting sun, we can extrapolate using previous experience and physics. We aren’t perfect at this, but we aren’t usually too surprised. When inanimate change becomes too surprising we assign animate forces to it.
[normal] Change with living things, like watching a bug or passing a car, requires us to get ‘in the other mind' to make predictions. A lot of what we care about is animate, so many of our change models are based on the forces acting on other animate things.
[normal] Our minds are constantly predicting the future (everything we see is a prediction of up to half a second into the future!). We are keyed to notice differences with expectation and reality (surprise), as that is where we adapt and learn.
[normal] The primitive for encoding thought in this system, the ‘number’ if you will, is directed segment. A start, a transition, and an end. It can get more detailed with start and end conditions, different resolutions and limits, relations and equations.
[normal] I think this pattern should be the basis for encoding concepts in AI, and is what I’m using. This has led to many experiments with animation, patterns, design, machine teaching, and…
[normal] …ultimately a fairly different, more organic math system for encoding it. More on this next.
