[Title] Arithmetic with Segments
[normal] Arithmetic with Segments
[normal] The last article talked about how multiplication of segments can be thought of as two squeegees cleaning a window. One cleans each side which creates four quadrants (both dirty, back clean, front clean, and both clean).
[normal] An interesting unlock here is, it allows treating these quadrants as Boolean objects even though they have length or area. With two inputs, you get the four possible areas that have 16 possible combinations.
[normal] Now if the clean areas are 0, and the dirty areas are 1, we get ‘quantified boolean’ areas:
[normal] Which also works in one dimension. Note the overlap in one dimension is position to position (relative) rather than point to point (absolute). Also multiplication works differently because the two values need to be compressed into a single dimension.
[normal] So the two inputs, A and B, generate four areas, and those areas can be combined in 16 possible ways. This Boolean truth table allows isolating any combination of those areas. These isolated areas can be quantified numerically, and the results can be combined to create mathematical operations and equations. The advantage here is you have one base layer system that you can use to derive different aspects of arithmetic and mathematics.
[normal] There are many ways to combine two segments, but the process will be the same. The steps are:
[normal] Determine primary points of interest
[normal] Assign sections to Boolean categories
[normal] Quantify their values as lengths, areas etc
[normal] Combine and extract results
[normal] Recurse/repeat into equations
[normal] Points of Interest
[normal] Two segments, along with a zero point for calibration, create five points of interest, which will create four potential areas. This is true in 1D or 2D. There are two ways to generate these areas — for comparisons and addition we use position to position (relative positions), and for ratios and multiplication we use point to point (absolute positions from zero). This is true no matter where the calibrating zero point is placed. There are other points of interest, like midpoints and mirrored points, but these are second order.
[normal] Notice how if these segments are anchored at zero most areas will cancel. This represents our normal numbers, and shows they are a subset of segment numbers. All operations discussed here are still valid, just many of them are no longer meaningful.
[normal] These four areas of interest allow asking comparison questions, like, “does a quadrant have a non zero value?” (bool), “are two values the same?” (==), ?is A greater than B?” (>), etc. We can also get information by observing the points, like max/min values, negative portions, direction, polarity, and tests for mirroring and symmetry. While there are many ways to inspect these divisions, it is possible to come up with an exhaustive list. These are primary building blocks of ordered interactions.
[normal] Assign to Boolean values
[normal] If two segments are merged, there are four possible overlaps (none, both, A only or B only). There are 16 possible combinations of these that allow isolating any areas of interest. It is useful to pause and think about these in concrete terms. Like A could be the writing I have left in this article, and B the illustrations I have left. NOT B would be the completed illustrations, XOR would be the partially completed sections, and AND would be the remaining work. As you can see, these concepts can be very intuitive, and Boolean operations are a good way of isolating an area of interest.
[normal] The ‘true’ can represent ‘todo’ or ‘completed’ here. This is always true, which is why every Boolean operation has an inverse. This also suggests that ‘false’ doesn’t represent nothing, it is a measure that is the opposite of ‘true’. The correct way to represent this is a negated segment in the same position. This may seem impossible, but in fact a number line moving in the opposite direction does this (positive left rather than positive right). This inverse representation allows combining true and false is reasoned ways.
[normal] Quantify Areas
[normal] We have four points on two segments, and a zero point. By defining a ‘1’ point we complete the unit basis, allowing us to quantify these Boolean areas. This value can be in the form of lengths or areas, and can be partial, directed, and inverted.
[normal] The other thing to note is, these calibrated number lines do not have to be linear, they can be log scale (or otherwise accelerated), a repeating signal like a sine wave, or any mathematical function. A non linear number line is just a normal number line with valid operations applied to it.
[normal] Whatever the calibration, two measures will be merged into one. The method of merging can just be yes/no (bool), one for one (addition), scaled with each other (multiplication), or repetitions (powers). All logical systems of merging are valid math, but these are the primitives.
[normal] Combining as Needed
[normal] Now we can start operating on areas of interest. The correct operation will depend on what you want to know. We are used to there being a single correct way to multiply, and a single correct subtraction, but that is mostly an artifact of our regular numbers having only one option. Again, you can construct these intuitively just by looking at them. How much more A has been done than B? (Transfer A - Transfer B). What ratio of the work is completed? (True/AND). Stretch A by B? [normal] The correct way to combine two values is to combine them in a way that produces the result you are looking for. Just that.
[normal] Equations
[normal] While it is beyond the scope of this article, you can see how more complex equations could be formed by combining more inputs, looping outputs back into inputs, etc. Combining this with the testable Boolean operations even allows a form of programming to emerge.
[normal] How Segment Math Applies to AI
[normal] One of the difficulties with teaching machines to use mathematics is that arithmetic itself lacks a unified conceptual substrate. Or maybe better said, this substrate is assumed -- it is the thing arithmetic is answering questions about, the thing we experience every moment we're alive. We have an intuitive sense of how gravity works, without any calculation. Gravity is clarified and quantified with mathematics, but it doesn't emerge from it.
[normal] Math gives us a large toolbox of operations, things like addition, ratios, squaring and comparisons. We use these tools to describe and predict how ordered things will affect each other in the real world. There are many ways these things can interact, and the most common ways end up as symbols on our keyboards (+-/*=%^!|&<>~). A computer doesn't have this innate sense of the world though, so we need to present it with a full view of 'raw order interacting'. With this it can learn the context where different operations have predictive power. Like us, it will discover that the simple addition of lengths is a more common operation than taking a cube root – but it needs to discover that itself, from 'the full experience', in order to have a context.
[normal] So what is this ‘substrate’? It is every way ordered things can affect each other, along with some unpredictable disorder sprinkled in. This is the mathematical description of the soup that reality emerges from, and the substrate that AI must live in.
