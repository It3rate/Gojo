[Title] Pin, Accumulate, and Merge
[Heading 2] The Business of Magnitudes
[Title] The reason it's hard to imagine exactly what multiplication will do in higher dimensions is because we don't have a great mental model of what exactly we mean by multiplication. Or even addition for that matter. In fact, there are a few stages to operations. First they are selected, pinned together, and an operation is chosen. Then things can be joined at one point, merged point to point, merged every point to every point, or merged point to point by a Boolean rule. Finally, these expansions can be folded back into their original dimensions. Specifically, pinning selects the operating space, merging combines elements within it, and folding collapses the resulting space down to its simpler original form.
[Heading 3] Pinning
[Title] Imagine you have two measures. They're separate and you are thinking of ways to compare them together. Let's take hands of playing cards in a Go Fish game. Alice has 3 cards, Bob has 6 cards. First off, we need to establish what we are comparing. Alice and Bob might be in two different games, or in two different cities. To compare, you have to join them together minimally. So mentally take 'one point' on Alice's cards, and join it to 'one point' on Bob's hand. Geometrically it might look like one of these:
[Title] At this point, the geometry doesn't really matter; the important thing is that these two sets of cards are put into the same context by joining them somehow into a single space. This is not adding them together, or combining them in any way. This is the first stage of any math operation: the selection of elements and pinning them together. This pinned space becomes a frame you can inspect, add data to, and perform operations in.. Imagine picking a new card; you could choose it from either Alice or Bob, or add one to their hands. With the frame as a reference, you can track what happens. It's basically a space where everything lives, and it tells us we are considering these together, their basic structures, and we can encode and track (numerically) every element that exists in the space.
[Heading 3] Addition
[Title] Now with them pinned, let's try adding. You can take every value (card) from one hand and 'merge' it into a card from the other hand. You knit things together point by point. Alice card with Bob card, Alice card with Bob card, and you end up with [ABABABBBB]. That would total 9 cards. You could imagine them mixing proportionally as well, something like [BAB,BAB,BAB]. You could even do them randomly [B,B,A,B,A,B,B,B,A]. The point here is that, in this system, every position mixes with every other position and the total accumulates. When pouring red and blue  glasses of water into a jug it feels like mixing, when adding three books onto a stack it feels like appending. You can accumulate with a heuristic or not, there is no right way, so it is considered instantaneous. But it’s your algorithm! Specify a heuristic and play it out over time if it serves your purpose.
[Title] Another way to think about addition is to take all of the first value and then append all of the second value, tip to tail. When we do this visually, we like to think of it as three apples followed by six oranges for a total of nine, but addition is still a merge, not an ordering. There is no rule in addition that allows you to specify that position 7 must be an orange. A merge will always destroy information about the original elements, and if you want that information going forward, you need to preserve it in some other way.
[Title] You could imagine doing all of these different accumulation heuristics continuously; the first way would be a blending of some sort, from start to end. And you haven’t created new ‘stuff’, you’ve just combined existing things. The second method would be taking the first portion and then adding the second portion to it. Generally, we don't think of doing addition continuously, so this doesn't normally matter. That said, they are different things, and there are many situations where it does matter. A recipe will often tell you how to add things while mixing, for example. And it always matters when things happen continuously, whether addition, multiplication, or even rewired boolean operations.
[Title] The most important point, though, is that you have now taken two collections, pinned them together, and then made them into a single collection. When you just pin them, you still have the two lines to work with, and you still know the value of the first, and the value of the second. When you decide on an operation and merge them, you end up with a single value (the length), and you have lost the original two values. The original information was folded into new information, the original dimensions were folded into a single dimension, and the original order is lost.
[Title] These are two steps, pinning (where input information is available), and merging (where results are known). Pinning allows you to work with separate things in a new space; merging creates a new space and information, and erases the older spaces. It's the equivalent of the left side of the equation and the right side of the equation. If we were collecting data on Alice and Bob's card hands, we could pin them together into a frame, and that frame could track and compare the cards. If we're collecting meta-information about the game, like the score or the total cards dealt, then we're less interested in the individual characteristics. In this second case, we want a merged set of elements as we care about ‘who won’ not ‘how they won’. Joining versus merging is a very key idea in mathematics, and we don't distinguish between these two enough.
[Heading 3] Multiplication
[Title] For multiplication, we can start with the same pinned elements. If addition is every portion of one element combining with a mapped equivalent portion on the other element, multiplication is every portion mapping with the whole other element. If this is done continuously all along a length, it looks like stretching. If it is done portion by portion, it looks like multiple additions. It is similar to addition, just it is not 1:1, it is 1:ALL. So 3*4=12 might look like one of:
[Title] The first two methods are more like repeated addition, 3+3+3+3, or 3 to the 'additive power' of 4 (powers essentially mean repeat in math). Even with the repeat-additive, we still have a question of the input, a single ‘unit’ can either be three things [a] or one thing of measure 3 [b]. Lastly it can be one thing, of measure 3, stretched to 4 times its length [c]. Three glasses of wine, three cases of wine, and three gallons of wine all have distinct measurement physics.
[Title] This is clearer when the values aren’t linear, where four rows of three are obviously additive, and a rectangular blend isn’t. A blending of A and B into a rectangle is a true multiplication because of the expansion. Three apples four times, vs. a 3-foot by 4-foot piece of cardboard. You can imagine these happening continuously: one representing four transactions with apples, and one stretching to fill an area. The result is the same, but these are very different operations and suggest different equations will be relevant around these two contexts.
[Title] Why does any of this matter? With people creating the equations and making these tiny interpretations, we get by with language papering over the cracks. We know we can have half an apple but not half a butter, and we choose operations based on that understood context. Machines could do this just as easily, but we only give them half the information. We don’t tell machines ‘twice as many’, or ‘twice as much’, we just say ‘2x’. This is mathematical computable information we are withholding, because our encoding system ignores it. Interpreting reality doesn't involve super complex math, but it does require making good guesses about the simple math that should apply in a given context. It also relies heavily on interpolation, and as absurd as it sounds, interpolating between anything, even addition and multiplication, isn't impossible if you model them specifically as continuous systems.
[Heading 3] Probability and Merging
[Title] Another way to think about multiplication is as a probability space, rather than a scaled line or area. Imagine a variation of poker where three of your cards are exposed, and two are hidden/unknown. In the Texas Hold'em version, the exposed cards are called the flop, and we'll call the other two the 'hidden' cards. So looking at each player's flop (exposed cards), you can calculate some kind of score that tells you the probability they will win. If you put Alice's score on the Y-axis, and Bob's score on the X-axis, that multiplication (into a rectangle) tells you the state of the table based on the known cards. The aspect ratio tells you who has a better chance of winning, and the total area tells you how strong the hands are. If both had 3-of-a-kind, we'd get a large area, and if they both had bad cards we'd get a small area. Bob's X-axis would be wider than Alice’s height if he had 3 aces to Alice’s 3 kings. So this is both a multiply ‘space’ and a multiplied result.
[Title] Let’s say Bob has a score of 20, and Alice 30. The space is the two scores pinned on each axis and the ability to merge them. The resulting area is NOT a rectangle, it’s just the area of the rectangle. If you displayed this area as a circle or a squiggly shape, it would still be an area of 600. Showing it as a rectangle is handy for us, and helps us interpret both spaces at once, but ‘area’ isn’t defined as a rectangle. The area space required one number, the axes required two. You can think of this space as the full algebraic equation A*B=C. The current instance might be 20*30=600, and the (20*30) part has information, and the (600) part has information.
[Title] Thinking about this space and the operation, it can be characterized as A*B=C. This is algebraically abstract, but that does not mean it can't be parameterized. If we know the maximum score is 50, and the minimum resolution our scoring system can provide is 1/2, we can refine the space to be two axes, each with 100 ticks, and the unit being two ticks. All scores start at 0, the minimum, so we only need one quadrant in this setup. Lastly, we need to be able to add values to each axis, and we need the multiply operation.
[Title] This parameterized space of 50*50 is in fact also made of numbers and operations, just like the input of 20*30 in the previous example. This means you can input 20*30=600 as we did (or more correctly, 40/2+60/2=1200/2 because of the resolution limit of two ticks), and you can use it as a space itself. The 20*30 space may be more useful when speculating further about the current round vs the game. There is no mechanical difference between a framing space and input into a space. And it goes further: each axis is also a space, where it has a min (0), a max (50), a resolution (2 ticks), and an operation (numerator/denominator). You can add any values to either axis. The result, and area of 2500, is also a space – and in fact the same space as the axis (min: 0, max: 2500, ticks: 4, and a division operation). Both the left and right-hand sides of the equation are spaces, and both are also values. You can input new values into any space if you choose, and it will just piggyback on the parent's range, resolution, and operations.
[Heading 3] Negative Numbers
[Title] Let's expand this space a little. So far we are only allowing positive numbers, so how about negative numbers? One way we can do this is to align the score axes' zero point with a 50% chance of winning. Remember, these aren’t ‘true probabilities’ that go from 0 to 1; they’re just the results of some scoring system. So now the values of Alice and Bob's scores can go from -25 to +25, still with two ticks resolution. These values will always be anchored at zero because they are one dimension; they just can now go from 0 to 25, or 0 to -25. And if they are positive, that hand wins at least half of the time.
[Title] This does bring up a very key piece of evidence in our quest to understand spaces. We can all understand 2*3=6, and it’s easy to place the subsequent rectangle on a graph, but what about -2*-3=6? The resulting area of 6 is the same, so the area should go in the same place as 2*3=6, right? And yet visually that makes no sense. We have a left-pointing line and a down-pointing line, and when we ‘connect’ them we get a rectangle in the top right? -2*3 and 2*-3 are just as confusing, as both give a -6 area (whatever that is), and go in either the top-left or bottom-right.’
[Title] There are a few things going on here, but the most important fact is that the result given as an area is not 2-dimensional in this sense. Area alone is one dimension in that it is specified using one number. The units are two-dimensional, but we’ve erased the connection with their information. We’re saying 6 feet squared is a total area of ‘6’, split however you want between orthogonal units. Again, we can visualize an area as squares, circles, or squiggly lines, but we do it with a single number. Speed is also two-dimensional if you look at the units, but it is specified as a single number (and thought of as a single value, of course, like any rate is). And if you dig, dig, dig, mass (kg/m³) and temperature (distance²/time²) are actually 4 axis units. Once a space with many degrees of freedom is folded though, it operates within its new, lower space’s degrees of freedom.
[Title] There is still the issue of the actual graph we have, though. We intuitively know where those positive and negative areas ‘should’ go. Even though the plot describes two spaces at once (A*B and area), this is allowed. We should look closely at what it is telling us.
[Title] If Alice’s exposed cards have a score of 20, and Bob’s are -10, we know the rectangle will naturally be in the top-left quadrant. These values are opposites combined, positive score and negative score, meaning Alice is favored, and Bob will probably lose. The top-left and bottom-right spaces have opposing forces, creating a strong comparison signal Just knowing the area is in one of these quadrants tells you the scores are a bit lopsided.
[Title] The other diagonal, bottom-right to top-left, tells you more about the overall strength of the hands. Top-right and both hands are statistically winners; bottom-left and both hands are statistically losers. These are very akin to the cross product (strength quadrants) and the dot product (comparison quadrants). Of course, each time you get both scores, the rectangles can tell you the full picture if you look at the shapes and sizes. It’s just that the quadrants alone hold information, before you measure anything. And in more complex spaces, this quadrant information is very helpful, as we’ll see next.
[Heading 3] Adding a Dimension
[Title] Back to poker. The three cards we see allow us to mechanically calculate some score, and we can plot that on an axis, -25 t0 25. What about the two cards we don’t see? We usually dismiss unknowns as noise or chance, but that’s why we’re terrible poker players. A good player has a lot of information about those cards. How the person bets, how confident they look, how they bet last year, if they scratched their butt, etc. I don’t know, as I don’t play poker (obviously), but if this information exists, it can be modeled. We can call it the ‘confidence’ a player has regarding their secret cards. First, let’s model this on two axes: X is score, Y is confidence. For simplicity, we’ll assume these are the same range and resolution, but of course, they don’t have to be.
[Title] Assuming confidence is positive-up, and score is positive-right, the top-right quadrant represents a very high chance of winning, with the bottom-left quadrant being very low. Top-left (high confidence, low score) is maybe bluffing, or a low-chance big hand. And bottom-right (low confidence, high score) is maybe a novice player, or a trap. The point being that the values along the backslash diagonal sort of cancel each other out, and the forward-slash values reinforce each other.
[Title] Once again, two quadrants represent a comparison of properties, and the other quadrants represent the combined scale of them. This makes it hard to compare two players though, as you would need a 4D graph, and the confusion that brings. We can fix this though, with one weird trick…
[Heading 3] The 2-Dimensional Axis
[Title] Imagine taking the vertical confidence axis and rotating it counter-clockwise until it is parallel with the score axis. It now points positive-left, and its unit ticks point left as well. Everything on this axis is a start point, and everything on the ‘score’ axis is an end point. We can now create a directed segment, from confidence to score, all in one dimension.
[Title] A highly confident player with a good score would have a very long segment, starting at say -20 and ending at 20. A length of 40, pointing in the positive direction! And opposite, a very sullen player with a bad-looking hand would have a very long segment as well, but pointing to the negative. The length of the segment tells you about the certainty of your prediction, and the direction tells you of the predicted outcome. With uncertainty, we would expect shorter lines, and sure enough: a confident player (positive confidence, so start far left) with a bad hand (negative score, so end far left) would likely be a shorter line, all to the left of zero. And a highly confident player with a bad score would be a shorter line to the right of zero. Our predictions in these cases come down to how confident and how bad the hand is; the arrow can still point in either direction even if it is all on one or the other side of zero.
[Title] This opens a few questions. First, is this even natural? Yes. Anything that is ordered, like a line, has two directions and two orientations. We are used to the two directions, positive and negative. The two orientations feel new, but we deal with it all the time on a vertical axis, arguing whether it should be positive-up or positive-down. We agree that both orientations exist, and the question is which should be discarded. In fact, there is no reason you can’t use both at once; it just requires two distinct units and two distinct values, one for each unit. We can think of one value as a start point and the other as an end point, then we get a directed segment.
[Title] Linguistically, this is like a dominant measure and its opposite, like sad<->happy vs angry<>happy. Arguably you haven’t fully specified an attribute until you’ve also specified its opposite.
[Title] Another question is why use positive-left instead of having two positive-right points. Of course you can do both, and we usually specify a range like, ‘from 10 to 20’. However, when a range is defined with a shared unit, you really just have separate two values and some guidelines. Additionally, the fact that anything ordered has two orientations is just ignored. As a bonus, a length in the new system gives you information about the magnitude of the differences as shown above. It fits perfectly with the natural duals you see everywhere in numbers, operations, groups, and symmetry. As you use these directed segments, there are many natural ‘true things’ that fall out of them. And no surprise, they map almost perfectly to something that already exists in math…
[Heading 3] Complex Numbers
[Title] If you call the positive-left unit the inverted (or imaginary) unit (‘i’) and the positive-right the rational (or real) unit (‘r’), then you can specify the directed segment as (3i+2), which has a length of 5 and points to the right. Note that the writing of it is order-independent; (2+3i) is the same number. It is handy to have the inverted portion first though, as that is the start point, and our regular numbers are just (0i+2), where the 0 cancels leaving 2.
[Title] All complex number math works for these directed segments, with the only difference being that rotation wrapping isn’t enforced. You can still have that modular behavior by embedding your values in a space of limited range that wraps when it overflows, but you must choose your frame to behave that way. As a bonus, boolean math is very natural on directed segments. And handy for us, we can pin two of these axes together to create a space to fully analyze the two poker hands we’ve been discussing.
[Heading 3] Complex 2D Spaces
[Title] We can now join these two axes of directed segments and try the same multiply operation on them. The score of the exposed cards is the same, going from -25 to +25, and they are represented by the end point of the segments. The start point represents the unknown cards, which is evaluated by the confidence of the player. When multiplying these together (without folding down), we get four quadrants. Each of these quadrants has specific meanings. The quadrants have the same meanings as before, but they are all present at once. So it tells you the total confidence of both players, the total hand strengths, as well as how their hand strength-to-confidence ratios compare to each other. These quadrants are familiar if you remember complex multiplication, which goes something like, in terms of ‘confidence & score’, cc+cs+sc+ss. Note this is normally written as (a+bi)(c+di) where ‘a’ and ‘b’ are Bob’s confidence and score, and ‘c’ and ‘d’ are Alice’s.
[Title] Note that the normal (ac-bd)+(ad+bc)i is changed to +(ad+bc)i+(ac+bd). The order changed because we are going with start->end, and the complex side is the start. This formula is a space with 4 degrees of freedom until you fold it, so we keep it expanded in the intuitive order. Remember that either order has the same meaning though, we establish the ‘start’ meaning with ‘i’, not order. As for the - turning to a plus, areas have an inverse, just like segments. Multiplying two positive i’s creates a positive inverted area. It becomes a negative number when converted from a start value to an end value during folding. You can see this by the fact that interpreting the 2i endpoint as a start point would make it -2 (the position is exactly the same, just the perspective changes). The notation here doesn’t fully express the chirality of the area, so we just leave it as is for now.
[Title] The two directed segments, when merged, will create a rectangle in each of these quadrants, and each rectangle will have an area and an aspect ratio. More importantly, all of this information will have a specific meaning. It has information about the relative and total strengths of the hands and confidences. Finally, this 4-quadrant result can be folded back into a single directed segment: (a+bi)(c+di) = (ac-bd) + (ad+bc)i, which will also have meaning. That is, all the confidence plus the scores merged, and the two ratios of confidence to scores merged. It is more about the final judgment, like ‘Bob is more likely to lose—the cards are close, but the confidence favors Alice.’
[Heading 3] Boolean Operations
[Title] There are three primitive operation categories we can use when merging values: Accumulation, Scaling, and Boolean operations. Just as we can map continuous magnitudes onto these axes, we can also apply discrete logic to them.
[Title] For two inputs of either true or false, there are 16 possible rules for the outputs. Common ones are AND (only true if both are true), OR (true if either are true), and NOT (only true if both are false). Less common ones are things like 'true no matter what', or even Implication (A → B), meaning ‘if A is true, then whatever B is, otherwise true’ (only A true + B false is false).
[Title] Now conveniently, we notice that 2*3=6 on a graph looks like AND, as well as OR and IDENTITY. But when we get to directed segments, AND is just merging the shown cards (score), and OR is the total score and ratios. IDENTITY is the whole thing. If we think of the score as true, and the confidence as false, we can do these operations, and many more, using only boolean operations. This is a whole rabbit hole I’ve written about elsewhere [1][3][4], but there is gold in there. You can find rules of symmetry, and a reasonable argument that contradiction in the actions of quadrants are what allows you to break out of one dimension into two [2].  If you extend Booleans with probabilities (imagine the score ended up being 90% of the signal), you can interpolate them, and the discrete nature of Booleans fades away (as do the Boolean categories themselves). It becomes more like a 16-pointed symmetric polyhedron with an operation at each point. Then you can interpolate between any two points to explore the interior space. There is a shape there if you study really hard into the precise meanings of these operations. Which I haven’t. All’s I got is , ‘trust me bro’.
[Title] Polyhedra aside, probabilistic Boolean interpolations are computationally simple, extremely expressive, and offer fairly easy-to-understand approximations by rounding up to surface points (which are the traditional Boolean operations above).
[Heading 3] Separation and Error
[Title] And the information separation allows us to embrace error as information; 2*3=7 tells us where the tension is, and gives us options to correct it. Normally we just reject the answer, but with this framework, we can also adjust the question. The question itself is data in an existing space, and it holds very different information than the result. We can even question the frame. As a result, we now have a computational framework to discover the correctness of our models through balancing evidence. And rather than rejecting error, we seek it out. We are not trying to prove a model with confirmation, but asking, “What is the perspective on this that is most likely to give error or surprise?”
[Heading 3] Conclusion
[Title] With (2*3) it was harder to see the difference between the pinned equations and the folded result. This is because the ‘area’ of 6 also really seems like it has sides of length 2 and 3, similar to the input. In reality ‘area’ has no such requirement. It is much easier to separate these two concepts with directed segments. The pinning and merging become four quadrants, and the folding of these results in a new directed segment. Both of these stages can be very important to encoding the measures and concepts we want to work with, so it is important to give them a formal home in math. Ultimately we understand things by holding a number of these spaces in our focus at the same time, and resolving the error into the most likely solution. The simplest example of this process is how we use two separate concepts at once, two merging input segments, and its area output. It really feels like these two perspectives are a single thing, and we even teach it that way. Why? Because this is how the great illusion works.
