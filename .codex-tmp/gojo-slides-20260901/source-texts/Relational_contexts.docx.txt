[normal] Relational contexts
[normal] All information is the result of comparing something measurable with a ‘ground’, and this ground value is always external to the object in question.
[normal] There are no yardsticks in the universe, so by necessity our meaning comes from the potential of everything being compared to everything else. This does not mean it’s just a 24/7 free for all though. We always select a non random frame of reference to encode information. This choice is as important as the value we are interested in, but it is so taken for granted that it is often invisible to us. The height and depth of an iceberg is referencing the water level, a color is a material’s ‘reaction’ to visible light, a width is the relation of two sides to space, and a big dog is referencing normal sized dogs. There is no color material attached to a tree, and no depth property inherent to an iceberg. It feels like a dog has a size all on its own, but without comparison it is just an arbitrary volume – and what is the size of volume? Is a sphere big?
[normal] All of our concepts, primitive, compound or metaphorical, spring from the physical world, but it is important to remember that the physical world contains no information. It doesn’t compare anything to anything else - it just is. It is numerators without denominators. You could argue that two points exist and the distance between them is information, but who’s two points? Those must be selected by something else, as the two points know nothing about each other. Then they must be compared with another two points someone chooses, otherwise it is just a length, and a length isn’t long, short, or 72. It’s just a length with no information other than it’s a length.
[normal] The physical world has energy, matter, space and time. Even if conceptually combining these (space to space is distance, matter and space is density etc) there is still no information. All states and changes just ‘are’. Is speed fast? If I’m thinking of two points, the only information you have is what I’ve already told you - that I’m thinking of two points. A value between them exists, but a) that is already an implicit comparison and b) it has no meaning and no way to be expressed.
[normal] It is like a numerator without a denominator, but that implies the top and bottom are both capable of being a number. In reality (and in math) a number itself requires a denominator (7 is 7/1), without it the numerator is just a ‘floating value’. Math rightly doesn’t commit to what numbers represent, but numbers are assumed to represent something – even if that something is  just an abstract transferable unit in a numbering system. What that something actually is will be expressed in the denominator. More concretely, in LanguageMath, ‘seven dogs’ would be 7/’a-dog’. A group of seven dogs might be 1/’a-pack-of-dogs’.  The numerator alone is just a floating value, without meaning until it is grounded with a denominator. A big dog is ‘big/a-dog’. With the denominator alone you still have a dog, but the numerator alone is a floating ‘big’ that actually tells you nothing about the size (a big ant or a big planet?). We don’t have a word for this floating value really, it’s like if I say ‘this much’ but you can’t see what I’m referring to. It’s a measurable thing that exists, but without context (a denominator) it could represent literally anything and therefore means nothing. Maybe it’s just an adjective or adverb, but without connotations. The universe then isn’t full of numbers, it’s full of floating values that only become ‘language numbers’ when minds enter the scene, with their categories and expectations.
[normal] What we do then, is always always have a ground — a frame of reference of some sort, that the property in question relates to. And this ground is always extrinsic to the object itself, because it has to be in order to generate a comparison. So a property isn’t an attribute of an object, it is the way an object relates or reacts to something else. Sometimes this dependence is clear, like color being a relation to visible light, other times it is based on context, like how balanced an object is depends on a fulcrum, and ultimately a gravity field. When comparing an object to itself, it will always be identical, which only tells us ‘same things are the same things’. So an external calibration must be used. A heavy dog’s perceptual weight is based on some average dog weight, and the numeric weight is based on some agreed unit of weight like a pound or kilogram which is based on our gravity field. If you dissect a dog, you won’t find a ‘weight property’ in there. A way to think of it is the grounded property (denominator) is on ‘a dog’, while the focused value is on ‘the dog’. This isn’t the whole story though, as objects have grounding beyond their physical form - a table of data uses the grounding of a table as ‘a place to spread out objects of interest’, while ‘a table’ filters out metaphorical groundings that aren’t relevant to a physical table. The ‘table concept’ not only includes all these groundings, it is made of all these groundings and nothing else.
[normal] The more common a ‘ground’ is, the more likely it will become a category or concept in our thoughts. Pinks, browns and oranges can be thought of as types of reds, but this would leave the ‘ground red’ doing a lot of work - light red, dark red, yellowy red etc. Sub colors feel natural for red, but we don’t do the same for blues or greens. Dark blue, cyan, navy blue, and baby blue are describable, but don’t get the full orange, pink and brown color status. The color monikers make them easier to use as a ground themselves, think ‘hot pink’ vs ‘hot baby blue’. The important thing is we choose these grounds by habituation, and we do the same with non-words (like dog sizes, and the water level relating to icebergs).
[normal] There is a certain math with grounds that language uses very flexibly. We can set the ground (say the water level) and then create a positive or negative expectation (height or depth) and calibrate them (a little high, very deep). We can also negate these expectations directionally (‘he was less than helpful’) and invert them (‘very unhelpful’). We are also very good at tagging a resolution along with our grounding (‘about a day's journey’ vs ‘three and a half hours at a brisk walk’). Our minds do this all invisibly with great speed, and our numbers extend the accuracy to the extremes. Ironically numbers do not allow flipping the positive expectations (‘un-‘) or expressing the resolution, unless, shameless plug, you use the number system I’ve been working on, which does :).
[normal] So we can imagine now, instead of a concept having some giant amount of properties with expected ranges, we have concepts that just interact with things they encounter in the world. There are a lot of interactions, but we only ground the common interactions we care about. This means all things have all properties, even if they aren’t commonly associated together. All things react in some way to visible light, so they all have ‘color’. We don’t need to attach a red color to an apple, we just expect it to appear red in visible light. And seeing it in the orange light of a sunset doesn’t surprise us, as we expect things to react differently to different light values. The ‘math’ of red is not stored in an apple definition, and a blue apple is still an apple, just some extrinsic property of it has been modified. This means that we only need to understand how things interact with visible light, and everything known and unknown automatically gets a color. And we can even apply color perception to things that don’t intrinsically interact with light at all, like a blue mood or white knight.
[normal] Properties being an interaction with something external is why metaphor is so easy in language. Legs on a house seems wrong, but in a flood zone you see it a lot, they are called legs, and nobody is confused. I can even say ‘a walking house’ or ‘an angry house’ and an expected image comes to mind. A house walking upside down with the legs coming out the roof however, just seems wrong. This is because legs are a common grounding we can apply to any concept (this idea has legs!), but the grounding has them underneath.
[normal] Everything we can imagine can have legs, but unless we have a context that focuses on them they aren’t mentally present. This is true even for things defined by legs, like a table or chair. ‘He served all six tables at once’, does not invoke a mental concept of legs. A table includes concepts like four legs, a flat surface, a place to gather, an eating location, a work surface, something you sit in front of, and so on. These concepts aren’t part of the table itself though, and the only one that needs to be valid is the one that fits the active context. If you suggest we ‘go sit at the table’, and then go to the ping pong table, that would require clarification. And when you do your homework on the floor, there is no need to borrow concepts and math from the table’s ‘place to do work’ modality. ‘Place to do work’ lives independent of tables, and it can be applied to literally anything, just like every other property. Again, this is because it is how an object relates to using it as a place to do work, not that it is inherent in the objects themselves.
[normal] This also explains how for every ‘requirement’ needed to define table, I can give a table example that doesn’t have that requirement. Made of wood? Aluminum. Four legs? How about three. Flat surface? Warped table. And floors have flat surfaces, are they tables? In the end, a concept is how we use an object for grounding, not the object itself. You can table a proposal while chairing a meeting, while standing on one foot in a park. The concept of the table is in the grounding, not in the wood.
[normal] So this seems like everything affects everything, and there are a lot of things, so how can we possibly compute all this? First, we reduce things to context, which is probably not more than five or six properties at once. Second, we expect things to react in similar ways. Heating anything up will make it warmer, usually, and too much will melt or burn it, usually. This allows broad reuse of default expectations, and then learning variations as we drill down (metals behave a little differently than average, and copper a little differently than that). Third, these properties also tend to affect each other in similar ways. A larger dog tends to be heavier, and ‘larger is heavier’ is the default expectation for everything. This doesn’t mean we die of shock seeing a helium balloon, just that registers as surprise, and surprise remembered is learning. This allows us to not just tweak our balloon expectation, but creates a new size/weight algorithm that we can use to associate to other things (say clouds) and use this new concept as a ground (“your idea will go over like a lead balloon”).
[normal] The fourth ‘compression trick’ we use takes advantage of the fact that patterns in one area are often similar to patterns in another. This lets us map these patterns separately from their embodiment, and reuse them as we see fit. Heat transfers through metal like the way panic moves through a crowd like the way a fart disperses in a room. You can be between jobs, between the sheets, or between a rock and a hard place. We separate out the pattern, in what I think of as ‘super prepositions’ - words that describe a reusable pattern, including things like jagged, corner, filtered or endless. Properties are always compared to some extrinsic ground, and the expected/allowable patterns and constraints that result are also extrinsic to the comparison. This results in an extensive toolbox we can mix and match to assess familiar and novel things.
[normal] When we see a brand new object, we are pretty well equipped with default expectations. These defaults don’t just give us better than random predictions, they give us a ‘ground’ to calibrate with. “An otter is like a wet cat but more playful”. The denominator is the familiar default cat with its many known relations to the world, and numerator is how otters relate to social environments (giving context to the denominator, which is now the quiet, aloof, independent, and certainly dry cat). This tells us a great deal about the unknown otter, as we have so many cat defaults, and playfulness was chosen as a major difference (which drags with it hints of energy levels, speed, safeness, curiosity). Notice how ‘playful like a puppy’ would be less informative, because it just compares one property, rather than relate to a ‘ground’ animal with selected differences.
[normal] CAD
[normal] “Sure, maybe”, I can hear you say, “but what does this all have to do with computer aided design?”. Well that’s the thing, CAD is at the intersection of human concepts and mathematical computability. So a good CAD model needs to leverage computation in the same way we do. So not only does this apply to CAD, CAD is the best tool for discovery here.
[normal] Normally a CAD model will take a conceptual object, say a trace in a PCB, and add properties to it, say path, endpoints, width, branches, thickness etc. Then elsewhere you model the edge of a table and you add other properties, say position, strength, straightness, material, texture, joint adhesion, scale, precision etc. In reality though, all things have all properties, because the properties are not inherent to the thing, they are a relation to something external. The edge of a table has a conductivity (it has a reaction to a voltage difference) just like a copper trace. And a trace has a texture, even though we may not care about it. If you try welding on a wooden vs metal table, you will care about electrical properties of tables, and you won’t have much trouble with it because you already understand charge flow to that level. So all properties will be applicable to all things, even if you don’t always focus on them.
[normal] Second, you apply metadata like working ranges, elastic ranges, and breakdown ranges. You aren’t specifying the actual current or frequency in a trace — that will be from its environment. You are just specifying how it behaves in the normal ranges of its expected environment, and when it fails. If you attach the trace to a pin, the pin will also have these properties and expected ranges, and here we can compute predictions. Perhaps there is too much current and we need to widen the trace. Or the voltage may sag and we add a capacitor. The point is we can ALWAYS map values to properties we care about at these junctions, because all things will react to and potentially transform the environment. You can imagine AI being able to insert a capacitor when it detects voltage sag by working to keep properties in expected ranges. The way we do it now is to show it capacitors, and expect it to derive ‘why’ they are there and extrapolate this to other situations. People would also fare poorly in this situation.
[normal] CAD is normally constraint driven, you specify two lengths are equal, or an intersection is at a right angle etc. Then with all these constraints, you end up with a fully defined object. However, you always need at least one grounded element, much like in the real world. Unlike the real world, these specifications are very concrete, and operate at the same low level of abstraction. We can’t say ‘this edge is smooth’ or ‘that hole is big’. It more this distance is mm, that line is parallel.
[normal] We also can’t take information from other levels in CAD. The software may say this pin is used as a high frequency clock signal, an antenna, a led signal, or a power source. That isn’t inherent in the chip or the PCB layout, only the software has this information. Yet this is crucial information that informs trace routing decisions!  Neither the input nor the output of PCB designs encode such things. It might be in a noisy environment with lots of vibration, or it might be in space. It might be a military future project, or a cheap toy. This is all important context all the way down, and it informs everything in the CAD model. Any attempts to ‘AI generate’ circuits are doomed to fail without this context, much like humans would be.
[normal] Another missing tool in CAD is permissive constraints rather than restrictive ones. A line that is allowed to change its path or have branches is more actionable than specifying a single possible path using restrictions. These are more like observations than constraints, like one edge is sharp on a knife. You don’t specify anything concrete, like which edge, how long it is, or if it’s curved. It is a grounding element, a concept, that applies to the knife you are designing. And with that information, and the fact that it is going to be gripped, we get new design observations, like ‘make sure the grip doesn’t easily slide into the sharp edge’. The whole thing can be designed with these different levels of concepts, and then instantiated repeatedly in custom ways, akin to parameterized concepts.
[normal] Really, any time you see numbers in CAD, that is an embodiment of a concept being specified, not the concept itself. If we list affordances and constraints, at all levels, many satisfactory embodiments can be created. If we require a game controller to ‘feel comfortable in the hands’ (how it relates to hands), then a kids version would look different than an adult one. How do we encode this? Same as above. We know how things relate to other things, we know various patterns (smoothness, tactile clicks, color contrasts), and we have AI+people. We do the math, much like our language and mental models do, and we get output we can iterate on with more conceptual guide rails.
[normal] The future is coming, and it no longer wants us to work at the numeric instance level. We need to get used to expressing things conceptually with grounded concepts, much like we do with each other. You won’t program the numeric motions of a robot any more than you specify the footfall positions when giving directions to a stranger.
[normal] This is all totally doable with our current technology, we just need a fresh look at formats, at math, at concepts, and the fresh worldview that comes with all that.
