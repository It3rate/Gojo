[normal] Universal Math
[normal] Math is discoverable. The math we use is a patchwork of discovered and invented tools that were brilliant and appropriate for their time. But these tools would not be discoverable. Many mathematical systems have been invented throughout history, and all are quite different from each other. And yet they are also consistent. They all reveal show how ‘measures’ relate to each other. There are common core truths that feel universal. On a good day they feel inevitable. This is an attempt to discover inevitable math, as revealed from first principles.
[normal] Having explored these ideas for many years, I have two main takeaways.
[normal] First, math should be much more about thinking than learning. A language must be learned because vocabulary is arbitrary. Reasoning about sounds isn’t helpful.Questioning syntax isn’t helpful. Unlike language, math can be derived by thinking hard about it. We currently accept math as we accept language, as a standard we inherited, where change is gradual, and questioning it feels silly. This is wrong. Math has always evolved to fit the needs and capabilities of societies. We need our math to be derived from first principles, and directed to fit our needs and capabilities. Small inconsistencies drown large truths at scale, and we are definitely reaching for scale. Math is the ultimate scalable tool.
[normal] Second, math isn’t about math — it's about everything. We use math to express ‘pure’ ideas because it can frame them in ways that are simple and precise enough to prove. These pure and simple restrictions allowed us to make great leaps in the past, using tools no greater than counting beads. The downside is that we now view math as simple and precise, and we view art, language, or emotion as organic and unrelated. Art is not the opposite of math, in fact it’s a very intentional way of juxtaposing elements for comparison in order to derive meaning. All things have to follow the basic principles of math because they are inevitable. This does not mean everything is precise and preordained. Rather it means true math is more comfortable with estimation than truth, just like everything else. Perhaps math is no longer the right word for this, but it is the word we have.
[normal] Our society’s goals are larger than ever – to probe depths of physics, to explore distant planets, and to harness synthetic intelligence, to figure ourselves out. Our piecemeal approach to math is becoming a major limiting factor in this progress.
[normal] Last and most important (and hopefully most obvious) — this is not the final word on universal math. I encourage you to not only doubt all of this, but to be arrogant enough to try your own version.
[normal] What is universal?
[normal] Math is anything that generates information by comparing differences or changes. We care about it because this is the only way to generate information. Universal math is the subset of these information generators that map to reality, but are explainable without reference to anything earthly, including ourselves. Existing math has parts that are universal, parts that are human centric, parts that are just for fun, and yes, even parts that are mistakes. Perhaps it is like art - sometimes it reflects reality, sometimes it is emotional, sometimes it’s ornamental, sometimes it just isn’t very well done. Regardless, it is all wonderful to have, and the world is better for it.
[normal] This exploration is an attempt to carve out a math where the axioms are based on simple physical truths we’ve learned over the centuries. The rule is this:
[normal] Universal Math must be derived from first principles using only ideas that are universal in nature, with no regard to our own environment and experiences.
[normal] It shouldn’t seem ‘conceptually human’ to the intellectual experience of an octopus, an immune system, an alien, or a future AI. So what do we know, to the best of our ability, to be always true?
[normal] Energy can not be created or destroyed
[normal] This requires that every operation needs to have an opposite operation to maintain the balance. Equations must balance. We can, and often do ignore the opposite, but we should always be aware that it happened. If we select three apples, we have reduced the unselected apples by three.
[normal] We can not create things to do math on. We need to either select them from existing things, or transform existing things into things we want to use. This is true even when thinking of math, somewhere in our brain changes must take place before we can imagine the number 5. All operations must start with selection, and at some level, the stuff of the selection already exists. Likewise, we can never truly delete things we no longer need, we can only transform them or go back to ignoring them.
[normal] More subtly, this tells us the total amount of ‘information’ in the universe is constant, in the physics sense. If some information is transformed to new information the original information is lost, unless it is preserved by something else recording it (which causes that second thing to lose its original information). Like with energy, we can concentrate it, structure it, and dissipate it, we just can’t create or destroy it as that would change the total. Keeping this in mind helps us to recognize what we are preserving and what we are throwing away as we change things. For example 3+2 is not the same as 2+3, in spite of both resulting in 5. We are used to discarding the equation and focusing on the result, but every portion of every statement contains information that is accounted for.
[normal] Equality is when we can’t tell the difference
[normal] Comparing things is the heart of math. Every object is defined as a collection of traits with their probable ranges and relations. The only things that can be measured are these traits, and these measurements are used to separate things into categories we care about. In order to have two things to measure, the two things must have some trait that is separable. It may be at a different location, or measured at a different time, but at least one trait being separable is the first requirement for making a comparison. If there isn’t this, then there is only one discernable object, so it isn’t possible to compare them. Math begins when we can tell the difference, and the difference is enough to be sure we have more than one thing.
[normal] Classifying things we can’t discern as equal does not imply they are the same thing. Once we take two separate measures, they may be too close to know which is greater, or they may just be within some tolerance. When we can’t tell the difference, or don’t care about the difference, then we have equality. This doesn’t mean they are the same, just that we consider them equal in measure. We might have two eggs, one slightly larger, but we don’t consider this to be 0.9 or 1.1 eggs because our defined valid egg sizes are a range. Someone can make 100 dots on a paper, but we can only count them correctly if they are all in different locations. If there are 100 dots one on top of the other and we need to count them correctly, we must devise testable ways of doing this. If successful, from then on we have a new ability to see past our previous resolution. Much of science is looking for ways to improve our resolution - to move past an ‘equality’ that we feel is separable. On the flip side, much of our understanding is based on ignoring differences we don’t care about.
[normal] Things diffuse from high concentrations to low
[normal] This is the second law of thermodynamics, where total entropy can never decrease. Significantly, there is such a thing as high and low, greater and less, before and after – and there is a natural direction for these. A lot of what we care about, in fact life itself, is organizing against local entropy. This means our natural ‘positive’ directions tend to be against entropy (build vs destroy, assemble vs disperse, grow vs wilt). Time itself has a direction, as well as cause and effect, which is important for continuous systems.
[normal] In one dimension, when something diffuses from a point it creates a flow moving in both directions. In two dimensions it creates a circle, and in three dimensions a sphere. This is easy to visualize when imagining a hot spot dissipating from the center of a rod, a sheet of metal, or a large room. This results in a two direction line (1D), a circle (2D) and a sphere (3D), which are the unrestricted default shapes for these dimensions. It is helpful to characterize all measurements of traits with these kinds of observations. For example time is one dimensional and only diffuses forward, so its default is a one directional line. Color can be thought of as three dimensional (RGB), and one can model its natural directions and diffusions in various helpful ways.
[normal] Everything is relative
[normal] There is no way to measure something without comparing it to something else. Imagine you exist alone floating in dark space. Would you have any idea how big you are? You would know your leg is larger than your arm, and your teeth are harder than your tongue, but you would have no idea how large you are overall.
[normal] There are no standard correct units of measure, so what we choose as a unit is a matter of convenience, and we can change that at will. Saying your arm is 3 times longer than your hand can just as correctly be stated as your hand is ⅓ as long as your arm. In fact it asserts it. Units are the primitive components of measure, and there is much to learn from exploring them exhaustively.
[normal] Nothing is infinite
[normal] There is not one thing in the universe that we know to be infinitely small, infinitely large, or infinitely anything. There never has been an infinite number or infinite decimal places used in a calculation, neither by a person or by a machine. You can not imagine infinity, but you can imagine that you can imagine infinity. This makes it a concept, and you can do math on concepts. But you can’t do math with infinity as a number.
[normal] Using infinity as a primitive feature of math was a mistake. It isn’t just an aesthetic concern. If the primitives we use for proving the ‘truth’ of math contain a falsehood, that doesn’t automatically make its conclusions false. An infinitely small point on an infinitely long line, or the infinite precision of real numbers in fact result in a much simpler system because we can ignore so many things. But this benefit is also the problem. The elevation of infinites has led us to incorrect expectations, and obscured useful truths.
[normal] If we accept the reality that everything we can measure in the universe is bounded on the high and low sides, our math becomes about segments, confidences and probabilities. We have to look at what happens at the ends - do things just end, do they transform, do they fade or lose meaning, do they move beyond our ability to measure them? Much of the work of math, engineering and science involves exploring these ends, quantifying them, and discovering ways of moving past them. There is much to be learned by acknowledging the world is finite, and calibrating our minds accordingly.
[normal] If there is infinite precision, we seek absolute truths. When this ‘truth’ proves illusive, we fall back to the lazy thinking that of a world where truth is beyond us, or where truth is whatever we claim it to be. In reality, we are only ever trying to bound the truth, and narrow that bounds through effort, perseverance, imagination, and critical thinking.
[normal] Math that is not centered on infinity is larger, richer, correct, and wide open for discovery and innovation.
[normal] Everything is changing, always
[normal] We can imagine something at absolute zero, where things become perfectly still, but in fact physics tells us that absolute zero is unattainable. Even at the ‘heat death of the universe’ things will not be still, its just energy would be so dispersed that it can’t do useful work. Remember energy can not be created or destroyed, and energy is always in motion at some level. It’s just that as it spreads out evenly there would no longer be gradients needed to do work.
[normal] This implies that math is always a dynamic system, even if we can snapshot things to get useful results. We may not always care about the dynamic aspects, but we should be aware that a momentary equation is never the whole truth. Continuous systems with feedback are more complex, but are also more natural, much like a movie is more complex and more natural that a photo. We live in a universe where everything that exists is a continuous feedback system. Our math should be built to naturally accommodate them.
[normal] Traits are the primitives, not objects
[normal] Discrete things are made up of definitions. A definition is a probable range of a continuous thing. You can have a fraction of a discrete thing (half an apple), and you can count discrete things. You can not have a fraction of a continuous thing (half a length is still a length), because it is just a measurement with no reference. The uncertainty with discrete objects is that their definitions are probable and dynamic, and therefore have ‘gray areas’. A small crab apple is probably an apple, but an apple bud is not. Any line that delineates an apple bud from an apple is an agreement on a definition. The same goes for apples themselves, and in fact any other object that exists.
[normal] Continuous measures alone can not be divided or counted - just as you can not have ‘half a length’, it doesn’t make sense to ask ‘how many lengths are in a room’. At least not without clarifying further what you mean by a length. As we zoom in on a continuous property, our ability to measure breaks down, or we reach the limit of a definition, or we cease to care about distinctions. Continuous things end up either being discrete objects (like grains of sand), or too small, too far, or too still to tell. Continuous does not mean infinite precision, it means continuous values at a certain level of precision.
[normal] Discrete objects are ultimately definitions, defined by agreed upon collections of continuous trait ranges. Continuous things are the measurable traits of these discrete objects.
[normal] Therefore objects are made up of the probabilities of their traits. Some traits are more foundational than others, some have more variation than others, and which ones we focus on is also a matter of context. But certainly traits of objects are the primitives, and objects themselves the useful bundlings of them.
[normal] Our math should reflect this. Counting is not the primitive operation, comparing magnitudes is. We can compare magnitudes without numbers — in fact as we’ll see, the results of these comparisons are where numbers come from.
[normal] Logic is universal
[normal] Logic is the study of true and false. True or false themselves are always the result of a comparison coupled with an expectation. Comparisons of magnitudes are the primitive operations of any mathematical system, and logic flows directly from these. If a second planet is larger than a first one (measured true), and a third planet is larger than the second (measured true), then the third planet is also larger than the first (logically true). This comes directly from the energies involved, and the logical consequences are inherent in all interactions.
[normal] There are many ways of doing logic, and our current rule set is not the final word on it. But it is self consistent, and does seem to stand up to many inherent truths we observe. There is a core of it that is derived directly from nature. As with all things, logic breaks down to chaos at the extremes, becoming overwhelmed by the sheer volume and variability of relations. Discrete becomes continuous, and continuous becomes chaotic. Logic is universal, but this does not make the universe infinitely logical.
[normal] Probability is universal
[normal] Probability is prediction of an unknown. It follows directly from logic, when systems scale beyond our ability to quantify and process the input. When magnitudes move beyond our ability to measure them we can only characterize the possible results and their likelihoods, and then design new ways to test these expectations. Logical systems can become less predictable at scale, but also random system can become more predictable in aggregate. Predicting a single coin flip from the millions of inputs controlling your hand would be impossible, but in aggregate one million coin flips would be very near 50% heads.
[normal] Much of science is trying to discover things beyond our resolution. If math doesn’t contain infinitely large values or infinitely precise measures, probability becomes an essential tool for bounding and describing values. It is important to characterize the ends of a measured segment - sometimes it is a simple termination, sometimes a transformation, sometimes a gradual change based on a definition. And very often it is something that passes our ability to measure, where we are unable to even sense a pattern. At this point we must use probability to estimate what may lie beyond. Probability allows us to quantify what we know, what we don’t know, and what is unknowable. It can create predictability from uncertainly, much like the chaos of the quantum level can still create the certainty of a rock.
[normal] Symmetry is universal
[normal] Symmetry often implies bilateral symmetry, where one side mirrors the other. It isn’t just in life forms, but in any mirroring operation (negation), so it is a very fundamental operation in math as well. There is also ‘duality’ that seems to appear everywhere in math, with every operation having some sort of reciprocal partner operation (because energy can not be created or destroyed). Selecting something also ‘doesn’t select’ everything else. If a magnitude is 3 times larger than a unit, the ratio is ⅓ when making the larger object the unit. These ideas are found everywhere in higher geometry, logic and algebra, and may be as universal as ‘every action has an equal and opposite reaction’.
[normal] Symmetry is more general than reflection or reciprocals though. In 2D geometry there is also translation (non flipped motion) and rotation, as well as new symmetries built from combinations of these. These are most easily seen in ornamentation patterns used across all cultures since ancient times. They also play a large role in Group Theory. When there is a pattern, it implies there is some type of symmetry. Symmetry is when something held invariant while an operation unfolds. Ultimately to understand change we must have something held constant for reference, much like we need a constant unit to measure properties.
[normal] Symmetry rules allow for generating patterns, and these same rules can be used to predict patterns. They are also a way of encoding these ‘pattern machines’. Much like numeric equations, we can think of the rules as a recipe. A recipe can generate a cake, can be derived from a cake, or can just be the encoding of a cake. The benefit of viewing rules as an encoding is code can be inspected, manipulated, and interactive. With this view, symmetry becomes a full computation system, similar to numeric equations, but more directly mapped to reality by its constraints (and therefore more complete).
[normal] This broader idea of symmetry is the foundational element of operations, transformation, and indeed all predictable change. It is universal in one sense due to conservation of mass, and in a more abstract sense because where there is no symmetry there can be no information.
[normal] Binary is universal
[normal] Doubling with one extra piece of info. Naturally twice as long or folded in two.
[normal] The most basic operation when comparing things is to be able to tell one from the other. When measuring something, the measurement system breaks down when we can no longer separate readings. If there are a hundred dots on a piece of paper they can be counted, but if they are all made in the same spot counting becomes potentially impossible.
[normal] Every time we can separate one piece of information into two we double the information in that piece. Therefore the smallest piece of information in a system can be represented by a bit. Information theory shows us that things like compression, transmission, and existing information are also very important, but information is most naturally represented in bits.
[normal] This does not mean that decimal doesn’t work, or we can’t roll up bits into hexadecimal, just that the most natural system for measuring the world is binary. Other systems can work, but they are more likely to obfuscate truths, and make natural things seem unnatural.
[normal] Complexity is inherent in simple things
[normal] Simple and straightforward rules can quickly turn into patterns, complexity, and even randomness when iterated. Emergent behavior can be completely uniform or impossible to predict. This implies the future doesn’t have to be knowable, calculations aren’t always calculable, and simple systems can break down at scale.
[normal] Take numbers, that can always add one to get the next one. It seems obviously infinite. But if you take a million to the power of a million a million times, you end up with a number so big that you can’t generate the next one. You can’t add numbers of that size, or take a random number between two of them. It would be hard to define anything about a number that still can be shown to hold at that scale, because it is so many times larger than all the plank lengths in the visible universe.
[normal] So while we can make rules and usefully use these to make predictions, we need to remember that these systems can also go their own way to defy perfect understanding. The world can be neat and orderly, and also chaotic and messy. That is why it is so interesting.
[normal] Dimensions are traits
[normal] Dimensions are most easily imagined as spatial, 1D, 2D or 3D. However, anything measurable is a dimension, and every dimension is a unique and measurable trait. A ten dimensional object isn’t some impossible to imagine geometric abstraction, it’s simply an object where ten traits have been considered.
[normal] It is possible to define any object or concept using a collection of valid ranges of traits, and information on how those ranges relate to each other. There are obviously a lot of traits to choose from in the world, but we only care about a much smaller subset of them, and even then we only consider a few at a time depending on context and metaphor. In fact, context could be defined as the traits we are currently focusing on, and metaphor defined as transferring traits from one object to another.
[normal] Traits are always continuous at their discernible resolution, definitions are based on probability ranges of traits, and instances are based on the measured value of traits. It is possible to sample the traits of a limited number of instances to estimate the ranges and relations of traits for a category. More samples will refine the understanding of the category, but with this type of encoding good results can come from many fewer samples than traditional machine learning. Additionally, existing machine learning networks can be leveraged to measure and weight traits based on their utility to people. There is no need to hand craft these networks, but because they are interpretable, they are able to be debugged and ‘taught’ without large scale retraining with new data.
[normal] What is the Human Part?
[normal] Some things are, to the best of our knowledge, universal - but that isn’t the whole story. We exist in a very local environment in this universe, and our intelligence is well adapted to it. Specifically we have gravity, ten fingers, a body that runs on food and desire, and a very strong social dynamic. The thing that really sets us apart from the impartial universe of atoms and galaxies is we are surrounded by life. Life is anti-entropy, an inverting of the natural order of energy itself. It requires an unusual layer over the pure math of physics, with its own special rules. Our math reflects both layers, the universal layer and the human layer, and they are hard to tease apart.
[normal] Having human perspective reflected in our math is not a mistake. It’s a continuously evolving way of adapting to the world and times we live in. The reason to separate it out from universal math is not to remove it. Rather it’s to better understand what is local to our experience and what is universal.
[normal] Base ten
[normal] We have ten fingers, so we mostly use base ten numbers. Babylonian calculation was based on tables of ratios, so they used a very divisible base 60 (sexagesimal). Mayans used base 20. But by and large we seem to prefer base 10 for everyday use, and certainly it is the dominant base in use today. Computers however use base two (binary), so it could be argued that we use binary for calculating, and translate to decimal for display. The Romans used the abacus for calculating, and translated to Roman numerals for display. Base 10 is a very human way of interpreting quantities.
[normal] Squares, angles and gravity
[normal] We consider squares and rectangles the core geometric shapes in our lives, more so than triangles or circles. This is because we live in a gravity field on a planet large enough that it seems flat. Anything that needs to stand up needs to be perpendicular to the surface, so the rectangle becomes a very natural shape. 90 degree angles are certainly very important - the most a pair of lines can diverge from each other’s direction is 90 degrees. But four-sided figures require a lot of rules to define, and a lot of rules to specify how they change as lengths and positions change. Compare this with changing a circle’s radius, or moving a vertex of a triangle. Ancient geometry is filled with triangles and circles, as they worked up from minimum axioms, but it is good to keep in mind our bias to squares. For example, a length and a width naturally forms a triangle, not a rectangle.
[normal] Because of gravity we are also very aware of angles. This fits quite well with our perceptions, and we have extensive branches of mathematics presented in terms of angles. Angles ultimately represent ratios of traits, height to width in geometry, but non geometric traits can be compared and related in the same way. The phase angle of electricity uses trigonometry adapted from geometry, but it is really just describing relations. The electricity won’t ‘fall down’ like a leaning building would, but our math evolved in gravity, and we use the tools we have.
[normal] Poor non-linear intuition
[normal] Most aspects of our lives take place in a fairly linear way. Time ticks on, the world seems flat, dropped things don’t reach crazy speeds. This is sensible. If something grows or shrinks exponentially for too long it would replace everything else or disappear completely. Given that we exist, normal things aren’t going to have long term exponential growth. The result of living in this environment is that our intuition for exponential things is pretty weak. Perhaps our emotions are exponential, things like panic, rage and love. Maybe it’s how we deal with those rare instances of compounding stakes, not overreaction as much as exponential reaction. We could do worse than think of counting as a stroll in the woods and factorials as moments of blinding passion!
[normal] There are many other areas of math that our intuition can easily mislead us. Things like probability, very small or large scales, emergent behavior, or even paradoxes and optical illusions. All are based on tricking our intuitions, and yet we do adapt. There are many things that seem intuitive now but were considered very strange at the time of discovery. And many more we’ve gone back and forth on. Here is a very human pattern - imagine an idea that seems totally counter intuitive, normalize it to the point we can’t imagine things a different way, and then remake the world until it also fits the idea. Sounds crazy, but what are currencies, national borders, video games or sports teams? They are ideas that have been made into real things with real consequences. We do the same in math, where some things are real and some are constructs, and we can’t tell which is which.
[normal] It is important that we use our intuition as it usually serves us well, but we shouldn’t encode it in the low levels of our tools. And we should get used to right answers derived from first principles not always ‘feeling right’. At least not at first.
[normal] A sense of the divine
[normal] We can sense divinity, the all seeing, the all knowing, and it shapes how we see the world. This is one of our superpowers in fact - imagination, creativity and perseverance are directly linked to this faith in the unknown. This obviously helps us in many ways, but it does leave us with the impression that there are perfect answers to every question if we look hard enough. Our math, with its ‘infinite’ precision, and infinite expanses reinforces this.
[normal] In reality we are only ever narrowing the bounds of truth, not finding perfect answers. Recognizing this makes the world much more understandable, and our goals much more tractable. There are things we can confidently say are true because they are well within the bounds of what we know to be true. Other things are gray areas, and others are confidently false. Reality is neither ‘perfectly describable’ nor ‘a pure construct of the mind’. This feels right when paired with our everyday experiences, yet somehow we feel that things beyond our understanding should have clear and exact answers. We feel that, at least the abstraction of math, that the world can be precise and complete. It can’t be though, because it too exists in universe that will always be fuzzy at the edges. A math focused on bounding and quantifying truths would feel less precise, but it would be able to usefully inspect and qualify many aspects of reality that are currently too ‘squishy’ for algorithms. Honesty over precision is always a good trade.
[normal] We have a sense of limitlessness, but it’s honest methodology and bounded calculation that has allowed us to understand things so far beyond our daily precision.
[normal] We have history, so does our math
[normal] Math was created by the first city states, or perhaps it caused them. It was used to manage these states, tracking land, trade, taxation and astronomy. This started with recording simple arithmetic information, but scaling required a much deeper understanding of the relations between measurements. This led to ‘recipes’ that worked out how quantities would transform under various conditions.
[normal] The discoveries in math were first and foremost about ways to make difficult calculations tractable. What started as a tally system became a number system, what started as counting became addition and then multiplication. Every time we figured out a better way to calculate (tables to abacus to slide rule to computer) it profoundly affected both our number systems and our algorithms.
[normal] The Sumerians and Babylonians used tables of columns to calculate, ratios were very central to these calculations, and their number system was based on the very divisible 60. Later the Greeks and Chinese used multiplication tables, and moved to base 10. The Romans extensively used the abacus, and their numbering system reflects how the results are encoded – 8 (IIX) is 2 less than 10, 12 (XII) is two more than 10 — as represented on an abacus. Note that all of these systems used zeros in calculations (an abacus has the concept of zero), they just didn’t record the results with a positional zero.
[normal] Hindu/Arabic numbers were base 10 positional systems, which is where our current decimal system comes from. They also used the abacus, but their math was getting very symbolic by this time (~900AD) which required the more algorithmic decimal number system. By the 1600’s logarithmic tables and soon after the slide rule were invented, making multiplication almost as easy as addition. This allowed (or was caused by) great advances in math, particularly the invention of calculus, and was the basis for a lot of the math that we use today. Math at this point became as much about symbols and proofs as it was about numbers This led to a great proliferation of mathematical symbols, many still in use today. This also powered (and was powered by) the industrial revolution, which lasted right up to the computer age we’re in now.
[normal] The computer age changed a lot of long held truths about math. Calculation is now the easy part — so much so that it has outpaced our ability to reason about it. We still use many of the industrial revolution’s mathematical systems, but it is all encoded in much looser and more scalable programming systems. Under the semi-human interface, programming quietly uses binary numbers, fakes infinite values, and expands out obfuscated formulas meant for ease of calculation. There is a great friction in programming, between our inherited system of math and a more natural one that fits our current reality. Slowly, reality is winning.
[normal] Basic operations
[normal] Our basic math operations are addition and multiplication (and their inverses, subtraction and division). We see the world as discrete objects, and we care the most about outcomes. Because of this, we don’t really differentiate between operations if they give the same results. Repeated addition (3+3+3=9) and scaling (3*3=9) are thought of as interchangeable. The same goes repeated multiplication (4*4*4=64) and repeated scaling (4³=64).
[normal] We also assume the left side of an equations has already has been selected, that math is a ‘post selection’ process. Though we have both selection and repetition incorporated into some algorithms (for example the Fibonacci sequence or Taylor series), it isn’t considered a distinct step most of the time. This is partly due to our focus on goals over processes, where the path we take is usually less consequential than where we end up. A long chain of events are often substituted by values like PI, or functions like sine and cosine. This is justifiable, as there is no need to redo that work on every usage, but it has caused us to ignore steps that bring clarity to the process. And has made us blind to the fundamental mechanics of these transformations.
[normal] Life is anti-entropy
[normal] We know that the natural direction of the universe diffuses towards more space and less heat. With life, when something grows it accumulates more energy and organization, not less. This is so ingrained in us that we probably have many of the natural directions backwards. Higher numbers are more naturally cooler, shorter, less dense and less organized. Or perhaps it’s just that the natural direction of the number line is decreasing - more like 10, 9, 8 than 1, 2, 3. In any case, it is good to keep in mind that there are natural directions for the universe, and natural ones for life, and they tend to be opposite.
[normal] Reality compression
[normal] Reality is vast, much vaster than what our brains could ever take in all at once. This means we need to use filters and compression. The optic nerve bandwidth is around one megabyte per second, which in computer terms is like a poor internet connection. All of what we see is imagined by our brains based on a relatively small amount of data with a large amount of processing. Sometimes by extrapolating what’s relevant, but mostly by ignoring what isn’t.
[normal] The second physical issue we have with seeing, is that interpreting visual information can take up to 200 ms. That may sound fast, but it means by the time we ‘see’ a fastball leave a pitcher’s hand, it’s halfway to home plate. The way our brain gets around this lag is by making predictions, and showing us the predictions instead of the truth. It will then course-correct when wrong, and convince us the error never happened. Words take even longer to process (we only process 2 or 3 per second), but given they usually arrive at that speed we don’t notice.
[normal] A third thing to note (and there are many more) is memory is *very* limited, even short term memory. One of the ways we deal with this is we carry around context rather than every fact in a running situation. Context is focusing on traits that are relevant, and discarding others. A book for example has many interesting things you could know about it - who wrote it, what is it about, was it good... But if it is being used to prop up a short leg on a stool, we only care about how thick it is. This exponentially reduces the information that needs to be stored and retrieved in any given situation. We do this in math as well, discarding the setup and intermediate steps to focus on the final result. Formulas also ignore every detail that isn’t important for the result. Ohms law doesn’t talk about wire color or the time of day. Math is possible because, like physics, the results are the same no matter where use do it. This is also what makes a portable context possible - if apples turned into an watermelons while crossing a room, context would be meaningless.
[normal] This isn’t to say that what we see doesn’t reflect reality. What we see is a very good and very efficient interpretation of the world, even if it can sometimes be fooled. It’s just that our minds aren’t perfectly reliable, and in fact nothing can be. This is why we need to attack problems from multiple creative angles, with honesty, diligence and humility. It is very hard for a mind that only sees expectations to see beyond its expectations. Even on the rare occasions when we tell it to.
[normal] Understanding through metaphor
[normal] Almost all our understanding is done through metaphors, built on a small number of primitives, which are often 2D spatial primitives. There are hints in language, with phrases like ‘in the mood’, ‘under a spell’, ‘across time’ or ‘between jobs’. Interestingly, the spatial models used are somewhat culturally dependent.
[normal] We seem to mentally visualize math in the same way. Certainly a mental number line is very familiar. Simple geometry in motion is so easy to conceptualize it feels non-metaphorical. When we visualize things like shapes, graphs, electric fields, or laws of motion, we may actually use shapes that are fairly close to our primitive mental building blocks. The same may be true of other artifacts we create, like written glyphs, ornamentation patterns, simple cartoons, and animation.
[normal] Importantly, we also build our mathematical systems through metaphors, where we build complex concepts from simple primitives, and apply familiar patterns to novel situations. Group theory explores the pure patterns of relations, removed even from math itself - like the mechanics of metaphors stripped of the concepts.
[normal] We simplify
[normal] Our brain is a prediction engine. It continually predicts the momentary future, the short term future, and the longer term future. It also continually evaluate these predictions, updating not only the estimates, but also the model that does the estimating. In other words ‘learning’. Because of this, reading about things that are beyond our ability to see directly comes naturally to us. This includes abstract and imaginary things. The idea that we are ‘guessing’ is more obvious when we look for electrons or exoplanets, but in fact we predict and evaluate everything, from simple snack plans to the secret thoughts of friends.
[normal] This makes our minds very sensitive to change. Understanding the mechanics of change detection is crucial to understanding cognition. The first step is to model a ‘start’ situation, focusing on states elements that are relevant. Because models of reality must inherently be dynamic, they will involve prediction. This is model is then (somehow) stored. As the model subsequently transforms, the new states (or at least their changes) are also recorded. Lastly the two models are compared, looking especially for differences and unexpected values, and these differences are evaluated.
[normal] Differences evolve continuously over time, so models must be more that the simple difference between two states. A model that captures the differences in two photos wouldn’t help much in understanding a full movie. In order to capture such a long sequence of changes, the changes themselves need to be modeled. The simplest encoding of motion (change) that already exists is motion itself. 1D motion and force along a line is the simplest, and 2D allows for forces that don’t line up exactly. These are both abstractions of the more complex 3D world we live in, which means they are inherently a simplified abstraction. Looking at language, we seem to use this simplified physics to express change. Looking beyond language, 2D representation seems to be a fundamental encoding tool.
[normal] We create 2D representations like blobs, points and paths in order simplify them, and we move them around to simplify change over time. We use 2D artifacts everywhere - graphs, maps, photos, blueprints… These are all complex fluid situations reduced to single 2D objects that can, and they are easy to evaluate, adjust, predict and update. We certainly do this in math as well, it could even be argued that math is just a formal numeric encoding of these kinds of reductions.
[normal] The math we understand will ultimately be an abstraction and a simplification. Our heads are only so big, and the world happens outside them. The question, “is there a universal math?”, is perhaps better stated as, “are there a universal abstractions?”. Being able to make accurate predictions about novel high level traits strongly suggests universal rules. So yes. We abstract things, and we probably do so in ways that encode their natures.
[normal] We magnify differences
[normal] Differences are often very subtle. Our minds will magnify and even misrepresent differences in order to see changes more clearly. This is generally a good strategy, as it gives us much better perception without the need for more accurate senses or more information. We can see this in early stages of vision, where edges and luminosity differences are prioritized, and flat unchanging areas are discarded. We also see it in our categories, where cats and dogs are thought of as opposites in spite of both being small domesticated furry mammals. This can sometimes amplify error of course, making us overconfident in results that may not even be there. Generally though, zooming in on differences is a great way to increase resolution and filter noise, as long as we are aware of the caveats.
[normal] We magnify similarities
[normal] While we are very attuned to subtle differences, overall we expect similar things to be similar. This is a good bet. Our ability to categorize depends on most traits remaining similar across instances. Because traits are all evaluated to be within ranges, some traits in a category can stretch, as long as most others hold. For example we know apples are red, and that they can also be green or yellow - as long as they still have most other ‘apple’ characteristics. A blue apple is too big a stretch on a property that is too fundamental. We’d predict a blue apple to be a toy, or perhaps a painted apple.
[normal] In the common case, we only need a few measured traits to make a good prediction – as long as these traits were fundamental to the object definition, the object was common, and the values were expected. We magnify other similarities until they disappear into an average. Our expected size of a ‘small dog’ is fairly precise, even though everyone might have a slightly different standard. But it’s difficult to think of a dog being slightly larger than a small dog. We’ve merged these size similarities, so we find it easier to key on a new trait, like the ‘small noisy dog’.
[normal] Shared trait ranges that form a category allow us to not only categorize new objects, but allow us to make reasonable guesses about traits we haven’t verified. A child might guess a pear is juicy like an apple rather soft like a banana - many of its other traits are apple-ish. Of course we are sometimes wrong about these guesses, but that type of error helps us update the categories ranges and expectations, or even start new categories.
[normal] We expect similar things to be similar in math as well. Mathematical domains are roughly organized into categories, and these categories tend to investigate common phenomena. Sometimes math is duplicated in different categories with slightly different syntax and calculations, such as the various ways matrices are described across domains. More fundamentally, we expect the same results when expressing the same equation in different ways, and we are very aware when changes matter (3/2 vs 2/3) and when they don't (2+3 vs 3+2). Our intuition tells us when new situations feel like math we’ve used before, and often guides us into finding the right solution to novel problems. These strategies are largely left unexplored in formal math, but the field would benefit greatly from a better understanding and harnessing this type of predictive reasoning.
[normal] We are made of desire
[normal] While we aren’t that great at computationally simple problems like addition and multiplication, our minds are very well adapted to much more complex ‘organic’ concepts like language, design or music. The most ‘difficult-yet-easy’ skill we posses is the ability to understand and navigate human social systems. Some argue that our over-sized brains evolved to capture the outsized rewards of winning in this social contest. Inferring someone’s state of mind from a story seems simple to us, but the mechanics involved are mind boggling. We know where information comes from, how reliable it is, who knows about it, and who knows who knows, and who might be hiding what, and why. We know who wants what, and how badly, the imagined history of micro-pacts and payments over years, the changing of moods, and all the mind games that people use to try and influence each other’s perceptions in the pursuit of advantage. It makes 3D chess look like tic-tac-toe.
[normal] The underpinning of all social relations comes down to breeding and resources. And it isn’t just competition for these, although it is obviously a large part of it. We have evolved to be very weak alone, and almost unstoppable together. At a certain level we understand the social group is the machine, and we are the parts. Every part looks to its own interest to maximize its potential, but clearly the fate of an average individual is more tied to the fate of the group than their ranking within it. And without a group, the individual’s fate is certain.
[normal] Human behavior is calculation without certainty. Some uncertainty comes from randomness, some from the unpredictable environment, and some from secretly being a few steps ahead. Knowing exactly what people actually desire would be extremely helpful step in predicting behavior. Sometimes we wear our desires on our sleeves, sometimes we hide and misdirect, and most often we’re oblivious ourselves. Keeping a desire secret can be a great advantage, and there is no better way to keep a secret than to keep it from yourself.
[normal] While most math is not set up for ‘emotional calculation’, there are many fields that are. Economics, psychology, game theory, and political studies to name a few. They have had pretty dismal track records so far - mostly because these are fields that are actually orders of magnitudes more complex than they seem to be. But it’s also because our math is historically based on precision and perfect information, and things like probability, causation and interpretation are kind of bolted on the side. As we get closer to understanding ourselves, we will be better served by a system of calculation that can describe our mental models, social motivations and emotional states. This will be amazing, though obviously not universal.
[normal] We see objects as primitives, not traits
[normal] We identify everything using concepts, and concepts are easiest to understand when presented as discrete things. Common categories, things like oranges, chairs or horses, are so normal that we see them as building blocks of reality. When looking closer we can see that they are categorized using their traits. Tangerines, sofas and mules feel nearby, or perhaps special cases. The reality is that they (mostly) fit into common ranges of traits that we use to describe a useful concept area, not a universal truth.
[normal] When combining object categories with our feeling that truth is infinitely precise, one can get a feeling that everything fits precisely into some divine taxonomy. This structure actually works fairly well most of the time, but it always unravels with deeper understanding. Not every object category has exact bounds, there are always edge cases, and exceptions are everywhere. This doesn’t make categories wrong or meaningless though. Clearly at a basic level reality maps pretty well to our mental perceptions of it - it isn’t our lack of imagination the prevents us from walking through walls.
[normal] The main confusion is that we imagine the world as exact pre-existing discrete concepts, rather than just the ranges of properties in common objects that we find useful. A chair with a leg missing is still a chair, but a chair cut in half is half a chair. The half chair has fundamental traits that are out-of-range — enough that is no longer fits into our definition of a chair. If someone successfully sat on the half chair, we’d probably still call it a chair.
[normal] We are inclined to see counting 1,2,3, as the basis of all the math that comes after, because those are the first discrete objects we encounter. Things like ratios feel like they are built from these simple numbers. This is incorrect, as numbers are actually quite complex and highly specified objects. Counting tree requires a good definition of a tree, and even then they don’t have a natural order.
[normal] Looking at an object’s traits as the real primitives, things become much more natural. We can easily compare two durations or two temperatures without resorting to numbers. We can also create definitions using such comparisons - the ‘hottest part of a day’ is really just some temperature range paired with some duration range. In fact we can derive numbers themselves from the resulting ratios of these comparisons. One length twice as long as another gives a ratio of 2.
[normal] Ironically the earliest math system of the Sumerians and Babylonians were based quite strongly in ratios. By Greek times non-numeric geometry was still foundational. The simplicity of calculating with discrete values, and the efficiency of symbolic calculation moved us further towards numbers. Eventually even geometry and ratios themselves were represented numerically. Numbers, unlike lines and ratios, could be extended to ‘infinity’, or at least imagined to be capable of it. No doubt this made sense given the needs and capabilities of the time. But times have changed, and it may be time reexamine the power relations have over entities.
[normal] Math is for anticipating outcomes with understanding. The formula for understanding has the same structure of a math formula — ‘inspect, select, predict, try, evaluate, repeat’. Ultimately math is the encoding of learning.
[normal] Changes in math
[normal] Computation is no longer the bottleneck in math. This is of course mostly due to computers and improvements in the algorithms that run them. This new form of computation is much more than a high powered slide rule for calculation. It incorporates decision making and interactivity, with the ability to hide complexity in abstract interfaces.
[normal] Our ability to scale ideas is drastically better than ever before. This is true in all aspects of society, not just engineering and software. Even our ability to scale is rapidly scaling.
[normal] Math historically has been limited in scale and complexity due to the limitations on solving calculations and parsing logic. Because of this, it was (correctly) designed to explore and understand small areas, and to try and extrapolate ideas from rules. The syntax reflects this brevity, but this is a brevity that modern computation are in no way bound to. There are good reason we don’t use mathematical syntax for programming languages.
[normal] Complex simulations are now routine, with ever increasing levels of accuracy. While models will always have limitations, they can be very useful, when these limitations are understood. In the past we would simulate ideas in our minds, and verify with experiment, calculation, and occasionally catastrophic failure. We now have machines to dynamically test whole ranges of ideas, and in many cases even generate optimal solutions.
[normal] The latest directions in math rely heavily on machine ‘intelligence’ in some form. It is early days, but certainly computation is being pushed into areas that were not historically thought of as mathematical. This is broadening our concept of ‘numerical’, into something much more organic and informative.
[normal] The math we inherited has been well encoded in computer languages and libraries. It has become normal for people to use high level math without needing a deep understanding of it. One can understand the utility of sines and arctangents without a deep understanding of the Taylor series. This has allowed many more of us to leverage math as a tool, and is certainly overall a good thing. But it has moved math from a  ‘thinking’ exercise to a ‘learning’ one. Learning math should not be approached like learning vocabulary - there is no source of truth, so doubt is acceptable. Essential even.
[normal] Changes in science and engineering
[normal] Computation is no longer the bottleneck in engineering. Changes in math have always been in lockstep with changes in engineering, as well as changes in society. It is hard to tease cause from effect here, but certainly the ability to do super-human calculation has touches everything.
[normal] There has been an explosion of sub fields in math, science and engineering. Specialization is near universal. The one constant is that all fields are investigating relations of magnitudes (real or imagined) and the concepts (real or imagined) they define. What is missing is a universal encoding of these processes. If current processes were computer code (and many are), they would be thousands of functions, many vaguely similar, some contradictory, all without standard structure or convention. Knowing what we know about scaling complex systems, it seems this could be improved.
[normal] Our levels of precision are many orders of magnitude better than even 100 years ago. With this precision comes a much better understanding of how things function, and a much better ability to reproduce results. When interacting with the real world, our precision is often the bottleneck. This makes old ideas like significant figures very relevant. Unfortunately our computation system is inherently ‘use more precision than you need and don’t worry about it’. This is the luxury of excess compute power, but it does obfuscate what is really going on. The edges of precision are where discovery happens, we would do well to treat that area more formally. This is happens to some extend in science and engineering already, we should double down on this in math and computers.
[normal] Changes in society
[normal] There are way way way more literate and numerate people in the world today than anytime in the past. This has been true for hundreds of years. Much of the rapid progress we have made is simply due to more people building on the work of more people. People are by far the most important ingredient in any progress we make.
[normal] One of the biggest changes in human history was the invention of writing. We could always communicate, but it was very hard to preserve information long term. 10,000 years ago, there was a profound shift in our ability to thrive in this world, and it was because we could preserve information with writing.
[normal] We now are in a new phase of history, where we can easily communicate information, as well as retrieve it, test it, discuss it, document it, and collectively advance it. We are still adapting to this profound change, but it does significantly increase our potential to make progress on all fronts. We normally attach ‘trust metadata’ to information — who sourced it, who else knows it, and who gains from it. This has always been as important as the information itself. In this new information avalanche, we unfortunately haven’t had the equivalent avalanche of metadata. Reputations of institutions and individuals disseminating information have proven a poor substitute for trust. We would do well to develop a rigorous and honest metadata system for information.
[normal] Overall, we not only have more powerful tools and a more connected society, but also a deeper understanding of ourselves. This understanding will drive much of our future — a gift in the hands of many, or a curse in the hands of few. May we err on the side of the many.
[normal] Deriving the Math
[normal] With a list of universal things, we can now attempt to derive a universal math from it. It is important to account for every step required when making a calculation. The idea of a calculation should be broadly interpreted, and exhaustively examined. We should end up with a very generic template that can be used to structure calculation, and if it is truly universal, it should be applicable far beyond traditional math.
[normal] Assess and select
[normal] The very first step in making an assessment of any sort, is to decide what to assess. This may seem obvious, but there is a lot to unpack when examining this.
[normal] Imagine selecting apples from a pile in a grocery store. Unparsing this, we must first decide we want to buy apples, and decide collecting them should be our next task. We then assess the pile, select each one by one, and retrieve them until we have enough to complete the task.
[normal] Next we may collect something else, recheck our shopping list, or imagine what we want for supper. These tasks all require focus. All tasks require focus. Because we have approximately a single focus, we are most comfortable with single selections, and tend to group multiple tasks into a single task (filling a bag with apples vs selecting an apple six times). This is true for mental tasks as well. We can only think of one thing at a time, so we reduce things to a single broad category rather than the many concepts it involves.
[normal] Single selection works fine for our minds, but of course in the real world more than one thing can happen at the same time. Somewhere else in the grocery store someone is running the checkout, perhaps a drop of rain just hit its roof, and somewhere in the galaxy some grain of sand just blew against a rock. We can selectively focus on these imaginary events one by one while reading about them, but selection is too narrow a concept to describe them all at once. This single mental focus is a very human state, but it can be interpreted broadly. Events that aren’t interacting are just independently existing, they are not ‘focused’ on each other. Wind ‘selects’ a tree by blowing on it. If there isn’t a calculation, there isn’t a selection.
[normal] Traditionally math has been about the things we inspect (mentally or physically), but with the advent of powerful computation, we can now simulate as well as calculate. This makes the selection step more of a ‘select all’, where we just run things for a number of steps and inspect the results. Simulation lies somewhere between theory and experiment, but we still select what we wish to simulate.
[normal] In the case of the universe itself, it is always selecting all, and assessing all as it goes. The main difference is those assessments are only ever dealing with fluctuations in the fields they are in, so calculations are very very local. We can see gravity causes the moon to stay close to the earth, but a moon particle no need to know the ultimate source of this force. There is no ‘selection’ needed, because there are only local fluctuations, with no alternate possibilities, no outside information, and no predictive power.
[normal] Our concept of selection can be broadened to accommodate multiple selections, and they can be assessed repeatedly. These assessments can be guided by imagination or experiment, or like most things, guided by observing meta-results of natural forces over time. Whatever the mechanism though, some form of ‘assess and select’ step is required before predictive calculation is possible.
[normal] To demonstrate this in math, we can look at something as simple as 1+1=2. The ones here represent the selection. The utility of a selection step is more obvious when looking at sequences, as in the Fibonnaci sequence: 1,1,2,3,5,8,13,21... Here the next total is created by selecting the previous two totals and adding them. Selection is a specific step in this algorithm, with a specific procedure. There are many solutions in math that rely on algorithmic or dynamic selections. It is always clarifying to determine why a particular selection was made. Understanding the question, the context, and the motivation is often as important as understanding the result.
[normal] Selection is the initial step in any mathematical process. It sometimes comes through human assessment, and sometimes it is already selected by being part of a larger system.
[normal] Select is unselect
[normal] As the first law of thermodynamics tells us, energy can not be created or destroyed. This requires the things we select to already exist, so we are actually just mentally transferring them from the set of all things to the set we have selected. The total does not change.
[normal] Even when we imagine things, there is a physical process that conserves energy as it invokes mental images from our minds. It seems natural to us that objects can be created out of nothing because this process is totally hidden from us. But they can’t be. All operations must balance once everything is accounted for.
[normal] By being aware of what we don’t select, it is easy to see that the total group we choose from can be a selection itself. When we pick apples at the grocery store pile, we are ‘not selecting’ the other apples in the pile, nor all apples in the world. This ‘total possible’ is often ignored, but is essential information for calculating things like potentials and probabilities.
[normal] Selecting is also ‘not selecting’ — there is a set that we have excluded, leaving a net zero change in the total. Of course we can ignore the unselected part, and we usually do, but at the core this isn’t how things work. Note that net zero does not imply that selections are reversible. Selections can be dynamic, and the selection itself can be ‘destructive’. Net zero just means any change is a transformation rather than actual creation or destruction.
[normal] A selection ‘equation’ would be something like: total becomes selected plus not selected — where we understand the total itself is going to be a selection. What prevents ‘total categories’ from spiraling up forever is that possible selections are bounded by what is ‘nearby’ in a physical or mental context. We can build a chain of contexts to anywhere we care to imagine, but we don’t need to invoke the Milky Way galaxy every time we focus on a mound of apples. Even for inanimate interactions, distance and time serve to keep selections immediate and calculations local.
[normal] It isn’t just selecting that is formed by opposing operations - add has subtract, multiplication has the reciprocal (division), rotate has unrotate, and on and on. We see this most clearly when we manipulate an equation around the equal sign, but it is everywhere. It is everywhere because it has to be. Energy is always conserved.
[normal] At higher levels of abstraction this pattern is sometimes called ‘duality’, where for example a cube is the dual of an octahedron (the center faces of one become the points of the other). Geometric algebra leverages this type of duality to reveal quite amazing concepts hidden in everyday geometry.
[normal] Every operation, including selection, is a rearrangement of what exists. To fully account for an operation, the books must balance. It is the law.
[normal] Everything measurable is a trait
[normal] A trait is any kind of property that can be measured, sometimes belonging to an object, sometimes not. It is the rough equivalent of what an adjective describes in language (we say adjectives describe nouns, but in reality they quantify properties of nouns). When we say a dog is warm, small, furry and brown, we are quantifying the traits of temperature, size, texture/hair type, and color.
[normal] We can only add trait values together if they are of the same trait. If a tree grows one foot the first year, and two feet the second year, we know this tree is then three feet tall. However if it grew one foot the first year, and grew two branches the second year, we do not add these numbers.
[normal] In algebra, traits implied and based on user context. It only deals with measures, and these can be letters (unknowns) or numbers (knowns). The equation 3a+2b only talks of quantities, with the unwritten context that is filled in per use. This makes it a very powerful tool for abstraction For example calculating the growing length of hair or the slow warming of a stove may well use the same formula — and certainly the same algebraic system.
[normal] In math, single letters can also represent constant measures such as e, or constant formulas such as Σ, specific vectors such as V and many other things. Traits are sometimes implied with letters, such as V=IR always representing voltage, current, and resistance. The math part is generally trait less, where science and engineering often implies traits. But it is a mess. Formulas such as 4/3πr³ certainly suggest a sphere, and r the trait of ‘radius’.
[normal] Traits are things that can be measured. Math is mostly about the measures and how they can naturally relate to each other.
[normal] Multiple traits
[normal] Related traits can sometimes be combined into a new trait by a specific form of multiplying (more on multiplying later). For example width and height traits can be joined, giving an area trait. Different traits can also be combined into new traits as well. Width and temperature can form a new trait in a system that somehow relates them, like with thermal expansion. Color and temperature can be combined when referring to heating metals or the warm feeling of red and cool feeling of blue. The color and temperature of apples has less utility as they are uncorrelated measures. There is no trend there, so the combination into a single trait doesn’t result in meaningful values. As we’ll see, when unrelated traits are combined (with ranges), the result is category definitions, not new traits.
[normal] Most traits can be broken into smaller traits. Even something as simple as length could be broken into the length before the midpoint, and the length after. Area can be separated into length and width, but when dealing with the area of an irregular shapes that may not be helpful - area would be be the fundamental trait there.
[normal] Sometimes traits are only partially related, like how the length and width of a triangle are related to its area. In these case a full description of the object could be made with multiple partial traits. The speed of a bicycle is affected by pedaling speed, gearing, hills  and wind - all partial ‘force’ traits. As long as all the partial traits are accounted for, the ‘speed trait’ is defined.
[normal] Uncorrelated traits that do not combine into new traits are the ‘primitives’ of any system involving magnitudes. They are the primitives of this math system as well.
[normal] Traits in the real world
[normal] There is no list of all traits, as that would be unimaginably long. Fortunately we only care about a tiny subset of all possible traits, and nature itself cares about even fewer. The precise mass of an object is something that is often relevant to us, whereas the precise qualification of textures or smells is not — to the point that we don’t even have words for most of them.
[normal] When thinking of the physics of the unfolding universe, we might imagine it is accounting for all these obscure traits. In reality it is just reacting to local conditions and has no information on why these conditions are the way they are. If photons needed to care about a star’s corona, or even its mass, that information and special calculations would have to be stored and run on every photon in the universe. There are traits that affect physics at a high level, but as you climb to that scale, these things become more about our understanding than physics itself.
[normal] As for the traits we care about, there are many, but not unimaginably many - after all, we can imagine them all. Many of these come from our evolutionary inputs and needs, such as sensory input, motion, nutrition, safety, reproduction, knowledge, and social chess. Others are more abstract, but tend to be built on these primitive traits through metaphor. There is no specific number, but one hundred traits would cover a lot of ground, and a thousand would probably be adequate for most situations.
[normal] Trait math
[normal] In math, traits are the thing a value is talking about, and all mathematical transformations are composed of trait changes. Even when we abstract away the traits, with 1+2=3, we have assumed these are talking about the same trait - perhaps distances on a number line or some such thing. There is always a trait backing a value. It can be very advantageous to abstract traits away, but they are what give these manipulations consistency. Fully quantifying these traits is what allows science to emerge from measurements.
[normal] As a mental model, we can imagine traits as a line. We know from the second law of thermodynamics that things can have a direction and gradient, so this line goes from low to high in at least some abstract way. This is not an infinite line, it will eventually have two ends, and as we’ll see later, those ends will be characterized. For now though, each trait is a line that represents all possible values, ordered, for that trait as you move along it.
[normal] The universe can be described in terms of its traits, and we can gain a high degree of comprehension with a limited number of them.
[normal] Focals: every value is a segment on a trait
[normal] A trait represents all possible values of a property, and a value is a segment on it. We’ll call these values ‘focals’, as existing words like point, value or segment already have meanings. Focal suggests the part that is focused on, whether a point or segment. It also suggests that the values aren’t necessarily exact, which is correct.
[normal] The endpoints of focals can never be described with infinite precision or infinite certainly. The simple reason for this is that nothing can be measured or even described to an infinite precision. This is true in all math, though not always admitted. The reason we get away with this is that we can usually measure things with much more precision than we need.
[normal] If it is impossible to measure an endpoint with infinite precision, then it stands to reason that a single point will also have limited precision. That doesn’t make it a segment, it is more a qualified blur. But along a line, a blur has length, or at least length potential. We’ll call these focus points when referring to them specifically, but in reality they are just very short focals. Two pieces of information are considered equal when we can’t distinguish them from each other, so when a focal becomes so short that the endpoints can’t be separated, it also becomes indistinguishable from a focus point.
[normal] Trait and focal endpoints must be characterized
[normal] If everything is a segment, and both scale and precision have limits, then what happens at the endpoints? They can’t be infinitely precise points, so they need to be characterized. We need to define why they end, and how they end. Traits are not a purely abstract concept, they are actual properties of things we can inspect or imagine. So the how and why of endpoints will vary, and our goal is to qualify that variation.
[normal] So how do real properties behave at their limits? It would be very useful to quantify these behaviors, as they contain meta-information that can be incorporated into calculations. This would be difficult to incorporate into terse mathematical syntax, and calculation would be difficult. However, it is straightforward to incorporate into code for a math library, where use and calculation become mostly automatic.
[normal] It should be possible to create an exhaustive list of the ways measurable things behave at their limits. Here is a first attempt at such a list.
[normal] Tolerance
[normal] Very often our precision in measuring something is limited by not needing more precision. Whenever we round numbers, like a three hour drive or even a hot day, we could have been more precise but didn’t need to be. More precision implies the extra information is important, so unnecessarily adding it leads to misunderstanding. Driving 3 hours, 7 minutes and 21 seconds puts the time in focus rather than the trip.
[normal] There is often more precision available, but the context calls for a certain number of significant figures. A person’s height is usually measured to the nearest inch. People are about an inch taller when they wake up, so extra precision here would at least need an asterisk. Weight fluctuates even more, as we go through around 12 pounds (6 kg) of food and water per day. Again, 12 pounds is not exactly 6kg, but they are both very approximate numbers.
[normal] It would be nice to be able to mark numbers by the precision of their estimates. People are usually careful to use significant figures correctly in science, but it is a pretty adhoc system based mostly on trust.
[normal] Limits of precision
[normal] As one moves to the extreme ends of traits and precision, things get harder to measure. There are two reasons for this, first it is physically hard to measure things that are at such different scales, and second, it’s hard to tell if you are right once you’re done. We can date rocks that are over 3 billion years old, measure distances to far away galaxies, and measure distances down to picometers. With all these extreme measurements, error bars increase until they overwhelm the measurement itself, so precision is more of a probability cutoff than any exactness. It’s also normal for these estimates to be adjusted as we find new techniques to measure things.
[normal] Limits happen at both the small and large scale. At one point numbers just become too large, distances too great, or time too distant. At the small scale, well things just become too small. There can still be information here, for example often a value will approach a value without ever reaching it. This is commonplace in calculus, but also with temperature approaching absolute zero. Numbers themselves exhibit this trait, as they approach some ideal value as you add precision, but you never reach that infinite precision.
[normal] The limits of our precision have been improving rapidly for the last few hundred years. These abilities are one of the key drivers of progress. We should be aware when a value represents ‘the best we can currently do’, as the quality of its information will likely increase over time. It also may indicate a bottleneck that needs to be overcome before more progress can be made.
[normal] Transformation
[normal] Objects are defined by trait values, and these values normally remain in the ranges appropriate for the object. What happens when moving outside these ranges depends on both the trait and object, but it is common that the object just becomes something else. For example larger and larger grains of sand will eventually become a stone, then a boulder, and at the extremes a planet, a star, and a black hole. This is all just conceptually increasing the mass of an object. Increasing the temperature of ice will eventually make it steam, increasing differences in speaking go from accents to dialects and languages. If we keep dividing the amount of water in a cup, we eventually get to a single molecule of water, and after that we have hydrogen and oxygen.
[normal] Transformation at limits can either be some form of phase transition, or it can just be the limit of our definition. Often it is a mix of both, and often there are overlapping gray areas. A one year old is very different than a 40 year old. One is a child, one an adult. There is an ‘definition transition’ on someone’s 18th birthday, but people near that age could be considered either, depending on context. The trait of age doesn’t pause as the trait of childhood transitions to the trait of adulthood.
[normal] It can be important to know what values outside focal ranges mean. Sometimes this indicates an error, and sometimes it is just a change in category.
[normal] Limits of computation
[normal] There are things that start easy, and get progressively harder until they become impossible at the limit. Factoring large numbers is a good mathematical example of this concept. Factoring numbers under one hundred is a grade school problem, yet factoring very large numbers is so hard that it’s the basis of modern cryptography.
[normal] When thinking of precision, the value of PI is a better example. It doesn’t have a natural limit, and people have calculated it to trillions of digits. But there is a limit, because as numbers get that precise, inconsequential things come to dominate. Even storing a number that large is a huge undertaking, and the time and cost of computation becomes a limiting factor. Even encoding a number with a predictable pattern, like 1.66666… will eventually exhaust all resources. Patterns with even a tiny amount of computation per step, like the Fibonacci Sequence, become uncomputable at scale. Exponentials or a complex series can grow beyond limits quite quickly. And long before resource limits, the numbers become uninterpretable anyway.
[normal] Some things are known to not terminate naturally, but they will still overwhelm any system at scale. This can happen with complexity, predictability, interpretability, computability or just plain size. It is useful to know when measures are limited by resources, because it usually means they are unlimited in most practical senses. We can fix a practical limit and just label it beyond our resolution. A million to the power of a million a million times is indistinguishable from 1/0. And 1 divided by this result is indistinguishable from 0, at our resolution.
[normal] Error and uncertainty
[normal] Measurement error will often result in a cluster of samples around the measurement point, with positions closer to the truth being more likely. These points will almost always follow a normal (Gaussian) distribution. Understanding this probability distribution allows multiple measurements to give higher accuracy, with higher confidence than a single one.
[normal] It is important to remember that every sampled value on a segment will have the same measurement error, not just the endpoints. The difference is samples near the endpoints are less likely to lie within the segment. So when asking if a sample is within a focal range on a trait, we get a ‘true or false’ answer with high confidence everywhere except near (and especially just beyond) the endpoints. Like the best approximation with a tape measure, we can be certain our 5.19 lies between 1 and 10, but less certain it lies between 1 and 5.2.
[normal] Another common source of error comes from noise. Random noise interference in a signal will result in a normally distributed error. There are also many non random sources of interference. Overlapping signals create noise for each other in a data line, much like multiple people talking at the same time.
[normal] There are many other types of error. Breaks in a signal, signal offsets, and of course palin old mistakes in transmission. As error increases or compounds, it can swamp the signal making further measurement meaningless. Characterizing measurement error is very important when working at the limits of our precision. Even when working within tolerances, we must be careful error is uniform and doesn’t compound.
[normal] Error should be estimated and tracked directly in the mathematical system we use to calculate, rather than with an adhoc secondary step.

[normal] Every comparison requires a unit
[normal] A single measurement alone contains no information. Imagine a photo that contains a single line. Without context there would be no way of knowing if it is one micron or one light year in length. When starting from nothing, all we can do is compare measured values and say something about the comparison.
[normal] Moving from one thing to two comparable things is an extremely significant step, as we now have a category. With two lines, even though we still don’t know anything about the scale, we can compare them. For example one line might be about twice as long as the other, making the other about half as long as the first. We made one line the ‘unit’, then used that unit to estimate the relative length of the other line. Switching the unit to be the second line gives the reciprocal ratio. Just as there is no universal length, there is no universal unit, so the unit selection is arbitrary.
[normal] This isn’t just for measuring the length lines of course. Two temperatures, two colors, or even two tastes can be compared. Any two focal segments on the same trait can be compared. However, two segments on different traits, or the two separate traits themselves can not be compared unless they form a single combined trait (two lengths form an area). We can’t compare 3 inches to 40 degrees.
[normal] We choose standard units for common measures because there is no easy way to communicate a unit without an already existing unit. These standards are arbitrary, but tend to be in common sizes that keep the values in practical ranges for everyday use. We usually have multiple ways of measuring the same trait based on context, and they are usually convertible to each other if need be. Lengths have inches and centimeters and kilometers. These are different systems at different scales, but with known conversions they are all compatible focal segments on the distance trait.
[normal] Numbers come from ratios and definitions
[normal] Before introducing numbers, we should note how much we can do without them. For most of human evolution we didn’t have a very advanced numbering system, if any at all. We of course could tell many from few, but that doesn’t mean we had even the numbers from 1 to 10. Euclidean geometry was about constructing and comparing points, lines and circles, and was built without any numeric coordinates (which would have to wait until Descartes invented Cartesian coordinates in the 1600’s).
[normal] There are two options for generating numbers with the simple line system we’ve discussed so far.
[normal] First, we can use the ratios of two lines, making one of them the unit. If we can stretch these lines, then we stretch the non unit line to be twice as long, three times as long, four times as long etc. If we physically would do this, we can imagine the precision would be limited by our ability to measure. This gives us a continuous number system (we can create 2.5), within the precision of our abilities.
[normal] A second system could be created by just creating more lines and counting them. This will give us exactly one line, exactly two lines etc, and we don’t need a unit. The precision is limited here by the fact that these lines do not need to be the same length. This is also why this system will always be discrete - you can’t make half a line because it will still be a line. More subtly, the precision of a discrete system is always limited by the definition of the unit element in the system. In this case it is a line, but we might not count a line so short that it seems to be a dot, or a line drawn over another line. Even a Y shaped line could be 1, 2, or 3 lines depending on our element definition.
[normal] These two systems are very fundamental to how we see the world. Our number line is based on continuous stretching, and our counting is based on definitions of a discrete unit element. They do interleave though. As discrete elements move below the tolerances we care about we start considering them discrete. We don’t say ‘a sand’ or ‘a grain’, but we say ‘a pebble’ or ‘a rock’. We also build up definitions of discrete elements with multiple traits, using probable ranges of values for each. As these definitions get more specific, we can often start dividing discrete objects, like ‘half an apple’, but not ‘half a stick’. You can also use both systems together, for example by combine discrete ‘lines’ by their length rather than their existence.
[normal] It is very important to know if you are dealing with discrete or continuous measures, as the possible operations on each are very different. Combining discrete elements is fundamentally addition and subtraction, and stretching continuous segments is fundamentally multiplication and division. But more on this later.
[normal] Everything that can be done with a segment on a line
[normal] Traits are properties that have magnitudes ordered from lowest to highest in a linear gradient. They can be thought of as an uncalibrated line that could be extended to the lowest and highest possible values for that trait. A focal segment is a segment of any length along this line. So we have a line and segments on it. This defines one dimension.
[normal] With such a fundamental object, we should try to exhaustively enumerate all the unique operations that can be done with it.
[normal] Select
[normal] This can be done choosing an existing selection, or creating a new one. Creating a new selection can be done manually or algorithmically.
[normal] Direction
[normal] Every trait has a gradient of magnitudes, and every segment has a start point and end point. This means the segments have information about which direction they are pointing - low to high, high to low, or can’t tell. Note this has nothing to do with how the trait line is drawn, rotation has no meaning here. It is better to imagine the line going from red to blue, and the segment’s end point getting redder or getting bluer.
[normal] Unit
[normal] Once a segment is selected, it can be labeled the unit. Numerically this will mean the start point is zero, and the end point is one. These are the only perfect numbers in this system, because they are defined to be zero and one, not measured. The zero point is defined by the unit, not the lowest possible trait value - otherwise negative numbers would be impossible. We’ll assume the unit is pointing in the positive direction, but think about what it would mean to have a negative unit.
[normal] Repeat
[normal] Whatever we do, we can do again. This implies steps done in order. Technically this brings in the new dimension of time, at least abstractly. But because everything is always moving, all things naturally exist in the time dimension. So we made a selection, now we can select a second segment. And a third. Repetition is the second fundamental action in math, after selection. In normal math repetition is, in a very limited way, expressed by exponents.
[normal] We can also select a second unit, but only one unit at a time can be used to define zero and measure. This means a second unit selection more or less deselects the first one, at least for its tasks.
[normal] Count
[normal] With more than one segment, we can now count - upwards at least. Segments do not have to be the same length to count them, and we can not have half a segment, so this only counts whole numbers.
[normal] Addition
[normal] With repeated selections and counting what is selected, we can now count using whole numbers. There is still no set order to what we count. Perhaps all segments are counted left to right, or top to bottom, or largest to smallest. Or for low numbers all items could be counted at once. This would be a user defined system, as there are many ways to order random segments. Initially all addition has to be by ones (1+1+1=3), though the ability to count and add bootstraps selecting more than one element. With that we can say 2+1=3.
[normal] Sometimes when we add to an ordered set, we care more about the resulting set than the total. For example adding C,E to A,B,D we might expect A,B,C,D,E rather than ((C,E),(A,B,D)). When combining two groups into one, we gain clarification by filtering information that isn’t relevant to our task. This is a choice, and what is useful is context sensitive. As always, there are insights to be had whenever we notice we are making a choice, so it is important to keep a broad view of what something as fundamental as addition actually represents.
[normal] Note that with all this information so far, we still can’t count backwards or subtract.
[normal] Subtraction
[normal] At its most basic, subtraction is removing from a selection. If it is an ordered selection we are removing from, we traditionally remove from the ‘end’, or remove randomly and reinterpret the results. If we add 4 oranges to a bag (1,2,3,4), and then remove one, the remaining oranges could be (1,3,4). We usually consider oranges in a bag to be unordered, so we would either reinterpret the removed orange to be the fourth one resulting in (1,2,3), or reinterpret the oranges (1,3,4) to be (1,2,3). We would call this a bag of three oranges when focused on the unordered total, but if asked to count them we would have no trouble pointing to a first, second and third, in some order.
[normal] This may seem like hair splitting, but there are certainly cases where sets of things are ordered, and we want to delete a certain member. In these situations (1,3,4) is not the same as (1,2,3). It is important to keep a broad view of what subtraction actually is, or it will be hard to understand it in less intuitive situations.
[normal] With ordered counts (like discrete numbers), it is tempting to define subtraction as just counting backwards, but that wouldn’t cover cases where you subtract one group’s count from another’s. Counting backwards with a one to one correspondence is closer, but we still need to define what ‘backwards’ means here. If we used a heuristic for ordered counting (like say smallest to largest) then we can reverse it (largest to smallest). This makes A-B a multi step process. First make a counted ordered set (B) using addition, and place it at the endpoint of A. Reverse the order of B (mirror it over the endpoint of A) so it overlays A’s last elements. Then delete the corresponding elements from A.
[normal] Note ‘corresponding elements’ can always have multiple interpretations here as there are multiple ways of defining how things should correspond. Traditionally this is just one to one for each element in its given order. But as with addition, there are many branches of mathematics where the identity of each element in a calculation matters more than its result.
[normal] Negatives
[normal] We can add and subtract, and this means the values can end up at zero or lower, leading us to values that don’t have corresponding selections. There are two ways to think about these ‘negative’ numbers. The first is they are selections that have been made but not filled (negative selections), and the second is they are still positive selections, but of an opposite type of thing. For example a person could have ‘negative ten dollars’ or just have ‘(positive) ten dollars of debt’. These are not two ways of saying the same thing, even though they seem similar. When thinking about the operation, the negative number version mirrors a value at zero, so 5 dollars becomes -5 dollars, but adding a 1 will still make you richer. This is what negative means. Moving the count from asset to debt mirrors the whole perspective of that +1 means without changing any values. The value is still 5, but it is of debt instead of credit, and now adding a dollar (of debt) makes you poorer. So negation mirrors values on a number line around zero, where the second option mirrors the number line itself. More on this crucial point later.
[normal] Compare
[normal] With more than one selection, and a unit selected, we can now compare segments. This allows us to express continuous numbers, as one segment can be 2.5 times longer than another. Switching the unit will give the reciprocal, 1/2.5 (0.4). We can also compare the start and end points to the unit’s 0 and 1.
[normal] Slide
[normal] A segment can slide along a trait, without changing its length. This would at least make for easier measuring. Also this is deleting information about the original position of the segment. We should always pay attention to deleted information as it often can be used in other ways.
[normal] Snap
[normal] As points on a segment slide along a trait, they can snap to other existing points. Like always, this isn’t an infinitely perfect overlap, just an overlap so close we can’t tell which would be to the left and which to the right.
[normal] Add and Subtract Lengths
[normal] One can imagine snapping the start and end points to other segment points as you slide them. With the unit, this allows adding (and subtracting) multiple segments by total length, rather than just counting. We notice it also deletes whitespace and overlap information between the segment’s original positions.
[normal] Repeated Add and Subtract
[normal] We can repeat and we can count, so we can repeat a set number of times. So 3+3+3+3=12. This is considered multiplication in normal math, but this is NOT multiplication because there is no stretching. The result is the same, but this is hopping from value to value rather than moving through all possible intermediate values. In 2D it would be like a grid of points rather than an area. We usually write this as 3*4=12, but that confuses it with stretching. Really it is more like (+3)4 and stretching is (*3)4.
[normal] Stretch Segments
[normal] Segments can be stretched by moving their endpoints. This allows resizing a segment without affecting anything else. If all segments are resized to unit size, then counting becomes correct with count and total length. If a segment is stretched to be three units longer, it is the same as adding three, except it is a ‘continuous add’, rather than the discrete jump when you append a segment of three units. Segments can also be resized into precise fractional ratios of the unit. For example if two unit sized segments are scaled together to be one unit size, they will each be half a unit. All rational numbers (fractions) can be created this way. Two segments would be (*unit)2, and scaling it by half would be 2√(*unit)2, or (*unit)1/2  That is ugly syntax, and we can do better, but the concept is what matters. Repetition of any types of values are expressed with exponents, and you can have a partial repetition.
[normal] Stretch Traits
[normal] This is crucial. Not only can segments be stretched, but the trait ‘number line’ itself can be stretched. This will also stretch all focal segments on the trait line as well. This has the effect of  zooming in or out. However, if the unit of measure is preserved (or the whole trait), then the stretched elements will have new values that are the multiple of the amount stretched. This is multiplication, but all call it scaling to avoid confusion with multiplying by repeating additions of the same number. The action here is stretching (3*4) rather than repeated addition (4+4+4), so it is continuous.
[normal] Scaling is key to understanding a lot of what comes next, so double check the diagrams or watch the video if you are confused by this explanation. Scaling pivots at zero, so it will grow (or shrink) linearly in each direction from the unit’s zero point. Selecting the trait at the endpoint of the unit (the one point of the unit), and dragging it to the 3 position (meaning drag the whole timeline by stretching it) will multiply every segment on that trait by three. Selecting the trait at the 3 point and dragging it to the unit’s endpoint will multiply everything on that trait by 1/3. Note again that the trait pivots on the zero point of the unit as it’s scaled, so the negative side will stretch in the opposite direction to the positive side. Dragging from a point on the positive side to the negative side (or vice versa) is the same as scaling by a negative number– all segments will end up on the opposite side of zero compared to where they started.
[normal] Repeated Stretches
[normal] Stretching a trait by three units will scale everything by three, so doing the same operation again (using the original three unit distance will scale everything by three again. This is squaring. Doing it one more time is cubing, and so on. This is different than multiplying by 9 or 27 because the operation of squaring is independent of the scaling value. If you would after the fact change the three to a two you would still be squaring and cubing, but your end values would be 4 and 8 rather than 9 and 27. In the equation (*n)3, the n is any value, and it will be stretched (*) three times due to the exponent value.
[normal] Notice that this is the first time an operation can not guarantee it could end up on any point on this number line. If you square a number, it can’t end up on the negative side of zero. This is true in normal math as well and we are used to it, but it is a bit strange that there exist primitive operations that forbid certain results. It would seem unnatural if we couldn’t stretch (or shrink) a rubber band in the same way twice and not end up in places we could reach by stretching it only once. How does the elastic know about squaring negative numbers? These special cases require the often baffling “square root of negative one” unit called i. The next sections will try to ‘debaffle’ this problem.
[normal] Measurements and Transformations
[normal] When measuring something, we record the result as a number. We tend to think of numbers as a record of some value. Looking at equations however, it’s clear we also use numbers to represent transformations, like (-3*4+5). On a number line this says scale a copy of the unit by 4 (always start with the * element) mirror it (with the ‘-‘) and concatenation three copies of this. This gives a segment from 0 to -12, or if the addition is kept discrete, three segments of length -4 lined up starting at zero and ending at -12. Finally shift these elements to the right by 5 units. The tip of these directed segments ends at -8 as we would expect, but the full result is a set of 3 segments that start at 3 and end at -8. This is different than (+4*-2), which would be four segments of length -2, starting at 0 and ending at -8. Also different is (-2*4), which is two mirrored segments of length 4, starting at 0 and ending at -8. To get a solid segment, we could say (*-2*4). Unfortunately traditional math syntax was not designed for this level of precision, so it will look awkward. It is worth pursuing though. We usually ignore everything but the result in equations, but in many cases this lost information is relevant.
[normal] It is easy to see each operation/value as a recipe for a transformation when looking at an equation. More subtly, addition is always a shift of elements along the number line that therefore never creates ‘area’. Scaling makes an existing area larger or smaller, and will only shift things as a consequence of the scaling. The default starting point for addition is the zero position of the unit, and the default transformation target for a scale is the unit. An equation of (*5) really should be (unit*5), and an equation of (-3) is really (0-3).
[normal] It is now clear that to give the full information of what a number represents, we need to add the operator. The number 3 tells us something about three units, but only +3, -3, *3, /3, or x3 (repeat 3) give us enough information to draw it on a number line. +3 represents the position 3, and will transform any element three units to the right. *3 represents a segment from 0 to 3, and will stretch any existing element by 3 times its length. x3 represents the positions 1,2,3, (not a segment) and will cause an operation to happen 3 times. There are other operators (powers for example), and numbers also have imaginary sides, but we will cover this later.
[normal] Recording the transform of a number explicitly allows much more clarity in what is happening in an expression, and gives you the option of not throwing away potentially important information in an equation.
[normal] Rate of Motion
[normal] A full number has a value and a transform, and is agnostic to the element it transforms. As mentioned above, the default ‘input’ element for addition is 0, and the default input for scaling it is the unit. Because this input can be any element, the full value also represents a description of the transformation it causes. If a full number represents the record of a value and the transformation it causes, it makes sense that it also characterized the transformation. This is done by quantifying how a change in input changes the output, the ‘rate of change’. This is the basis of calculus, specifically it is the derivative.
[normal] First, if you just have a full number where the input is 0 (addition) or unit (scaling), then the result will always be the same, so the change is 0. The derivative of a constant is 0.
[normal] Addition of a full value will always offset an element, but only by the offset of its value. So +2 will never be more or less that +2 units away from the input. This can be thought of as 1:1 with the input, or 1 scaled by the net change. This means the derivative of addition is always 1.
[normal] Scaling by a full number will change the input to be scaled proportionally by its value. A full value of *3 will always change the input to be 3 times its original value relative to the unit. The stretch is always directly proportional to the value of the full number, so the derivative of a scaling is always the scaling amount.
[normal] Repeated scaling will grow input at an accelerating rate. If you scale something twice, it will change by the scaling rate twice. Scaling by the same amount five times will change it at five times the scaling rate. (*3x5) will change any input to be scaled by 15, and (*SCALExREPEAT) will change the input to be whatever the scale is, multiplied by the number of times it repeats. The derivative of repeated(r) scaling(s), also known as an exponent, is s*r.
[normal] These are the simplest derivatives, however things go much deeper of course. For example, a derivative of a repeated scaling can just be declared to be equal to itself (meaning it is changing by 3 times if it is 3, by 8 times if it is 8). This ends up being true when Euler’s number(e) is raised to any power. Another interesting one is when the derivative is the reciprocal of itself, which as we’ll see results in sine and cosine.
[normal] The most important thing to understand with all these rates of change, is that one dimension (a line) can fully represent all as pest of a full number, but only if the extra dimensions are encoded as time. If only taking a frozen snapshot, *2 repeated three times is indistinguishable from *6. When allowing for the time component (repeats), we can compress any number of dimensions into one, but essentially need animation (repeated frames) to visualize it.
[normal] The full representation of a value includes the operator, and this records a value, describes a transformation, and is an action that can be applied to transform an input. An adjective/noun, an adverb/verb, and an event. All in one.
[normal] Negative Units
[normal] So far units have always been positive. That is intuitive to us, as all our math is based on positive units, but that isn’t a reason to not allow negative units. If we are going to allow them, what exactly do they mean? If positive units start at zero and move to the right to define one, then negative units start at zero and move to the left to define one. With a single 1D line, we can’t know if it is rotated or twirled, but we do know a forward and backwards direction. It is better to think of all lines as having hot and cool ends. Rotating or twirling such a line doesn’t change the information in the line, but we can always move to be warmer, or move in the opposite direction to be cooler. The fact that we represent increasing values (hotter) as moving to the right/forward is arbitrary. In some ways, with energy naturally dispersing in the universe, decreasing values over time(cooler) are a more natural ‘forward’. The direction we choose to be positive is arbitrary, but the difference is real. There are two ways to represent this type of gradient line.
[normal] Another mental analogy is to imagine going to work and coming home on the same route. On the way to work, your distance traveled starts at zero, and increases until you arrive. On the way home, you start at work, and your distance increases until you arrive home. This is the same path, but your orientation has changed. There are two ways to think about measurements in this path, one with a ‘from home’ unit we can call positive, and one with a ‘from work’ unit we can call negative.
[normal] A third analogy, just because this is so important! It is tempting to thing there are always ‘universal’ positive directions on a trait, say increasing information or energy vs decreasing. Due to laws of entropy this is often true, but there are many examples where it isn’t as well. We specify north as ‘the top’, but that is arbitrary in spite of being very common. The English alphabet is left to right, while many others are right to left. Ancient writing often wrote in a snake pattern, where each line alternated between left to right and right to left. There is no ‘universal’ more/less information direction in this choice. If we specify the ‘third’ word in a line, we need to know what direction to start from. In math we’d label our preferred direction as real, and the other as imaginary, but so would people whose alphabets are written in opposite directions. The takeaway is there is no universal direction, but there are always two known ways of looking at it. This isn’t just a preferred way of encoding either — we can flip which side is imaginary while working and there are real consequences. And unsurprisingly, the universe does this all the time. Where there is possible, there is actual.
[normal] This all may seem like semantics, but any time you can separate two phenomena, it represents an actual difference. If there are two ways to represent ‘forward’ on something as fundamental as a 1D line, then it has to be an important part of the natural math of one dimension.
[normal] Negative unit number lines and positive unit number lines are exactly the same thing, other than the unit. A trait can have a positive unit and a negative unit without physically changing any of its contents. However, the interpretation of values and application of actions will change based on the type of unit used, just as with a longer vs shorter unit.
[normal] Unit and Unot
[normal] Negative units and positive units are important, and can create confusing phrases like, ‘positive three on the number line using a negative unit’. For this reason ‘normal’ positive units will be referred to as units, and negative units will be called unots. Three units is our normal 3, and three unots is 3 on a number line measured by a negative unit (so the position of -3 on the equivalent unit number line).
[normal] Addition with Negative Units
[normal] Compared to a positive unit, a segment on a line calibrated with a negative unit (a unot) will have everything mirrored. If the end point is to the left of the zero point the value is positive, and if it is to the right, it is negative.
[normal] For the action of addition, new segments are always added or subtracted to the left most point rather than the right most point.
[normal] If a positive segment was taken from the unit number line and copied as is to the unot number line, it would then point in the negative. To do a true representational copy, the whole number line of the copied element would have to flip as it moved to the negative unit number line. The mechanics of a unot is to flip a number line around its zero point, and then measure with positive to the left instead of our traditional right.
[normal] Stretching by Negative Units
[normal] Stretching a segment on the unot side works as addition does, where scaling by 2 will move the left point until the segment is twice as long, and scaling by -2 will flip the segment over the zero point and stretch until it is twice as long in the other direction. Note however, that this is scaling by 2 ‘normal’ positive units. What does it mean to scale by unots (negative units)?
[normal] As we saw in the introduction to unots, they invert the whole number line. Normally scaling by a negative value will invert the element being scaled, but this is different as it inverts the whole line, but leaves the elements where they are. So scaling by 1 unot just flips the number line around its zero point. Scaling by 2 unot flips the number line and scales its contents to be twice as large. Scaling by -2 unots flips the number line, and then flips its contents while scaling to twice the size.
[normal] So in all scaling there are really two operations, one on the timeline (trait) and one on the contents (focal segments on the trait). We normally don’t notice this as the unit effect on the number line is to ‘scale by 1’ which does nothing.
[normal] As an example, we’ll repeatedly scale 1 (a unit) by unot.
[normal] First run, flip the number line of the 1 segment, which makes it a unot based line. The segment is also flipped by this, so while it still starts at zero and points to the positive direction, the positive direction is left on this line. The two number lines are now synchronized as a unot line, so we can multiply as normal. The two values are 1 and 1, so this creates 1 unot.
[normal] Second run, scale the new ‘1 unot’ by unot. Again this flips the number line, which becomes a normal unit number line again. The endpoint is still +1, now pointing to the right. Again, now that these number lines have been synchronized we can multiply the segments normally. We have a positive 1, and a negative 1 (the location of 1 unot that we are scaling by on the ‘normal’ number line is -1). 1 scaled by -1 is -1 units. Notice how scaling 1 by unot two times (squaring) results in -1. The square root of -1 is unot. Incidentally, with this we can now once again reach any point on a number line by an operation repeated twice.
[normal] Third run, scale -1 by unot. We flip the number line again, creating a -1 unot. The unot we are scaling by has a value of 1 on the unot number line, so 1 scaled by -1 is -1 unot.
[normal] Lastly, multiply -1 unot by unot. This creates a -1 once the number line is flipped, and the unot value on this normal number line is also -1. So -1 scaled by -1 is 1 unit.
[normal] As you may have guessed, the unot number line that uses the mirrored perspective of our normal number line (left is forward rather than right) is where complex numbers come from. The unot part is traditionally called the imaginary part. There will be a lot more to say about this, but for now, complex numbers are a very natural and essential part of math. They are easy to reason about, and are present in all calculations we do whether we ignore them or not.
[normal] A Segment is a Complex Number
[normal] With complex numbers now being defined on a one dimensional trait line, they no longer have the ability to rotate, or any sort of two dimensional graphing. To graph a traditional complex number like 2+3i, there needs to be a point at 2 in the unit frame of reference (to the right of zero), and a point at 3 in the unot frame of reference (to the left of zero). Joining these creates a segment, which means any complex number can be represented as a segment on a trait.
[normal] Normally a point on a line is quantified its distance from zero, based on a unit size. If we would display this as a segment, it would start at zero and end at the point. This zero point in the new system represents the complex point 0i. A starting point of zero doesn’t affect calculations when we only care about the value of the end point, so we mostly don’t notice that it is even there. However, there are many areas in math where this imaginary side matters a lot. Every measurement on a trait is a segment - even a point. The segment from 0 to 3 is 3+0i, where the point at 3 is 3-3i.
[normal] Everything measurable is a segment, and every segment is a complex number.
[normal] A Value can be both a record and an operation
[normal] When seeing a point on a numberline at 3, this can be recorded as the value 3, but it can also be seen as the result of an operation that started at zero and moved by one unit three times. 3i would be the same, except we know it happened on the imaginary side. A segment from 0 to 7 can be described with a start point and end point, but it can also be the result of an operation that stretches a unit to be 7 times its original length. Similarly, 7i would be that same stretching, but only after ‘i’ flips the whole timeline. All constructs in math have this dual ability to represent the result of a transformation from a ‘unit’ starting position, or represent the action of the transformation itself.
[normal] In this sense, we can think of addition as acting on the discrete unit of a value (either an end point, or a count of one segment), and scaling acting on the whole segment continuously. It doesn’t make sense to scale a position even though all measures are segments, as we don’t know which direction it points when the ends overlap like this. Scaling the definition of a segment (needed for counting them) doesn’t make sense either. Similarly, we can’t get a continuous range with a single addition, so ranges will always indicate scaling. Of course we can still add total lengths of stretched elements, stretch a unit to a position, and use the end point of a stretch in further addition. These are the results of two operations however. It’s only the ease of conversion between addition and scaling that makes them feel like they both apply equally to segments and positions. Addition doesn’t leave a ‘trail’ like scaling, and therefore it only transforms positions, as scaling only transforms ranges.
[normal] When trying to derive processes in more abstract domains, the transformation meaning of a value will be as important as the numbers it is made up of. 2+3i is as much a transformation recipe that can be applied to other segments, as it is an encoded representation of the ‘2+3i’ segment.
[normal] Comparing
[normal] Comparisons are measures of similarity to some unit. To compare complex numbers, we should first attempt an exhaustive list of the ways values can be compared. This will help us explore the many ways complex numbers (full real world values) can be compared.
[normal] There are three ways of comparing two values: compare A to B, compare B to A, or compare A and B to a third value C. The first two define the unit using only A or B, while the third allows an external unit C. However, as discussed elsewhere, there are no universal external units. This means C is actually a third value here, and so not a valid way of comparing two values. When not using an external unit (so only using A and B), there are still a number of variations that can be used for a comparison.
[normal] At the most basic, values can be compared additively or by scaling. Scaling answers ‘which is larger’, while additive shows ‘how much extra’. In these examples we can imagine lines on a page where we are comparing length, however as always these types of comparisons will apply to any measurable values.
[normal] Additive comparisons are of the form A-B or B-A, where one element becomes the unit (so 1-X or X-1). The result is the ‘extra’ (or the not enough). To measure this extra value would still require scaling, but even without measurement, the result is the absolute difference between the two values. Repeated subtractions of the smaller unit could give a rough count, but scaling can also answer count.
[normal] Estimating fractions can be done additively using only discrete values, by always taking remainders less than the unit and making them the unit. As an example, if you have a length 2.5 times larger than another, the smaller one goes into the larger 2 times with a remainder. This remainder goes into the original unit 2 times, meaning it was 0.5 times the size of the original. Adding these together gives us 2.5. We can get this result a little more naturally with scaling however, as scaling is continuous to the limit of our precision. Scaling the smaller unit into the larger one requires us to stretch it 2.5 times its original size. While scaling may be a more natural way of measuring value comparisons, there are entire branches of important mathematics that use discrete techniques. As always, every fork in the path is valid and leads to valuable insights.
[normal] While scaling comparisons are computationally simpler, they still allow many potential ways of comparing two values. As with additive comparisons, there must always be a unit. The possible comparisons are A/B, B/A, and then iterations with a composite unit like A/(A+B), or even B/(A*B).
[normal] We can also control our ‘selection of unit’ step, using a heuristic rather than A or B. For example, the heuristic ‘Select the longest line for the unit’ results in a value normalized between 0 and 1. This normalized value expresses how similar A and B are, with 1 being the same and 0 being not at all the same. ‘Select the shortest line for the unit’ would also compare similarity, though the result would always be greater than or equal to 1, and larger would mean less similar.
[normal] If we expand this to allow for negative lengths (imagine directed lengths on a number line rather than lines on a page), then we need to define what we mean by ‘longest’ when selecting the unit. If longest means ‘visually the longest’ (the absolute length), then comparisons result in a value between -1 and 1, with 1 being the most similar, 0 being the most unrelated, and -1 being the most mirrored. If ‘longest’ means ‘the most positive’ (greatest value), then if either line has a positive length, the comparison results in a value of 1 or less, and two negative lengths create a value of 1i or greater. The ‘most negative’ version would be similar, but any negative number creates a value greater than 0i, and two positives will be 1 or greater. All of these could be done using the ‘shortest’ unit rather than longest, resulting in opposite permutations. This all gets a bit complex, and starts to feel like we are inventing rules rather than discovering them. It also seems a bit unnatural that comparisons between two lines have forbidden values. The only heuristic here that allows a result that could potentially fall anywhere on the number line is a random selection of the unit. This is resolved in the next section when comparing segments.
[normal] Another option for comparing two values by scale is to synthesize the unit, for example making it the sum of A and B. Technically this unit is a third value, but because it is created from the first two should be at least considered. The (A+B) unit results in A/(A+B) and B/(A+B), each being a proportion from 0 to 1 (assuming only positive lengths). Allowing for both positive and negative values for A and B, the unit construction has a few options. If is the total absolute length (|A|+|B|), the resulting ratios would represent the amount of positive vs the amount of negative. If two values are positive in this scenario, the positive ratio would be 1, and the difference between the two positive numbers would be lost. Alternately, a system going from -1 to 1 could be used here, though this would require supplementary rules to define edge cases.
[normal] This is an example of how simple comparisons can break down using just our traditional real numbers. More and more ‘user defined’ rules are required, and the system becomes dependent on tradition as much as reasoning. And it isn’t just with comparisons. Imagine a segment that starts at 3 and ends at 5. Multiplying this by 2 would result in a 6 to 10 segment. But what would it mean to do the reverse, multiply those segments by each other? Or multiply them by a segment going the other direction, or existing on the imaginary side of the number line?
[normal] This complexity isn’t due to nature, it is simply because we are used to ignoring the imaginary parts of numbers. We do this because they are often (but not always!) zero. When the imaginary parts of a full number contain valid information that is discarded, we define a somewhat arbitrary set of rules on what to discard and how to preserve the rest, based on our needs of the moment. This more or less works, but a much better (and existing) solution is to just use complex numbers to preserve everything. Arbitrary heuristics have their place, but their price is usually steep: added complexity, destroyed information, and obfuscation of what is really happening.
[normal] Note: In all of this, we haven’t compared using zero. This isn’t avoidance. If an additive measure isn’t defined, then there aren’t two things to compare. If a scalable measure is zero, we know it isn’t actually zero, just beyond our resolution. In this case x/0 would simply be higher than our maximum resolution.
[normal] Segments as complex numbers
[normal] Complex numbers consist of a start point (the imaginary part) and an endpoint (the ‘real’ part), making a segment. Each of these points can also be thought of as separate segments starting from zero, which equates to the 2+3i type of complex encoding we are used to. We often think of ‘real’ numbers as a point on the numberline, but it is better to think of them as a segment that starts at zero and ends at the number. In this case the number would either be a complex number with a 0i imaginary part, or the endpoint of a segment with the start point defined elsewhere. Either way, when comparing two segments (complex numbers) there are four positions we need to look at, the two points of the first term (A and Ai), and the two points of the second (B and Bi). Using all four points allows all types of comparisons between all types of segments a valid operation. Not only can one segment transform into another through addition or multiplication, things like the square root of a segment can be calculated without worrying about NaN’s or infinities (in computer parlance).
[normal] These complex number segments are directed, meaning they have a start point and end point, and point towards the end point. For now we say the imaginary part is the start point, and the real part is the end point. This fits our current number system, where we mostly ignore an imaginary part with zero value, and just take the end point as the value. But remember, there is no universal preference for which side should be labeled imaginary. If you rotate the whole trait, the previously imaginary part will point to the right, and it behaves exactly as the former real part did. There is no measurable difference between these, other than they define ‘forward’ in opposite ways. This makes for two main operations, mirroring and rotating. Mirroring is negating a value, where 3-2i becomes -3+2i. Rotating is making the imaginary part real, and the real part imaginary, exactly as if you rotated the trait itself (though this isn’t a requirement of course, you are just changing the orientation). This pattern of rotation and negation is key for all math to come, so it is good to understand it well. Rotation and negation aren’t fundamental due to some magic cosmic dictate. They are fundamental because there are only four possible changes that can happen to a measurable value - move ‘forward’ and increase, move ‘backwards’ and decrease, and then from the rotated perspective, move forward and decrease, move backwards and increase.
[normal] Manipulating Segments
[normal] To compare two segments, all four endpoints are required (A start, A end, B start, B end). If just comparing the lengths, we need to ignore any gaps or shifts of these lengths along the numberline, meaning the segment must be shifted until the start point is zero. This shouldn’t be confused with ignoring the start point though! We need to shift it so the result isn’t affected by it, not ignore it.
[normal] These four points result in many choices in how elements are compared, some of them more intuitive (though not ‘more valid’) than others. It is always helpful to try and ground these operations in a concrete example. When comparing your work-to-home route to your home-to-work route there are a lot of symmetries, especially if it is just the same route backwards. But what about comparing two separate sections of each? Would the ‘total’ distance of the two be an addition or a subtraction? How would one scale into the other? One can imagine sliding start to start and end to end, but what does the encoding of that operation look like? Two segments are certainly comparable, but there are many ways to go about it. Existing addition and scaling process definitions can be extended to define processes for comparing two segments.
[normal] Adding Segments
[normal] Segment addition is intuitive - just add the start points to get the final start point, and add the end points to get the final end point. This is how complex addition works as well. Subtraction of course works the same, other than subtracting like points rather than adding them. Notice this is different than what the conversational concept of adding segments might be, where the lengths are added up. This would be placing all segments tip to tail starting from zero, and recording the final endpoint as the total length. This is also a valid operation of course, but is not adding segments in a complex number sense.
[normal] Multiplying Segments
[normal] Scaling segments by segments isn’t always intuitive, but when they are ‘zero anchored’ things seem fairly natural. These are segments like 7+0i, -6+0i, 0+5i, or 0+-4i where they will just proportionally stretch a segment along the numberline. For example we can see a segment that starts with the range 2-&gt;4 (-2i+4) and ends with 6-&gt;12 (-6i+12) has been scaled by three. The initial gap scales pretty naturally, and even if being scaled by 3i instead of 3, we can pretty easily see the timeline flip and then the normal scaling.
[normal] Things get less intuitive when we scale by a complex number that isn’t zero anchored, as we now have to account for the ‘gap from zero’ in both elements. Imagining this using regular numbers, it is like changing the stretch amount from 0-&gt;3 to 5-&gt;8. What does it mean to stretch a segment by a certain amount that includes an initial gap like this? Should the resulting length still be the same, or is it some balance of push and pull? Clearly the offset start point should affect the result somehow, otherwise stretching by 0-&gt;3 wouldn’t be any different than stretching by 5-&gt;8.
[normal] To answer this we need to consider all the positions that are affecting the stretch. The ‘force’ of a stretch comes from all the different sections of the range causing the stretch, and the result will be a new continuous range will be made of sections that can map back to the original ones. Because these new segments will remain in order, we can reduce this whole operation to stretches on the end points, and then infill the result with interpolation. As every section of the start segment (A) will pull or push every section of the end segment (B), we can reduce this to the sum of ‘how much start point A affects segment B’, and ‘how much endpoint A affects segment B’.
[normal] This is exactly complex multiplication.
[normal] (A+Ai)*(B+Bi) = ((A*B)-(Ai*Bi)) + ((A*Bi)+(Ai*B))
[normal] This looks complicated, and we can simplify it, but the important thing is to see what it is doing. On the left side, it scales the two endpoints together, and then the two start points. The start points need to be subtracted from the end points, because they represent the ‘gap’ range that was removed from the segment zero anchored endpoints would have made. We can’t just leave it at this however, otherwise we would get the same results no matter how large the gap was. So the right half is adjusting for how much the start points push and pull this resulting segment. The result of both of these added together account for all the forces on the start and end points of the original segment, and comes out as a new complex number. There are stretches on both the real and imaginary sides here, and in two cases the timelines are flipped as well (Ai*Bi) and (A*Bi). The results aren’t always intuitive, but if you think of at forces it is at least easier to understand what is going on. And this is a very normal way for things to behave in the real world, so it is worth building an intuition.
[normal] Note that in normal math, complex numbers are placed on a 2D grid (with i being the vertical axis), and then trigonomically converted to polar coordinates of lengths and angles. Math is done in this coordinate space and then converted back to points. There gives a sense that complex numbers are two dimensional, and multiplying them somehow causes rotation. As you can see, complex numbers are one dimensional, and have nothing to do with rotation. In fact all it really says is Cartesian coordinates can be converted back and forth with polar coordinates, which has nothing to do with one dimensional complex numbers.
[normal] Comparing Segments
[normal] As with values, segments can be compared using the absolute difference (subtraction), or the scaled difference (division). Subtraction is quite intuitive, making it easy to see how the remainder segment represents the difference of the two segments being compared. Division is a complex formula and not always an intuitive result, but is one of the more accurate representations of how ranges of values compare in the real world. That said, the four end points on two segments does mean there are other valid ways of comparing, beyond subtraction and division.
[normal] A common way of comparing two segments in traditional math is the dot product. Its meaning is fairly nuanced, but this idea is you draw lines from the origin to two points, and then by (X1*X2)+(Y1*Y2) you get a single real number value you can interpret. Looking at that equation, you can see it’s kind of showing how similar the real sides are, and joining that with how similar the imaginary sides are. In 2D these values represent (something like) how much one line would affect the other if it was scaled along its direction. If the lines are perpendicular there would be no effect, so the result of the equation is zero. A negative value would mean the lines are pointing away from each other (scaling affects the other, but in the opposite direction), and positive would mean they point more in the same direction. These results can also be obtained with |a|*|b|*cos(angle) if you convert everything to polar coordinates, though that is probably true for any types of values you convert to polar coordinates. This equation normally reduces two segments to a scalar, so it does lose most of the original information, and most of the potential information from the comparison itself. The destruction comes mostly from the addition of the X and Y results, as they combine two elements into one, losing any information about the ratio between them.
[normal] When looking at (X1*X2)+(Y1*Y2) in the context of complex number segments, the equation separates the real (A) and imaginary (B) components of the segment, and keeps them separate in the result (C). Two segments compared result in a segment rather than a scalar as you don’t naturally add the real and imaginary parts together (Ai*Bi)+(A*B)=(Ci+C). Of course you can still add or otherwise manipulate the real and imaginary parts if you wish, to find length as a scalar for example. Doing this with complex number segments works like a dot product in that it compares similarity of the segments around the origin (zero). If both values of C are the same the two segments are identical scales of each other, if they add to 0 they are mirrors.
[normal] Repeating Scaling of Segments
[normal] The repeated scaling of a segment is the same as raising it to a power - repeating twice is squaring, three times is cubing, etc. The mechanics of this is just stretching the segment to be n times its length a fixed number of times. Note that it is always the total length of the last results that is stretched, not the same initial amount. +(*3) repeated three times results in three dashes, whereas *(*3) repeated three times is cubing.
[normal] The repeated element getting stretched does not have to be constant. It can be derived from the result as with Euler’s number, it can be a series, or it can be arbitrary. Arbitrary becomes regular multiplication, which is very useful, but loses the special properties the constraints of powers bring. The number of times something is repeated can also change in each step. This is common with polynomials, in the form of 8x4-4x3+3x2−7x+1=27. The segment version of this would be the unit x being stretched repeatedly in steps, where the next polynomial always appends a new unit to the last result before stretching. The result is multiple directed segments all tip to tail, and the final tip would be the location of the final endpoint. The whole thing can be then scaled so the final position matches the desired result (27 in this case).
[normal] Due to a squared number having more than one solution (-22 and 22 are both 4) polynomials can quickly become very intricate, and even more so when dealing with full complex number segments. Many of the great discoveries in math, including complex numbers themselves, were based on trying to better understand polynomials. However, if a rule is imposed where the unit is always 10, and neither negative or imaginary numbers are allowed, you will get the positive rational numbers (3*102−7*101+4*100 is what we mean when we write 374). It may seem strange that one of the simplest things in mathematics becomes one of the most puzzling by just allowing negative numbers, but when you consider how these flip segments around the zero point, you can see how imaginary numbers (which flip the number line itself) are required to ensure any point can reach any point on a number line. All of these transforms lead to multiple solutions, which are all valid observable results in the real world, but also much more complex than simple positive scaling and appending.
[normal] Squaring is Repeating
[normal] What we know as squaring is a subset of the repeat operation, specifically repeating a stretch two times. Cubing is repeating a stretch three times. What else can be repeated? A shift (addition) can be repeated, so +34 is actually 3+3+3+3 (we normally see *34 as 3*3*3*3). This explains why we don’t differentiate between 3*4 being a continuous length of 12, 12 objects, a grid of 3x4 objects and an area of 3x4 square units. Stretching and shifting are different operations, but we don’t always distinguish them.
[normal] The other interesting thing about repeating is that we don’t have to limit repeats to just using the positive unit. We can repeat a negative stretch (-3*-3*-3*-3) causing the result to flip between positive and negative. We can also repeat with a negative unit, causing the cycling of 1, i, -1, -i. These are mirroring values and mirroring the number line. So while standard positive repetition creates a symmetry (ab ab), the addition of the two types of mirroring (ab ba, ɐq qɐ, and qɐ qɐ) allows the creation of all possible symmetries along a 1D line. Symmetry is the basis of much of modern math. It’s the only current system that can express all underlying patterns that are possible with repeated stretching and shifting of values.
[normal] What do complex numbers represent?
[normal] In general complex numbers are the balanced form of a number that is made of two opposing properties. This is different from positive and negative, as those measure the values that agree on what direction is ‘forward’. This may seem a minor distinction, but only because we are used to dealing with numbers that zero out the imaginary side (segments that start at zero). In fact, all measures are complex numbers of some form, because all measures are segments.
[normal] In electronics many values are already expressed with complex numbers, such as voltage/current, or the phase of an AC signal. In physics complex numbers are an essential part of the wave function, and used to represent various spins of particles. At higher scales, they allow the potential and kinetic energy of a system to be expressed as.a single value.
[normal] Credit and debt is often thought of in terms of positive and negative numbers, but in double entry accounting there are two accounts (credit and debit) and they always balance. Each account records the same thing, but has a different perspective as to what positive means. Another example would be exchanging money. If one bank gives 100:1 with no commission that would be a segment from 0 to 100. If another bank gives a rate of 105:1 but has a 5% commission, that would be a segment of 5 to 105. As you stretch these segments by whatever amount you wish to exchange, the return will be the same (the length of the segment), and the commission will be recorded by the start point.
[normal] There are many more examples of complex number use in math and science. Quantum spin, where a particle moves to one of two states when measured, is described with complex numbers. It has been shown in quantum mechanics that complex numbers are not just a convenient formalism, but a requirement. More generally, we see the pattern of two possible orientations along a gradient in more organic areas. In language we have ‘direction’ to ideas, like fast and slow, or good and bad. Applying very or not to these words amplifies or negates the direction, but keeps the ‘frame’ of the direction the same. Quadruples like fair-unfair-unbiased-biased, or combed-uncombed-tangled-untangled show the differences between a negation of a value, and a flipping of the context (number line) that happens when multiplying by i.
[normal] Ultimately, complex numbers represent all measurable values as they appear in the universe - aka numbers. Our current numbers represent part of a number, and we do most of our math with partial numbers.
[normal] Objects
[normal] Discrete ‘countable’ objects are always based on a definition, where traits are based on values. These definitions are made of probable ranges (focal segments) on specific traits. There can be many traits involved, and there can be multiple values on a single trait combined in specific ways. For example a zebra is black and white, whereas sheep can be black or white.
[normal] Trait positions always have an intrinsic value based on differences, even if there is no number or unit assigned to them. Abstractly, this is because there is a natural gradient of entropy in the universe, but in a more down to earth way, traits are properties that can be measured, and it isn’t possible to measure properties that don’t change.
[normal] The simplest example of things that can be counted are the counting numbers themselves. The definition of a number can be geometric, such as all numbers being represented by segments of equal length. It could also be defined in terms of discrete events, discrete objects, discrete locations etc. It is important to note that the definition of what we count doesn’t require a method of ordering. When seeing a bowl of oranges on the table we can count them, but there is no set order to them. There are also no fixed sizes, only probably ranges. Six oranges gives hints about the total weight, but there isn’t a simple multiplication by six that would be anything but approximate.
[normal] Much of math involves discrete objects, so it is important to understand exactly what this means. With traits and segment measures there is no ‘zero’, only values smaller than our ability to measure. With discrete objects there can be zero, as in you can not select an orange. This doesn’t imply that there can be infinity with discrete objects like it would with traits, as you can’t
[normal] stretch discrete objects. Stretching a zero would require infinite scaling to fit into a unit. With objects our only options are partitioning and adding (you can have half an apple with a cut, but even one apple twice the width of another apple isn’t considered to be two apples).
[normal] As mentioned, discrete objects have no set order, so the order must also come from a definition. This can be as simple as sorting by a trait in the definition, such as sorting apples by size, color, or sweetness. The order can also be something more complex algorithm based on multiple traits, or external events. They can also have multiple valid orders, or no order at all. How a set of discrete objects are ordered is important for many branches of math, as wide ranging as probability to calculus, to group theory.
[normal] Factorials
[normal] Factorials are written as 7!, which means 1x2x3x4x5x6x7. While they have many uses in math, a key idea they represent is the default scaling of complexity in a system as countable elements are added to it. This often implies order, but does not require it. For example if you give five people an apple each, there are 5! (120) ways those apples could be distributed. The order doesn’t matter, but those 120 permutations still exist. This is the default case, with no extra apples or people, and no conditions. There are ways to move this scaling into continuous values, but the concept remains the same.
[normal] This is probably the most universal ‘constant’ for scaling patterns, and is used as the ‘measuring stick’ for many dynamic systems that update themselves. Each update adds a default amount of complexity. The system must not only account for this, but also can use it to measure and direct itself. Factorials are used in probability and calculus, as well as in sequences of all sorts, such as calculating sine, cosine values, and Euler’s number.
[normal] The other thing to note about factorials is they get very big very fast - much faster than exponents for example. The complexity of the universe unfolding step by step ensures that even the strongest signals eventually get overwhelmed, fading over time, distance and noise. Math exploits this, allowing us to approximate some values to incredible precision - as the complexity of iteration increases, the relevance of each step decreases.
[normal] Grouping
[normal] Once multiple segments are selected or generated, they can be compared and categorized. Just as there are many orders that can be defined for a set of segments (order created, length, starting position…), there are also many groups. Grouping is a simpler operation than ordering, as it doesn’t require a definition to differentiate all elements in the set. In fact it isn’t even necessary to be able to distinguish individual elements. For example dividing segments by positive and negative pointing directions doesn’t require all segments to be unique, just their two possible pointing directions.
[normal] This is true with objects as well. The groups can be chosen from multiple traits, or even by whether or not they contain a trait. It is easy to see that while groups and objects are clearly discrete, there can always be ambiguity around the categorizations. A bucket for green apples and another for red can encounter apples that partially conform to both groups (a green apple starting to turn red), or nether (a yellow apple).
[normal] Groups are naturally discrete, however they can be defined for continuous properties, and they can be continuous themselves. For example, group assignment can be based on a probability of belonging to a category rather than a discrete yes or no.
[normal] Nesting
[normal] Groups can also contain groups. This is something we are used to in the real world, and it is also commonplace in math. When we say 3x4 (in the repeated addition sense, 4+4+4), we are creating a group of three things, that each contain a group of four things. This results in two levels of nesting: ((1111)+(1111)+(1111)).
[normal] This isn’t the case with stretching multiplication - exponents are the result of groups of groups with this operator. If we stretch a value by its value twice, say 3*3, this is squaring. If we do it three times (3*3*3) this is cubing. This is grouping a ‘stretch by value’ operation three times in a row, nested three deep. This results in three levels of nesting: (3*(3*(3))).
[normal] Nesting does not have to be uniform. Symmetry, polygons and groups (in the Group Theory sense) can be expressed in terms of nesting. For example we can see symmetries in nestings like ((1,2,3)(3,2,1)), and perhaps see a rectangle in the nesting ((1,2)(1,2)). We will cover this in more detail later, but keep in mind that nesting can be a great tool for inspection and comparison, as well as categorization.
[normal] Nesting and grouping of segments within a trait can use any of the  operations that exist in trait math. This means they can contain gaps, they can reference previously computed values, and they can iterate on previously derived values, or even values from separate traits. Doing all this is usually straightforward, however understanding what it implies isn’t always obvious - in spite of always being valuable.
[normal] Feedback
[normal] Grouping and nesting are ways of separating elements or actions into levels, but as alluded to, these levels can reference elements from previous levels. An expression with multiple levels describes operations that take place in steps. Each of these steps follow the normal expression pattern of ‘inspect, select, predict, simulate and evaluate’. As the steps are now separated we can see the loop is made of ordered steps, and these steps have access to the input and results of previous steps. A classic example of a feedback equation is the Fibonacci sequence, 1,1,2,3,5,8,13… where the next result is always the sum of the previous two results. One can imagine many ways of encoding this type of behavior with specific select and repeat operations.
[normal] It isn’t just algorithms that are based on feedback, in fact many important constants are derived with such feedback sequences. We are used to having pi, e, sine and cosine values just being available, but they ultimately have been calculated with a feedback mechanism. Euler’s number could be calculated by dividing a unit into N equal pieces and adding one of those to a unit (making say 1.01). When this total is repeatedly stretched by it’s result N times (so *1.01, then *1.201, then *1.030301… N times) you end up with approximately the ever useful Euler’s number: 2.71828… Notice how we not only needed to remember the previous result for the next operation, but we also needed to remember N.
[normal] Feedback allows some elements and operations to remain constant over time, some to vary, and some to change behavior. The symmetry of things come from what holds, and the variation comes from changing values and contexts. Looking at it this way, perhaps it isn’t surprising that so much of the world needs feedback for math to properly describe it.

[normal] Multiple Dimensions
[normal] The math so far shows all that can be done with a single trait, but the world naturally is made up of more than one trait. Many traits, even on the same object, are uncorrelated. The size of an apple in your hand is unrelated to its temperature, even though these are both traits we care about. This section will be about traits that are related to each other. Sometimes these are related in an obvious way, such as circumference and volume, and sometimes in a less obvious way, such as an apple’s color and its sweetness. In geometric terms we can think of these values as the predictability of one value given another, or how changing one value is likely to affect another. These segments are connected in some loose way, where one is like a partial unit of another. If the traits are unrelated, combining them together can result in an area or curve, both signs that the computation has escaped the one dimensional straight line.
[normal] A second way of looking at values moving beyond a single linear trait is when something impossible, it often ‘breaks into’ a new dimension. For example if a segment from 3 to 5 has a length of 7, it clearly can’t be a straight line, and thus needs a new dimension.
[normal] Even in one dimension, if pushing a start point by 1 unit per second results in an end point moving at three units per second, this can’t be a single segment. At very least there needs to be a second segment with a relation to the first that results in this speed difference. This second segment acts like a new dimension without being a new trait. The same applies to steps, if a segment doubles in length every step, then these steps act like extra dimensions, but in time rather than space.
[normal] All of these types of operations are expressed with powers, which at their heart represent repetition of some sort - multiple traits, segments, or actions. This similarity means we’ve already covered most of the math needed to operate on multiple trait dimensions. The one difference with multiple traits however, is that it’s never possible to create an observable area (or volume) on a single trait.
[normal] Two Dimensions
[normal] We will start by looking at two dimensions (two traits). Geometrically this allows curves and area, assuming the dimensions aren’t parallel and overlapping (which would mean they are fully correlated and thus can not create a difference required for area). It is worth pausing here and trying to establish exactly what curves and areas mean. The simplest area calculation is just squaring two perpendicular units together. This is the unit ‘squared’, which creates an area. Even the word ‘square’ itself already implies a 2D shape with four equal sides. And yet we have already been ‘squaring’ things in 1D (by repeating operations 2 times), so what are the differences here, and what are the similarities?
[normal] To understand the relation of 1D math to 2D, it is important to first remember there are no infinite values. This means that even though we can not see or measure the thickness of our abstract number line, it can not be infinitely thin. As with atoms, they are below normal resolution, but we can easily see objects composed of many of them. So as a thought experiment, imagine the number line’s thickness is the smallest possible value in our system (like the Planck’s constant of our number system). Take the unit segment, and repeat it on the number line by however many times that value fits into the unit. You will now have perhaps trillions of units on the number line, or perhaps only ten if you are drawing this all with a thick pencil. In any case, all of them together will have a certain ‘area’, even if it isn’t measurable. If you now make a second object using these lines stacked on top of each other rather than side by side. This will have the same area - the area of a square where each side is one unit, or 12.
[normal] The same works for grids of dots. Take a point at (0i,0) and apply (+1)x3 to make three more duplicates. This gives four dots, at 0,1,2,3. Now if you repeat that 3 times along the same trait, you get 12 dots all in a row. However, if you made three copies of the original four dots on a perpendicular trait, you would now have a 3x4 grid of dots – still twelve total, but in two dimensions.
[normal] With exponents, we can only see their true effect in one dimension with animation, and it appears as acceleration. With two dimensions, we can see it as an area, or a line that ‘accelerates’ away from the default linear path that its inertia would suggest (the tangent). So in a very real way, exponents are a way of perceiving multiple dimensions using time rather than properties. Multiple segments on a single trait can affect each other, but they can not escape the line. Because of this the effects are not noticeable with a single snapshot of a position, and only become evident with steps over time. With different traits, segments can still affect each other, but this can be seen in a snapshot, as they blend into each other ‘spatially’. Therefore the math is very similar, but the implications are quite different.
[normal] The last thing to notice with using full complex numbers with two dimensions, is that combining them will naturally graph areas as well as lines. Area graphing is very important in math, from simple shapes to areas under the curve in calculus, but we have very few natural tools for expressing or even displaying it. Most math at this point retreats to algebra or programming, but we lose a lot of insight when surrendering our intuitions to calculation. The real question is why does graphing in 2D not normally end up with areas? Areas and shapes are very normal occurrences in two dimensions, and their coordinates are easily represented on a grid. Area is the default plot for any 2D graph when using full complex numbers.
[normal] Correlation of two traits
[normal] Just as with segments on a single trait, segments on separate traits can be correlated. The new thing with a second trait is the traits themselves can be correlated. For example, the area of a square and the width of a square are fully correlated, but are separate traits. Traits can also be partially correlated, such as the area and length of a rectangle. And of course they can be uncorrelated, as with the lengths and widths of rectangles. The important part is to understand very deeply what these simple correlations imply, as that will reveal many insights in the resulting geometries.
[normal] When two traits (or two segments) are fully correlated, it means that a change in one results in a fully predictable change in the other. This can be thought of as maximum correlation (the opposite of uncorrelated) or minimum information (the same amount of information as uncorrelated). This distinction is very important, and follows a familiar pattern. The first question when looking at two traits is ‘does a correlation exist?’. If and only if that answer is yes, then the second question is ‘how correlated are the traits?’. Does this concept have hair? Yes. How long is the hair? Does this concept have a radius? No. And no second question.
[normal] This is the same pattern as requiring a selection before measuring or scaling which we see in addition vs multiplication. Scaling an object requires an object, whereas selecting an object (adding) does not. This is why exactly zero is a valid concept in addition, where zero in multiplication just means ‘scaled down beyond our ability to measure, but still there’. So correlation between traits is a two part question. First, is there correlation? And if so, second, how much correlation is there?
[normal] Correlation is a concept that is confusing because there isn’t an obvious ‘positive’ direction in the complex number sense. We naturally think of things like hotter, larger, faster, and happier as positive, so we naturally label cooler, smaller, slower and sadder as the imaginary direction. More correlation can be thought of as more predictable, or less informative, and it really depends on what we are hoping to do. If we are trying to predict an outcome, more correlation is better. If we are trying to gain information, less correlation is better (assuming the traits are connected at all). If I know someone’s name is John, I can predict he is male as that would have a very high correlation. If I was trying to guess the identity of someone with the first name a John, asking if he is male would be a low (but not zero) information question as there is a very high correlation between male and John. This is still a high risk high reward question though, because there are so many fewer women named John than men. A negative correlation question would be to ask if John is a woman. It is as highly correlated as asking if he is a man, just in the opposite direction. An uncorrelated question would be more like asking the time - the time of day is not correlated at all to John’s identity. This is an all risk no reward question, the equivalent of zero. And lastly, comparing something to itself is not highly correlated, but actually the same, and therefore not correlated at all. This is equivalent to ‘unit’ or one. Like stretching, correlation can be smaller or larger than we can measure, but we know it will never be zero or absolute as it requires one element to be compared to another separate element.
[normal] As with segments, it is useful to make an exhaustive list of all possible outcomes when comparing two traits that are correlated. These are not requirements for every comparison, just the limit of possible outcomes. First we can see that traits can be maximally or minimally correlated at the extremes, and there can be a gradient from one to the other. Ten fingers maximally correlate to humans, 13 fingers minimally. Next, traits can be highly correlated, but in opposite directions. Annual rainfall is very related to drought, but in a negative way – less rain, more drought, more rain less drought. Lastly, things can be highly correlated in the same direction, but disagree on the direction’s frame of reference. As we grow up, age and strength are correlated, and in our later years they are correlated again, but this time as age and weakness. Directions of traits are arbitrary, so this isn’t suggesting there is a universal ‘positive’ direction. It is just that there are always two perspectives on a gradient, so whatever directions you choose to two traits to be ‘aligned’ in, flipping the direction of one will cause them to be unaligned.
[normal] If this feels like the same as the possible relations of segments on a trait (which are described by complex numbers), that is because it is the same. There are four sections, positive, negative, and then positive and negative going the other direction. 1, i, -1, and -i. This is a very universal pattern with two measurable elements. It may feel like there are more patterns possible here, and there sort of are. Trait A can be forwards or backwards, and trait be can be forwards or backwards, so there should be four possible combinations. The caveat is that there is no ‘universal’ forward. In the abstract sense, both traits putting ‘north’ at the top is indistinguishable from both traits putting ‘south’ at the top. We can only say that whatever way we arrange them, we can create an arrangement that disagrees. That said, we often have known directions, like large and small, cold and hot, and this indeed results in four possible arrangements. This is valid, and there is plenty of math to be had here, but if you look closely this also requires a lot of information than two unspecified traits can supply. Universal correlation requires undistinguished traits without preferred orientations.
[normal] 2D Addition
[normal] Addition in two dimensions allows combining two trait segments without any stretching. Some of it looks like what we normally consider multiplication, but as we’ll see, multiplication by a non unit segment requires distortion along the axis being multiplied.
[normal] The simplest addition is a point added to a point. Addition along a trait is shifting, and this is the same in two dimensions. So a point at three in the X axis, shifted by 5 in the Y axis will end up at (3,5). A segment from 1 to 3 on the X axis will end up being a segment shifted to 5 in the same way. Lastly a segment from 1 to 3 added to a segment from 5 to 8 results in a rectangular area, starting from 1,5 on the bottom left, and filling to 3,8 on the top right. The key to 2D addition is understanding what mechanically is happening in these, and this becomes more obvious when a segment is added to a segment.
[normal] First, whenever we combine two elements A and B with an operation, A is the start point, and B is the transform. So +3+1 means we start with a point at three, and shift it by one. *4*2 means a segment from 0 to 4 is stretched to be 2 times longer. This is not a two way operation. It does not imply that a segment of length 2 is stretched by 4 times. It is important to remember we are transforming A by B. 2+3 is the same result as 3+2, but they are very different looking operations (start at 2, move 3 vs start at 3, move 2).
[normal] Note: In the full picture, the added value of the stretch is subtracted from empty space, so there is always a reciprocal operation happening (2 stretched by ¼, or with addition, an empty space shifted back to the original element’s space). We can ignore this for now.
[normal] In 2D addition, the first element will always be the initial condition. This will be the left side in our syntax convention, but not necessarily the X value on a graph (remember X could be heat and Y volume, and neither have an ‘X’ness). The second element will be the transform. So if we take these elements to be a segment+point, the result is a segment shifting by the points value. A point+segment results in a point being shifted by every ‘particle’ of the segment, resulting in a shifted segment. Lastly a segmentA+segmentB results in every particle of segmentA being shifted once by every particle of segmentB, resulting in an area. This area result is normally what we think of as A*B, but that is incorrect, or at least comes from our confusing repeated addition (3+3+3+3) with multiplication (3*4). If these were equivalent operations, we would have no way of differentiating a grid of points from a rectangle.
[normal] Transforms with addition work the same in 1D as 2D, even if the mechanics are less visually obvious. +3+4 along a line takes the point at 3 and shifts it 4 to the right, not the other way around. And logically, all the elements that are possible to create in 1D are legal elements to add in 2D. So a range of 4 points, say (+1)4, multiplied with a range of 5 points shifted by 3 ((+1)5+3) would result in an offset grid of 20 points. Those same four points added instead to a segment would result in four lines. 4 equally spaced segments added to 5 segments would result in a grid of rectangles. When adding segments in 1D, we add the start points, and add the end points, however this hides the many overlapping segments that were created in the process. The true result would be more like a smeared looking line, with the start and end points much lighter than the center. The total ‘area’ of all of those 1D segments would be the same as the area in 2D.
[normal] When adding repeated stretches together, the gradients become more visible. 24 is the unit doubled in size 4 times. If each of these segments is preserved, the segment from 0 to 2 will have four overlapping segments. 2 to 4 will have three, 4 to 8 will have two overlaps, and finally 8 to 16 will have one. On a line that is hard to see, though one could imagine it with repeated lines getting darker. If that was added to itself in two dimensions however, each of those layers has its own set of particles, and it would create a series of growing square areas, with the darkest in the bottom left. Obviously a smaller original value and a larger number of steps, say (1.522) the smoother that would be. For a perfectly smooth gradient you would want something like ((1+1/max)max), which once again is Euler’s number.
[normal] Addition is where every particle in the first element feels the ‘force’ of every particle of the second element, in a one-to-one manner. In each shift operation, there is never more than a source particle from A, and a shift particle from B. As we’ll see next, scaling (stretching) is where this shifting is calculated using a proportional weighting of the entire second element, resulting in very different transformations.
[normal] 2D Multiplication
[normal] By looking closer at the behavior of addition in 2D, we can see it is shifting in discrete or continuous steps. Multiplication is similar, where it is stretching in discrete or continuous steps. This will also become clearer when looking at it in 2D.
[normal] The first thing to see is that by stretching instead of shifting, the element being transformed can change its length as well as shift. The process is similar to addition, where every ‘particle’ of the source element, one by one, is affected by every particle of the end element. This creates a grid of outputs, which is why it can potentially fill an area. With stretching, the difference is we account for all points in a segment for each calculation. For example, if the first step is to transform start point A by start point B, we have to also ask how endpoint B will affect start point A. It is like all the points of B have a ‘gravitational’ effect on all the points of A. Of course the farther the point the less the effect, but these secondary effects do add up. Like with gravity, the ‘pull’ is more significant closer to the comparison than farther away, but the whole segment does have a pull. This pull of segment B is calculated for every particle of segment A.
[normal] This would normally be a very large calculation, however because these effects are all linear, we can just interpolate the end points. So we need to calculate how the source’s start point is affected by both the transformers start and end points, and likewise how its endpoint is affected by both of these points. The resulting four points form a shape that will be the outer bounds of the full interpolation.
[normal] We will try to develop an intuition for this, but it is helpful to know in advance that this ends up being complex number multiplication. If you consider two segments on the X and Y axis, and you represent those segments as complex numbers, then the resulting shape maps very naturally to the complex multiplication. It also results in an area, not a segment. Of course if the two traits are collinear, then only a segment is possible (overlapping lines can not create area). This is how complex numbers are multiplied in current math. Creating an area with operations on complex numbers may seem strange, but with normal multiplication we can make 2*3 equal 6, or a 2x3 rectangle. The only difference here is the first equation is for collinear segments, and the second is for perpendicular ones. There is much to say about partially aligned traits, as well as the ‘density’ of operations on collinear traits - more on this later. For now we’ll only be looking at perpendicular (minimally collinear) traits.
