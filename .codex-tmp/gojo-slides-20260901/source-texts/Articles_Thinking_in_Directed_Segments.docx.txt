[normal] Thinking in Directed Segments
[normal] Directed segments work very well for encoding concepts. They are very simple, a start point and an end point, which gives you a range with a direction. And they have a surprisingly intuitive mapping to the type of complex structures you find in grammar. I've talked about this a lot, but now that I’m getting further into it, I have, perhaps, some better examples.
[normal] In all languages, tenses can reference future, current or past events, and they can do this from the perspective of the future, current or past. They can use the event‘s point of view or the speakers. And they can focus on different aspects of the event; before, beginning, during, completion, or after — or contiguous combinations of those. Imagine eating a meal (I ate, I’m eating, I’m going to eat). The segment describes the duration of eating, and the calibration (where the basis 0->1 is placed) describes the temporal position of the event. This is [time : time], but you could imagine [distance : distance] ("there’s a very long bridge in 5 miles"), or any combination ("a 10 minute delay in 10 miles", "the stock went down last week" etc). [normal] There are many tenses available in English, but like all languages, there are many expressible tenses we don't have structures for. For these we just use extra words to describe the situation, but mathematically less common doesn't have to mean a more complex encoding.
[normal] The segment itself can be interpreted in different ways, due to the fact that it's made up of two zero anchored segments (zero to a start point, and zero to an end point). For example, the segment could be the event, it could be a description of a partially completed event, or it could two contiguous events. These interpretations determine the ways you can meaningfully combine segments, so it is important to codify this intent. Units as mathematical objects can help clarify this, as I talked about elsewhere.
[normal] Note that the direction of the arrow is also relevant. This polarity is lost in regular numbers (other than positive and negative), but it is common in both the real world and the math we use to describe it.  In fact the core of measurement, the basis itself, is a directed segment from 0->1. Here you can see directional encodings for gaining versus losing, a lot versus a little, as well as before versus after.
[normal] The basis unit has positive and negative aspects (and mirrored, but we'll save that for later). "The number goes up", means one thing for wealth, but another for debt. Most measure words in a language have this duality; either as a separate word (height/depth, strong/weak, calm/agitated), or with the prefix 'un' (happy/unhappy, fair/unfair). And as discussed in other posts, the start point of a segment is actually an inverted unit located at (-1->0), or (1->0). This gives us the 'i' in complex numbers.
[normal] This isn’t just a way to solve temporal encodings in language. Segments simplify encoding transforms from an initial state to a goal state (a ‘verb’), allow qualifying and comparing things (adjectives/adverbs), as well as categorizing objects (nouns). Even minor things like prepositions fit intuitively into segments. A segment has up to 7 distinct areas of interest. The focus can cover multiple contiguous positions, inclusive or exclusively, and involve multiple segments. This allows for a lot of combinations, and the human mind has evolved to make any such setups intuitive. Here are some examples of changing the focus to derive prepositions. This focus itself can of course be defined with a segment. And importantly, all these segments and focal areas can be trained for AI with things like attention mechanisms.
[normal] As you can see, there are may ways to interpret a segment. There also many ways to combine segments, whether through addition, multiplication, repetition, merges, and other operations. Yes — many ways to add, many ways to multiply. This may sound absurd, but that is actually pretty normal in math. Look at all the Boolean operations, there are 16 of them. Even in our regular number system there are different meanings for multiply. And in fact there are other 'intuitive' ways of multiplying that are considered wrong.
