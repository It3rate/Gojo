[normal] Segment Math and Symmetry
[normal] Symmetric tiling patterns are both deeply compelling, and mathematically unsatisfying. They have been used throughout the history of civilization, from the 12000 year old Göbekli Tepe onwards. These symmetry patterns hold deeper insights into how we calculate, how we think, and indeed how the world works.
[normal] Frieze patterns are a type of line group, with patterns that repeat only in one direction. These have been mathematically described with many rule sets and notations, and personally I’ve found them all a bit arbitrary. They all enumerate the patterns, but it feels more like classification than insight. Caveat, maybe I’m just too thick to appreciate it. And no doubt I’m adding one more rule set to the pile here. Apologies.
[normal] My take is these patterns are not base primitives to build on, but a visual embodiment of the rules needed to compare ordered things.
[normal] The segment number system I’ve been working on for the last five years (yikes!) make symmetry much more intuitive, as the four basic transformations (identity, negate value, negate number line, and negate both) are the same transformations used to construct segment numbers themselves.
[normal] TLDR, in the table below, each of the dots has an associated action that means the same as the encoding it represents. Notice how the seven dot patterns form 7 distinct shapes: nothing, point, horizontal, vertical, diagonal, corner, and everything. This is not a coincidence. With horizontal and vertical transforms, all 16 possible four bit bool patterns can be created. Finally, extending these binary actions to be ‘weighted’ creates various number systems – fundamentally a comparison using a basis and a value.

[Heading 1] The Four Units of a Measure
[normal] To start, let’s have a first-principles review of a line. A line can represent anything ordered. A line has two directions, and anything moving along it can move with or against those directions. For example, a line going up measures height, and a line going down measures depth. Positive numbers are either up or down, depending on the context (positive height is up, positive depth is down). Of course the same is true for horizontal lines, you can be travelling east, or travelling west. And you can be moving in those directions with the car in forward or reverse. Therefore, values on lines have four natural orientations.
[normal] We can’t just use arrows to indicate direction here, because travelling east → would be the same arrow as travelling west in reverse →.
[normal] Instead, imagine sitting on a cart with a swivel chair. You are facing the direction you want to go (east), and pointing in the direction of travel (east) with your left hand. So your arm is on the north side, pointing east (as in the top right image). There are four things that can happen here:
[normal] ⥬ Nothing happens at all, leaving your arm on the north side pointing east (identity, top right).
[normal] ⥪ The cart reverses, so your arm is still on the north side, but pointing west (negate progress, top left)
[normal] ⥭ You swivel around to face backwards, the cart keeps going. Now your arm is on the south side, still pointing east (negate goal, bottom right)
[normal] ⥫ You turn around and the cart reverses, putting your arm on the south side, pointing west (negate both, bottom left).
[normal] As you can see, there are four possible units here, combining forwards and backwards as well as east or west. The direction on the line, and the direction of the line.
[normal] And it isn’t just here, all measured values have these four perspectives, and not surprisingly, all symmetry is based on them as well. This is because lengths can only be measured relative to other lengths, and every length is created from two positions, totalling four measures. You can think of these as the four units used in every comparison, because, well, that’s what they are.
[Heading 1] The Meaning of Units
[normal] Now what do each of these mean? Our regular numbers only use the ‘east encoding’ (values increase to the right). This requires a value to measure, and a unit. For our numbers, the start point of both the value and unit is at zero, and the unit defines ‘1’ regardless of its length. 7 is 7 * 1 unit. This is how we can compress the four values into one.
[normal] It is easy to imagine an identical system that is ‘west encoded’. If you transform things horizontally and vertically, everything flips and now points west. This is similar to viewing it from the other side of the table, or rotating everything by 180 degrees.
[normal] It is important to understand that both of these perspectives are equal, nature doesn’t prefer right over left. Even though what is called the ‘negative unit’ here seems like it has a value of -1, it is a unit of value 1, just like all other units. A unit means ‘one of a certain thing’, like 1 inch, 1 degree, or 1 distance in the leftward direction. This is why we call them units — they don’t represent values, they represent context.
[normal] Notice also a positive unit can indicate a negative value, it just means the value grows in the opposite direction as the unit. -7 is 7 unit lengths in the opposite direction of the unit.
[normal] Back to the cart. Now we can ask, what would have to change to move the hand from pointing forwards to pointing backwards? This is ‘negating’ the gear you’re in, which negates the direction of progress. Changing from forward to reverse. And if already going in reverse, the same negating operation would change to forward. You are changing your direction on the line. This is multiplying by -1.
[normal] What would it take to get your arm to be on the south side of the car if it is currently on the north? In this case, the direction you are moving remains the same, but you negate the direction you are facing by swiveling your seat. If you were going forward facing east, you now have to face west. Forward was east, now forward is west. Even though you are moving in the same absolute direction (east), you have changed the direction of the line, as you are now going backwards. Numbers now get smaller going east. We don’t have a term for this, I’ll call it changing the polarity of the line. This is multiplying by -i.
[normal] So there are two operations here, negate your progress, and negate your goal. I promise this leads to symmetry! Negating progress is what happens when multiplying by -1, it negates a value. Negating the goal (toggling the polarity of the line) happens when multiplying by -i.
[normal] If we do both, we switch from moving east in forward to moving west in forward. We could call this -1*-i, but this double flipping causes the unit to be ‘upside up’ again, so it is no longer negative. This leaves 1i, or just i. It seems like a 180 degree rotation, but it is specifically a double flip, not a rotation.
[normal] So these are the base units for all measures, and all symmetry, 1,-1, -i and i. Both -1 and i will negate a value, and both -i and i will negate the polarity of the line. Note the choice of putting 1 in the top right corner is arbitrary. It is done here to map to our familiar numbers, with values increasing to the right. Any transformation of this cube is a valid orientation. Going west is just as valid as going east, positive up Y axis is just as valid and positive down. And, as much as it pains be to admit, driving on the left is just as valid as driving on the right. You can choose any orientation, as long as you’re consistent. I chose this one as it maps cleanly to our number system, and complex numbers.
[Heading 1] Encoding as Memory, Prediction and Action
[normal] Stepping back, notice how a number records a value. This value store can be used to recall or predict a value in a similar situation. If eggs are $5.00, then next time you see them you can predict they’ll be $5.00. Less obvious, but it does this by recording a ‘recipe’ that can be used to create that value from a unit. Numbers are actually very simple mini equations. For example the number 7 is the unit stretched away from zero, to end up 7 times the original unit length. In the same way, -7 is the unit stretched in the opposite direction, so it stretches to the left.
[normal] There is a subtle but important distinction when you multiply by a unit instead of a value. These will toggle the unit portion only. Multiplying by -i will flip the unit vertically (in reference to the diagram above), so a bottom unit will become a top one, and vice versa (-i→1 or i→-1). Multiplying by the unit -1 will flip the unit horizontally, where -1→1, or i→-1, and vice versa. And multiplying by i will flip things diagonally. Our math conflates units and values so this is confusing at first. Essentially we need four ‘yard’ units, one for each direction on a yardstick, and then the same thing for a yardstick where the values increase to the left. With this in mind, you should be able to convince yourself that transforms can change the units as easily as the values, and that they work as described above.
[normal] These transforms can of course be applied to things other than the unit. If you apply 7 to 3 instead of the unit 1, it stretches the length of 3 to 7 times its length (to 21) instead of stretching the unit. In the same way, if you multiply (2i+3) by (0i+7) you get (14i+21), just you are stretching two point segments instead of single numbers.
[normal] It doesn’t stop at numbers either. If you apply -1 to the letter ‘d’ you end up with the letter ‘b’, a flip across a zero point, just like negation of numbers. If you apply -i to ‘d’, you flip the polarity and end up with ‘q’. And multiplying ‘d’ by i, you get ‘p’, which looks like a rotated ‘d’. We can therefore create these four symmetries just using simple multiplication of units. This will make it much easier to tease apart and interpret the symmetries mathematically.
[Heading 1] Action
[normal] We’ll use a grid of four dots (bits) to represent the units of transform. Having them in a square rather than a single row allows them to visually line up with their actions (again, this is not a coincidence). The top row is bit 2 (negate value), bit 1 (identity), and the bottom row bit 4 (invert), bit 3 (negate line direction). The order is TR (1), TL (2), BR (4), BL(8).
[Heading 1] The Seven Symmetries
[Heading 2] No Transform
[normal] If we do nothing, whatever pattern we input will just repeat. A small point, but if a shape has a natural orientation like a heart ❤︎, the ‘do nothing’ action doesn’t correct a sideways heart ❥ to be normal. This literally means, ‘do nothing’.
[Heading 2] Point Transform
[normal] With a point, or single bit set, the input pattern will transform the same way every time. It is important to note here, that the input is always the output of the previous step. This is what causes the alternation. There are four of these patterns. The identity pattern doesn’t alternate, and initially seems similar to the first ‘do nothing’ pattern. Subtly though, it means ‘add the pattern untransformed’ rather than ‘add the pattern as is’. It is applying an action each time, just in these symmetries the input is always considered ‘true’, so it’s resetting things that don’t need to be reset.
[Heading 2] Horizontal and Vertical Transforms
[normal] Next is the horizontal and vertical lines. With two bits, they will always have two transforms. In the stripe patterns, we always have the identity copy, and then a horizontal or vertical flip, establishing the initial shape. This shape then gets re-input into the algorithm, causing this copy to get flipped in the same way, leaving the same shape. Flipping a ❤︎ left to right leaves a ❤︎. Flipping a ❥ up and down leaves a ❥.
[Heading 2] Diagonal Transform
[normal] The diagonal line is similar, other than the pattern mirrors. This is like creating an S, and then flipping it both horizontally and vertically and ending with S. Note this is not a rotation, it is a double inversion. It looks like a 180 degree rotation, but shapes can’t rotate in one dimension. Note to create a diagonal from top left to bottom right, you would just need to add whitespace to your initial pattern (by moving the vertical line that defines the ‘zero’ point of the left/right flip).
[Heading 2] Corner Transform
[normal] The corner pattern is the trickiest, as it creates a contradiction. It says, keep the initial shape and flip left/right, then also mirror that. This fills every quadrant, full symmetry. However, the missing dot in the bottom right says ‘there is no up and down symmetry’, which we’ve already created. This is an impossible request, at least within our constraints so far.
[normal] A missing dot is a ‘MUST NOT’ in logical terms, and is as much of a requirement as the ‘MUST’ actions. To satisfy the NOT condition, we must break the up/down symmetry. The ‘breaking’ can be done by shifting to the right (or left). Technically you could also rotate, shift upwards, or expand the bottom to break symmetry. However, these operations are not available using only units, in only one dimension.
[normal] The corner operation looks like two steps, however the symmetry breaking is just applying the NOT action in the same step. The key truth here is that any three dot pattern will create a contradiction, and only three dot patterns create a contradiction. It’s like someone asking you to put on both of your shoes, but make sure you aren’t wearing both shoes. Something has to give, and a ‘translation horizontally in time’ is ‘that something’ (put on your left shoe, take it off, put your right shoe, take it off, etc.). Notice how all the other dot patterns are linear in some way, while corners ‘break’ symmetry. Like in math, design and metaphor, corners are the entry point to new dimensions.
[Heading 2] Transform All
[normal] Last is the ALL pattern. This is self explanatory, however note you can imagine a lot of overlapping duplicates or repeated instructions present here. This is specifically ok, because there are no ‘MUST NOT’ instructions forbidding them. It is saying put on both shoes, also put on your left shoe, also put on your right shoe.
[normal] Here again are all the symmetries together.
[Heading 1] Why the Filter?
[normal] There are naturally 16 patterns with 4 bits, yet with Frieze symmetry there are only seven. On the surface, this filtering is caused by the repetition moving in a single direction, when there are in fact four cardinal directions encoded in the bits. You can see the filtered patterns map to the primitive patterns, albeit with some nudging and squinting. At a visual level, these are due to logical rules. Applying the same transform action twice will always lead back to the original state, making the result visually identical (if not the process). Add to that, a pattern tiling vertically may be similar to a different pattern tiling horizontally, and both are valid. But at its core, these symmetries arise from the same logical truths required to compare and create values. Symmetry presents the results of transforms, hiding the process, so it describes relations of the transforms, not the transforms themselves.
[Heading 1] The primitives of numeracy
[normal] Logic requires that any measurement is a comparison (there is no universal yardstick), and all numbers represent measurements of some form. Every comparison must use two ranges, one to calibrate (the basis) and the one to be compared (the value). This requires two ranges, denoted by four positions. Symmetry is a rule set that exposes these truths visually, with no assumptions about starting points or units. Symmetries aren’t core truths, they are a way of examining the relationships of core truths. Line symmetry has 7 primitive patterns and 16 total patterns It’s useful to examine what the filtered sets tell us, and why they naturally fall into existing groups.
[Heading 1] A Look at the Redundant Patterns
[Heading 2] Points
[normal] First we’ll look at the single points. Here is the bottom right (up/down flip), and you can see that with shifting it turns into the up/down symmetry pattern. This shifting is actually just shifting the pivot point (the zero point). This is always a choice, as there are no universal reference points to measure things from. You can see if tiling vertically you can come to a similar conclusion.
[normal] That said, all the four one-dot patterns represent repeatedly applying a single transform. The fact that they map to other patterns isn’t a coincidence, but this isn’t the core concept of a ‘point action’. The point action is about making a selection, choosing an orientation, or making a change. One. The repetition comes from the fact that we are tiling, not from the action the dot represents.
[Heading 2] Stripes and Diagonals
[normal] Two-dot patterns expose the behavior of symmetry pairs. There are six of these. The three that include identity: vertical, horizontal and diagonal. These three are primitive shapes in the seven listed above. The other diagonal (\) has no overlapping instructions, no identity, so it is the equivalent of a ‘both’ transform. The last two (left stripe, bottom stripe) alternate because they create ‘double instructions’ (flip and flip both), meaning the output will be a flipped version of the input.
[normal] Here is the alternation of the left stripe. This is a horizontal transform of the template, which is then inverted. This creates the \ diagonal, and when that output is reinput and transformed, it ends up the opposite / diagonal. While this looks similar to a contradiction (corner), it is actually alternating input – there is no forced break of symmetry.
[normal] Again, the idea that this is equivalent to a corner pattern is significant, but this isn’t the core symmetrical meaning. A useful analogy is to think of a toy car on a piece of track. It can go backwards or forwards in either direction, and when it gets to the end it can ‘bounce’ and drive back (alternate), or it can restart from the beginning (repeat). When the output is fed back into the transform pattern, either outcome is possible. Two-bit patterns are all about the symmetries of two combined transforms and the patterns they create, primitive or otherwise.
[Heading 2] Corners
[normal] Three-dot patterns are about contradictions. A corner that ‘bends’ on the bottom right (BR in the diagram) flips up/down instead of side to side. This is ‘glide reflection’ in other symmetry notation, and is considered the same pattern as the original TL corner. Glide reflection doesn’t discriminate between the type of reflection used, as these actions are already defined with two-dot symmetry. With three actions, the order of operations also changes the pattern. It may be easier to visualize the pattern similarities when tiled vertically, but ultimately the look of the pattern is just the hint.
[normal] The thing that defines corner symmetry is the contradiction of instructions, which forces the pattern to break into a new dimension.
[normal] Symmetry is focused on results, not process, so many of the results cancel each other. But that is not a dis! Prediction also focuses on results rather than the process, and with better-than-random prediction, you have the basis of intelligence. It is reasonable to think about these seven filtered patterns as building blocks of understanding.
[Heading 1] Symmetry as Action
[normal] This will be fleshed out in another post, but as you can see below, these symmetry primitives can also map to an instruction set for images and code. In the segment number system, the inverted line (positive left, bottom dots) represent the initial state, and the normal line (positive right, top dots) represents the goal state. This naturally leads to move-to, line-to, conditionals, branches, transforms, and transfer and return of control. Adding weights to these brings up a full system computation. Encoding in this system is not just interpretable, it’s executable.
