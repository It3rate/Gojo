[Heading 1] Numbers from first principles
[normal] To construct numbers from first principles, you must first imagine a world without numbers, without values, and without references. This is hard, so it’s good to have a few models to work from, models that get you from nothing to something. One option is to imagine a universe with nothing in it at all, totally empty with no previous knowledge. No size, no color, no time, no naked ladies. Start from literally nothing.
[normal] Another option is you can model limited information, perhaps a sensor that you haven’t yet read, a bacteria without perception, or some imagined thing I haven’t told you about. However you model it, the model is unable to make assumptions beyond the information it has –  it exists isolated from the rest of the world, so comparisons to known references aren’t possible.
[normal] As more information comes in, it gives our model new ways to describe, define and manipulate what it already knows. Each new observation, no matter how seemingly trivial, is a tool that can be used for math. And as I hope to convince you, while we need at least four pieces of information to create numbers, there is a lot of math before and after that point we are ignoring.
[Heading 2] Zero to One
[normal] So we have empty space, an unread sensor, or someone’s secret. We have no information. Note that this isn’t ‘zero’, it’s the absence of information. It is null rather than zero. Now if we imagine the same system with one piece of information, what can we say about it? We of course can’t give it a size, a length or a color, as there is only one value and we don’t know anything about it without bringing in more information. You don’t even know what property is being described, never mind what object it belongs too. It could be a grain of sand or it could be a planet, and it could describe the size or prescience of kryptonite. We can’t even really call it zero, as that implies the potential to scale, or be compared to another value that isn’t zero. We CAN say there is something rather than nothing, and not much else – though to be fair that is a pretty big step.
[Heading 2] One to Two
[normal] Now imagine two pieces of information. Note this is not a new piece of information appearing, as that would imply ordering, which implies change over time, and we haven’t derived either of those concepts yet. It's just two pieces of information.
[normal] Let's dig into the implications of this. If I hand you a paper with dots, each dot is a piece of information. The number of dots I made and the amount of information on the page are not necessarily the same though. I may have put 100 dots in the same spot and you'll see one, or I may have made them all line up and you'll only see a line. If you stand further away you may see less dots than if you're close. THIS ISN'T TRIVIA. The system is the system you perceive or imagine. The freedom is you can imagine things however you like, not that you can misinterpret what you imagine.
[Heading 3] Resolution
[normal] A piece of information (a value of some property) depends on separating it from other values. If you glance at a thermometer for 5 seconds, you will get one, or hundreds, or millions of readings, depending where you draw the line between your axons and your brain, and how you define ‘a reading’. This means the resolution of the system matters when encoding values, real or imagined. It doesn't mean you can't have two identical numbers, just that they need to be separated somehow. Two values can be separated by time, in a table, or I can take your word for it that one dot is actually two. But in a system where there are only two pieces of information, you need to be able to separate them somehow so they are ‘not equal’, otherwise you only have one piece of information. Going backwards now, we can say a single piece of information has a difference of zero, because we don't actually know if it’s a single value, or many identical values that are ‘equal’. It is a ‘distance’ of zero, at the resolution we know it by. Separate things are not equal, but they may appear equal at a different resolution. Every value in the history of the universe comes with a resolution, observed or not, ignored or otherwise.
[Heading 3] Direction
[normal] While we don’t know the order of the samples we have, we do know for sure there is a separation between them. This means they have two possible orderings, A to B, and B to A. This is NOT a positive and negative direction, it is a length of ‘n’ from A to B, and the exact same length of ‘n’ from B to A. This is akin to the Y axis point up and the Y axis pointing down. Either one of these orientations can have negative changes (when values move in the opposite direction of the orientation), but there are two orientations, not one. This is a crucial point to internalize, not because it is complicated, but because our math doesn’t use it and it’s important. Essentially here we are choosing a direction for greater, and the opposite direction is less, which only requires two points.
[Heading 3] Length?
[normal] You would think that with two samples we would have a length, but we don’t. In the empty space model, we can have two positions and still have no idea how far apart they are. They might be the distance between two fingers, or the distance between two stars, and without fingers or stars there is no way of telling. If you imagine a line, I have no idea how long it is without more information, even though I know it has two endpoints. In fact all measures, real or imagined, are relative to other measures – the universe doesn’t come with a universal yardstick.
[normal] While we certainly can’t quantify the distance between our two samples, we do know it has a length, and we therefore can attempt to extrapolate and subdivide it. We can take two readings from a sensor, and imagine this growing and growing until it is as large as we care to imagine, or subdividing it into smaller and smaller pieces until it is way smaller than we can possibly work with. We also can imagine positions twice as long or half as long. A few caveats though. Just because we can imagine it doesn’t at all mean it represents real information. Math operations are a bit like predictions based on starting values, which might be useful. These predictions also do NOT imply things can be infinitely large or small. In fact you can’t actually imagine infinity even though the ‘not stopping’ algorithm is trivial. However, you can imagine that you can imagine infinity, and you can still ‘do math’ on abstract concepts like this. Lastly, can’t imagine an extrapolated position somewhere along A and B without coming into possession of a third value, so let’s segway to…
[Heading 2] Three Pieces of Information
[normal] Three values allows operations like greater than, less than and in between. We can use two values to define an orientation, and then decide where the third point lies in regard to this segment. We could already measure ‘equal’ with a single piece of information as we can’t separate any further values that may or may not be there. With two values we understand ‘not equal’, because a second distinguishable value is by definition not equal. In quantifiable systems where all measures have to be relative to something else, comparisons are the cornerstone — and three values give us our first glimpse of this.
[normal] Let’s enumerate everything we can say with three values (not three numbers to be clear, think three levels of light). We take any two points and extrapolate them. Then the third point lies somewhere along this extrapolation. Again, this may not actually be ‘true’, I can extrapolate my age to 500, but that doesn’t mean I’ll live to 500. The units also may not match, our values might be temperature, distance and time. But our information is more like ‘this much’, ‘that much’, and ‘that other much’, no units in sight, and this is all we need to do math with.
[normal] Now in orientation A to B, C can be less than A, less than B, greater than A or greater than B. And then these values reverse in orientation B to A, and change again with A to C, C to B etc. This gives us 6 orientations, and the third value has two comparisons available. In English, this is simplified with prepositions like before/during/after, or behind/between/ahead. Language will often assume an orientation (higher is larger) but can usually flip it with context (deeper is larger). Math assumes a context and encodes it in the culture, leading to many arguments about things like the correct positive direction of the Y axis, and yet surprisingly little thinking about it.
[normal] We can also, sort of, create a number with three values. We can say A to C is three times longer than A to B, and that can represent the number 3. The problem is we can’t do that without using A to B as well as A to C, which is using the A value twice. This is like saying there are two A values and they are equal. So really that’s four values, not three. Oh, Mr. Segway man…
[Heading 2] Four Values – and Numbers
[normal] Every number is made up of (at least) four values. If you see two sticks on the ground, you can say one is about three times as long as the other. This is the number 3, but notice how it is made up of four values - the start and end of one stick, and the start and end of the other. We are comparing lengths, so we don’t care about orientation here, but certainly there is a way of thinking about two sticks where one is -3 times longer than the other. We would have to move them into a line (mentally or physically) and call the place where the ends met the start point. As they now point in opposite directions, one is -3 time longer than the other. Even when doing a normal comparison, we are lining them up somehow, as it is complex to include the ‘here to stick’ part in your calculations.
[normal] Note that when we line up two sticks, we end up with two values in the same place. This doesn’t mean we can’t distinguish them like back in the one peice of information case. In that case we had one piece of information, in this case we have two. If I make ten dots on a paper, all one on top of the other, it can be either one dot or ten dots to you, depending on what you know. We do math on information, not on physical reality.
[normal] So numbers. What we are really doing when creating a number is taking a reference ‘unit’ (a distance, a duration, a standard, or some other knowable interval of some precision) and using it to estimate how a second known range differs from it. Five inches, 40 degrees, twice the returns, half as good, partly true, lost it all – these all imply ‘numbers’, because they have a unit made of two values, and an implied two value range being compared. Notice how ‘more than a mile’, ‘a color range’ and ‘same as it ever was’ only require 3, 2 and 1 values, conceptually. Things can be measured without numbers, but the operations are more limited.
[normal] So really really, we are saying that all numbers have a numerator (the thing your number represents) and a denominator (the unit). In our numbers, both the numerator and denominator are represented by single numbers, but that is only because they both start at zero, and they both assume ‘positive right’. And even though we treat a segment from 0 to 3 the same as a point at 3, these are very different things, obviously. A point at 3 is really a value that starts at 3 and ends at 3, somewhat similar to zero, which starts at zero and ends at zero. It can’t actually have zero length, but it has a length smaller than we can use, or smaller than we care to use, just like zero. Remember back at the start, with empty space vs a thing we know nothing about. The point at 3 exists, and is very different than nothing at all. The point at 3 is also different than the point at 2 or the point at 0, showing there is something to it rather than nothing to it.
[Heading 2] Our Numbers
[normal] We now have all the primitives to define exactly what our numbers are made of, and what constraints they work under. This may seem trivial, but with an accurate model we will be able to understand the mechanics of the operations we do on them. With this we will find some separate operations are considered the same, and some very basic operations are missing. We will also be able to relax and expand these constraints to create new number systems, so familiar, and some fairly alien, but all useful.
[normal] Our numbers are created with two segments, a unit, and a value that is some scaled version of that unit. Each segment starts at zero, and the unit segment always ends at one (it defines 1). The positive direction is always considered to be rightwards in a numberline sense, but regardless of visualization, there is only one positive direction. Our numbers move towards the negative in the opposite direction of this. Any value segment that isn’t a whole multiple of the unit is some form of a partial number, fraction or decimal.
[normal] While these do require four separate values, the unit segments zero and one are definitional, the zero on the value segment is required, leaving only the end point of the value segment dynamic. This makes it very useful, as one free parameter is far easier to reason about than four. Note that this comes at a price — values we use in our math will often need to be adjusted to fit this system. If we find two sticks in the forest, it’s unlikely that they both share a common zero point and share a direction like this. Generally though, it is worth paying this price, and we will always prefer one free parameter when possible, even with multiple units (3 meters per second vs 6 meters per 2 seconds).
[normal] Note that this doesn’t consider the base of the number yet, or deal with rational vs real numbers, as these are all subcategories of these basic rules. A base 10 decimal or a base 12 fraction both have a unit and a value made of segments that are anchored at zero, and all operations on them are mechanically the same.
[normal] Also this does not cover the point at 8 vs a segment from 0->8, in spite of these being a very intuitive version of numbers to us. These actually require 8 values, and relaxing some of these constraints (essentially similar to what we know as complex numbers, which we will get to).
[Heading 3] Operations on Numbers
[normal] Values only become information when they relate to other values in non-random ways. This makes predictable operations the backbone of information. Addition and multiplication are some of the most basic arithmetic operations, and we need to elevate other things like ‘group those values’, ‘reference a calculation’ or ‘make a copy’ to operation status, rather than assume we have these abilities and everyone will understand what we mean.
[normal] However one chooses to define the mechanics of these, they really need to be explicitly defined with no assumptions, as the model we choose will inform how things behave as we scale and transform these values into more advanced numerical concepts. It is kind of surprising this isn’t done in math already regarding these basic concepts, but here we are.
[normal] Addition is grouping. Here is a question: with 2+3=5, are the 2 and 3 values ‘destroyed’ and you create a new ‘5’ value, or are they preserved and we now treat them together? In terms of apples, it seems the summed apples remain intact. In terms of segments, our intuition might be that we join the two value segments tip to tail into a new segment that stretches from 0->5. In terms of weight, we are just placing values together on a scale. All of these are fine — they encode different operations but have some fundamental similarities. For apples, to start with we need a definition of an apple, and some kind of container, mental or physical. For lengths, think of the rules you need for ‘tip to tail’, and the merging step. Is 2 merging with 3 or vice versa? For weights, we need the idea that all the values are considered together as one value.
[normal] The simplest version is we are now considering multiple values together, and nothing changed other than that. No need for moving things, sorting them, merging them etc. We are really just changing our categorization, not modifying the objects we are categorizing. Just five apples in one bag instead of two, same apples. Addition is grouping of values, the total is just a computable side effect of that. If we want to move forward with the number 5 we can just make a copy or reference of this calculation, but we haven’t altered or destroyed the 2 and 3.
[normal] Multiplication is scaling. 2x3=6 takes some initial value and expands it by a certain amount. It is the 2 getting scaled here, as in, if the 2 is red and the 3 is blue, the resulting 6 will still be red. The x3 is just ‘instruction’, the 2 is what is transforming. Note that while we are ‘scaling’ the 2, it doesn’t need to be a transform, we could just be moving closer or changing our measuring unit. This ratio scaling is in fact very related to how numbers themselves work, the unit segment is compared with the value segment creating a number. Notice how these operations can be a fact (A is 3x bigger than B), a question (how much bigger is B than A), an action (make A 3x larger), or an ‘action question’ (how do I make it 3x larger?). These four interpretations also hold true for addition, and in fact for all operations (and in fact for all information!). Are there other basic ways of using information?
[normal] So we can select and reference values, measure them, group them, and compare amounts and ratios. We can choose our frame (what change is considered ‘bigger’, what is our unit, at what resolution, what is our start point), and what operations we want to do. This gets us numbers and basic arithmetic.
[normal] All this may seem obvious or too trivial to be meaningful, but it does have some serious implications right off the bat. For one, the answer to 2+3 is really just 2 and 3 as a set, the ‘weighing’ them is just an observation. 2+3 is not the same as 1+4 unless you erase information which would require some erase operation. The Fibonacci sequence relies on not erasing this source information for example. But more fundamentally, 2+2+2 is a very different operation and result than 2x3 - one is a bag of 6 apples, the other is a big apple becoming much bigger or closer. We are rightly obsessed with totals because a single dynamic value is easier to interpret than many dynamic values. But as we relax various constraints and new math emerges, the differences between these ‘operational equalities’ will start to matter.
[Heading 3] Decimals and Fractions
[normal] The way we encode numbers is a big deal to us, but it actually has very little effect on the mechanics of mathematics. Whether ticks, decimals, fractions, hexadecimal, Roman numerals, or the Babylonian sexagesimal system, at their heart they work the same. Scaling is still scaling, grouping is still grouping, and the numbers that emerge come from comparing a unit to a value. The reason we have number systems at all is they help us with both standardization and variable resolution. This helps keep numbers interpretable as we communicate them more broadly, and helps us resume complex value groups into categories, as we do with object definitions.
[normal] To this point we are creating a number using the ratio of a value segment to a unit segment. This unit segment is arbitrary, it might be the length of a stick, the smallest size we can measure, or a perfect  value we imagine. I could measure my yard using 50 steps, and it would be good enough. If you want more precision, you change units, using feet or mm. The problem comes later when you want to change precision using the same unit. If you get a more accurate version of a step, you need a new unit for it - you can’t say 50 ½ steps, it needs to be 101 ‘something elses’. A system that allows partial values allows you to change the accuracy of a unit without making all your other measures invalid. A fraction system doesn’t obviate the need for multiple units to describe things of different scales (this also helps our intuition), but it does give the units we use a lot more latitude.
[normal] The way all numbering systems do this is by dividing the unit into parts (ticks if you will), and then appending this to the unit measure for more precision. A good way to understand the utility and limits of this is to look at five star reviews online. One review is divided into 5 possible ticks, 2 reviews have 10 possible ticks. If you get one 5 star review and one 3 star review, you have 8 ticks out of 10, so a 0.8 review score. Now if we switch to 10 stars per review, a higher resolution, nothing really changes. One review (the unit) is still one review, and a 0.8 review score is still a 0.8 review score. If we treated a star as the unit, then some perfect reviews are worth 10 stars and others only worth 5. The fractional part protects us from changing resolution, but note that the price of this convenience is that we are subtly throwing away information.
[Heading 3] Precision
[normal] In decimal we will convert 12 stars out of 15 to 0.8, or 80% perhaps. In fractions we will reduce 12/15 to ⅘. In both cases we’ve converted 3 reviews to an infinitely precise 1 review equivalent, but the ‘true’ answer is the unreduced (and unwieldy) 12/15. As you know, a 5 star review with one rating is very different from a 4.9 star review with thousands of ratings.This is numeric information we had, but our number system consolidated it into something easier to understand by removing information. The same goes as we scale up, 100 miles away probably doesn’t mean you counted every mile – it is acceptable if it is actually 97 miles. We consolidate the resolution to something useful by removing distracting information. Our modern numbers are infinite precision, and we organically add back resolution with context and gut feelings.
[normal] This is great for people, but a real problem when trying to teach computers about the world. Resolution is important in our world and in our language, and our numbering system erases it automatically. Math with resolution requires new encodings and rules, but is the major unlock to getting computers to think more organically.
[Heading 1] Relaxing and Adding Constraints
[normal] And this opens up a whole wonderful rats nest of mathematics that is new. The point at 3 starts and ends at 3, so what about a number that starts at 3 and ends at 4? And what if our unit points in the opposite direction or isn’t anchored at zero and one? How would we add this to another number made of four unaligned values? What about multiplying these kinds of numbers, how would you multiply two offset segments on a numberline? What does it mean to have a unit that is positive like 2/7 vs a negative unit like 2/-7? And if I can divide a stick into 10 parts and pick a midpoint (5/10) isn’t that more accurate (higher resolution, more information) than if I can only divide it into two (1/2)?
[normal] By answering these questions to your satisfaction, you will not only have a novel way of looking at mathematics, but your mathematics will be in a position to describe and understand the conceptual world we so deftly navigate. And back down to earth, this may sound very abstract, but remember that it's our math that has abstracted reality, these values are the norm in both the real world and our pure imaginations.
[normal] If an amoeba has two light sensors that change as it moves, there is no zero point, no universal calibration, and yet it is able to do enough math on these two signals to usefully navigate its world.
[normal] If you take two sticks in a forest, call yourself a zero point (a fifth point ‘observer’ position if you will), you now have two things you can measure to some approximate resolution, where neither one is a zero based length. One can be the unit, the other the value, and two other sticks could for a similar number. If non zero measures like this are impossible to add or multiply in a useful and systematic way, it isn’t me coming up with some wild esoteric example – it's a failure of our mathematics to quantify and manipulate a very large portion of reality.
