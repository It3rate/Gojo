[Heading 1] Complexity Spaces
[Heading 2] Introduction
[normal] Everything we understand in the universe is derived from understanding quantities. Not just by way of math and numbers either, our thoughts, our perceptions and all meaning comes from comparing and manipulating things we quantify.
[normal] Surprisingly we don’t spend much time trying to understand how exactly this all works at a
[normal] fundamental level. As in the ‘pre-elementary school’ level of understanding, “how can things be measured?”, “what is a number?”, “what is addition?”, “how can you compare things?”. This level of questions are answered with tradition, not inquiry. With the advent of AI, we face machines that have no tradition other than what we give them. It is becoming necessary to revisit our traditions, clarify them, and come up with a more complete framework for understanding quantities. This new framework quantifies concepts using webs of related perspectives, each with an inherent micro-intelligence. In this way, intelligence is distributed throughout the network in spaces of varying complexity.
[Heading 2] What is a Quantity
[normal] All quantities derive from a measure of change. This may not seem intuitive because it is hard to isolate a single measure from its context. It’s like a deep sea fish has no idea of air, and therefore has no idea of water. Imagine living in a void - it isn’t black, it is the absence of the concept of light. You can’t be ‘here’ because there is no concept of ‘there’. There can’t be quantity, because there is no change.
[normal] It's only once there is a second thing to compare to — something different from the first — that the concept of quantity begins to emerge. It was cold, now it's hot. This is bigger, that is smaller. Two samples may not be able to fully calibrate and manipulate quantities, but it is a giant phase change in perception. The change allows us to notice a difference, and therefore notice the concept at all. This change can be over perceptible things like time, place, or scale, but it can also be fully abstract or imagined things. The most abstract ideas in math may never touch the real world, but they are only able to exist as ideas by exploring unequal things.
[normal] If existence is the transition from 0 to 1, comprehension begins at 2.
[Heading 2] Reality Gets a Vote
[normal] While quantities can be extended to represent anything knowable, real or imagined, this doesn’t imply that they are free of worldly rules. In fact the reason the world is interpretable at all is that these rules are very strict, and apply universally and consistently. Values can be ordered by some rule, and the interpretation of that ordering is unambiguous. Smallest to largest does not permit 4,5,7,6 as a possible order. Operations have their own logic, and it is consistent wherever applied. 2+2=4 in arithmetic, in temperature, or in imaginary unicorns. Math can be pure or applied, but the rules are indifferent to the environment that spawns them. The commutativity of addition will hold whether you're adding temperatures or debts or rotations. This is fundamental.
[normal] Rules of a space are substrate-independent, and not optional.
[normal] All this to say, the rules are paramount. They need to be extremely well defined and their implications fully explored. The mechanics of simple things like addition or multiplication should hold just as cleanly for areas, population growth, oscillations, and plain old numbers.
[Heading 2] Unknown vs. Infinity
[normal] When thinking of number limits, you could say either:
[normal] They go on forever
[normal] It is unknown when or even if they end
[normal] This may seem pedantic, but the implications split into very different systems. The first gives you infinite-precision real numbers. The second gives you bounded math with uncertainty as a first-class element. Both are mathematically valid, both are useful, but only B reflects how quantities actually behave outside the abstraction. Not defining and exploring this is at least an oversight. Real numbers are a simplification of quantities, a useful subset, but they hide deeper truths needed to fully encode concepts with numeric tools. The limits of many important things large and small are in fact unknown, and this matters. We will be emphasizing the latter definition in this framework, treating real numbers as a valid subset.
[normal] The first implication of this choice is the concept of an unknown value. This can be because something is unknowable, unexamined, not yet understood, or simply random. It can also be outside a known range, like something too heavy for your scale. We tend to focus on the knowable in cognition and math, but in reality most things are partly known and partly unknown. Unknown is a first-class element when dealing with quantities (and reality!), not an edge case.
[Heading 2] Bounds and Resolution
[normal] The second implication of unknown limits is that all measures need some ‘known’ bounds and a resolution. Infinity allows us to ignore the ranges we are operating in. Without it, we need to specify what limits exist on numbers we are using, at both the large and small ends. Everything beyond these limits can’t be expressed without changing the resolution.
[normal] We can express resolution using fractions with a slight twist. We can say 1/2 is lower resolution than 2/4, which is lower resolution than 1000/2000. The denominator matters here, as it signifies resolution. A button hole that needs to be 7/16 of an inch should be measured more carefully than a recipe that needs 1/2 a cup of flour. The value 1001/2000 would still round to 1/2 at low resolution, but they are different values in reality. The extra information is lost to the 'unknown' part of a measure at lower precision. Operations are affected by this too, for example a definition of addition needs to include rules for how 1/2 + 2/4 is handled.
[normal] Resolution matters at the high end in the same way. When you choose a precision cutoff, you implicitly choose a maximum and minimum bounds as well. If you choose not to define these, they become unspecified, which is very different than unbounded. A dog can be many weights, but outside certain ranges it is no longer a dog. Five hundred tons is not a dog. The exact boundary is unspecified, but the fact that there is an approximate boundary is part of what the word 'dog' means.
[normal] We use precision and bounds constantly in practice, whenever we estimate, whenever we choose a time step, whenever we chunk a task. But we treat these things as metadata, something written in the margins or assumed rules of thumb. This framework proposes precision and bounds are treated as first class elements of measures.
[normal] Together these two implications mean that every measure exists inside some framework that has resolution rules and information limits. There is a real separation between what is measured and what complexity space it is measured from. The space holds to rules, and the rules calibrate the measures.
[Heading 2] Complexity Spaces
[Heading 3] Degrees of Freedom
[normal] Every value has a context that calibrates it. The number seven means ‘seven ones’. Seven miles is calibrated with one mile. Simple versions of these spaces appear everywhere. Perhaps most obviously in geometry - points, lines, areas, volumes, but the ideas extend much further: color, sound, social interactions or biological niches are all calibrated within some kind of framing space. Everything measurable is measured against some complexity space. Everything transformed is transformed within the rules of one. Mathematically they are akin to vector spaces, but with a wider net cast.
[normal] A complexity space is defined by the number of types it contains and how the elements created within them interact. A single space is by definition limited in scope — it cannot fully describe things more complex than itself. In practice we operate with many spaces simultaneously, each containing a mix of elements from the thing we are examining. A geometric example may help clarify this, though keep in mind that geometry is a narrow, restricted subset of this larger system.
[normal] Think of the degrees of freedom a point has within the complexity space of each: a point, a line, an area, a volume. That’s 0 degrees of freedom for a point (fully bounded), 1 for a line, 2 for an area, and 3 in a volume.
[Heading 3] Containers and Constraints
[normal] Now consider a line in each of these spaces. A line in a point: 0. In a line: 2. In an area: 4. In a volume: 6. A line in a point can’t move either end, so still 0. A line in a line allows each of its endpoints to slide, so 2 degrees of freedom there. In an area, a line can move each point vertically and horizontally, so 4. And 6 degrees of freedom in a volume. The space constrains what is possible before any other rules apply.
[normal] Now add explicit constraints. A line on a line has two degrees of freedom, both endpoints can move changing the location and size. Lock the length though, and there is only one degree of freedom. No matter which point you adjust, the effect is the same: the whole line moves in unison, like a point. It is one degree of freedom living inside a two-degree space. The math for this constrained line is the same as the math for a point. Not because lines and points are secretly the same thing, but because we have imposed the same constraints on both. The math follows from the degrees of freedom and rules of the space, not from anything inherent in a line or point.
[Heading 3] Number Spaces
[normal] This dimension reduction works the same way if you lock a pivot point and allow only the length to change, only the far endpoint moves. Add a unit length anchored at the pivot point, and this works exactly our standard numbering system. For these zero-anchored lengths, we know how to add, multiply, and raise to powers, and we know how transforms behave: commutative laws, distributive laws, closure. None of that is some kind of ‘law of lengths’. It is how any system behaves under these particular constraints, which is why it holds equally for areas in 2D, volumes in 3D, and any other similarity-constrained system of zero-anchored magnitudes.
[normal] What happens if you don’t constrain the start point and keep the unit? You end up with two unit lengths, one for the start and another for the end direction. This allows the start point to be based on an inverted unit that is positive left (i) and the end point in our normal positive right system. The math that falls out is exactly complex number math, but applied to segments instead of lengths and rotations. I’ve covered this extensively in other documents, and it is one of the most interesting things this system has revealed so far. This doesn't make complex numbers based on rotations wrong. Other than the rotation modulus (circles can return to zero degrees every rotation), this is just another way into the same complexity space. Because the defining characteristics of the space are the same, the math has to be the same. There are many ways to arrive at this space across many domains – in physics, electronics, algebra, and everywhere else you see complex numbers or free segments along a path.
[Heading 3] Recognizing Perspectives
[normal] The important thing is to understand that these ‘spaces’ are simply perspectives, and multiple perspectives can all coexist on the same elements at the same time.  A square of fixed size moving around a plane has two degrees of freedom: vertical and horizontal. As it moves, every point on it moves too. It is *entirely possible* that there is another perspective that only sees the left edge as a line that grows and shrinks. Two perspectives are now running in parallel — one governing position, one governing size. This is not unusual. The world is full of competing and cooperating actions that ‘negotiate’ outcomes. The key to useful perception is identifying which perspectives have a signal and knowing how to interpret and manipulate them.
[normal] Applying these dimensionality tests methodically can reveal errors in our assumptions. For example we often think of area as two-dimensional, but area is a single value (e.g. 6cm²) that grows or shrinks, thus it is 1D. An X and Y axis is 2D because they haven’t been merged together, only grouped. So we create points such as (3x, 2y), and multiple points to get more complex paths. Once we multiply we get a merged area of 6, the 3x and the 2y are gone leaving only the 6. This is not a bug. Reducing dimensionality is what allows us to reduce any level of complexity down to the simple math that is relevant.
[normal] Apples are actually very complex objects, with an overwhelming number of inter-related attributes. True as that is, when adding the weights of two apples we don't have to account for atoms or skin blemishes. We have reduced the apples to one dimension, the weight, and can now use simple addition that works just like all other addition. This doesn’t mean the aroma or color isn’t important in some other perspective - maybe the skin blemish prevents one from ending up on the scale. This matters, but it matters to a different perspective running in parallel. In the physical world these attributes are all running and interacting continuously. It is our minds that have managed to reduce this to separate computable heuristics, allowing us to hallucinate the truth. Our minds, and perhaps soon our machines.
[Heading 2] Error and Opportunity
[Heading 3] Category Error and Measurement Error
[normal] If I was to guess the weight of your mother, sight unseen, how many ways could I be wrong? Leaving aside the question itself being a mistake, the errors fall into two categories. First is the guess itself is simply off - I guess 150 pounds and she’s a solid 300. This is ‘measurement error’. The second type of error is not using the right frame of reference to start with, category error. That could be wrong resolution (measuring in tons or picograms), or being out of frame bounds (9000 pounds, or 2 pounds). And just to be complete, guessing your mother’s weight to be reddish-blue is also a category error.
[normal] This separation is category vs measurement, the frame vs the contents. This is what is meant by the complexity space, and the measures calibrated within it. When we think about error, we almost always focus on the value and assume the frame. Even in math and science, the frame is usually taken for granted. When building a complexity space, though, we must specify not just the parameters and their transforms, but also the resolution and bounds. This  doesn't mean the frame is fixed — all its parameters are mutable, just like any other values. If I find out your mother is in fact 500 pounds, I simply have to update my weight range of plausible values for mothers.
[Heading 3] Error is Information
[normal] The opportunity in this is that error becomes quantifiable and correctable at every level. In current machine learning training, all the knowledge in the world is absorbed in each pass, and correction happens globally. At inference time, the model one-shots its best guess, from a top down whole world perspective. That this works at all is remarkable. That the whole endeavor requires computers the size of football stadiums, powered by nuclear reactors, grinding month after month, is equally unsurprising. Simpler intelligence distributed throughout the system has a chance to scale with much less compute with better heuristics, akin to machine calculation vs biological.
[normal] The reason we have one-shot error resolution is that our math can never be ‘wrong’. If I say pick a point on a numberline, there is no wrong answer. No segment is incorrect, no area is invalid, no volume is ever ‘not a volume’. There is only measurement error, but it is invisible in the system. If our measurement error is detectable within the system, every perspective will understand error pressure, and every complexity space can help correct it. Each space gains agency, each can negotiate its own goals, and each can contribute to outcomes it isn't even equipped to perceive directly. This is how biology works, because it is a more powerful architecture, compared to having some external intelligence correcting errors based on final outputs.
[normal] A geometric intuition helps here. Imagine a segment on a number line. Many points will fall outside it, but each one knows which direction they would need to move to end up inside it, or which direction the segment would need to move. Put segments on both an X and Y axis, and a random point is even less likely to fall inside their intersection. And if you have two areas on perpendicular planes, there is a good chance their projections will not intersect at all (more on this later). Each of these complexity spaces can negotiate with the others towards a preferred configuration, and each can do so using only local information. No perspective needs to understand any other, or even be aware of them, other than the tensions they create. The negotiation happens through shared substrate, not through explicit coordination.
[Heading 2] Communication
[normal] The beauty of multiple parallel perspectives is that communication between them is automatic. It requires no message-passing, no coordination layer. It happens because all the independent perspectives operate on the same substrate.
[normal] Think again of the square moving around a plane. The bottom-left point knows nothing of the square, but it still can ‘want’ to move to the right. The area knows nothing about position, but it can ‘want’ to remain the same size. These pressures are local, but act on each other, causing all points on the square to move rightwards together. The communication happens because they are all perspectives in the same space.  Even when there is a contradiction — say the top-right point is anchored — the system resolves it by having each perspective push its preference with some level of confidence and then react to whatever the resulting state is. An incorrect adjustment will increase the tension in the system, and self-correct. This type of error is also a feature, which can be used intentionally to explore beyond local minima. No perspective needs to understand a contradiction to participate in resolving it.
[normal] This is the key. There is no need for explicit cross-level communication. The resolution mechanism is how the system responds to forces, which is inherent in the complexity space itself. And it doesn’t mean everything needs to be deterministic, as there are plenty of unknowns and ranges of uncertainty.
[normal] This is exactly why car suspension systems and analog electronic circuits can be modeled with the same math. Engineers have explicitly used this analogy (the force-voltage duality) to design F1 dominating cars.  A discovery in one transfers directly to the other because they are the same space: the same elements transform in the same ways and produce the same results. It would be more surprising if that weren't true. It would mean math and logic were somehow environment-dependent, which would be their undoing.
[Heading 2] Level of Focus
[normal] A practical heuristic for problem-solving with distributed intelligence: start at the level of focus that matters, and reduce everything else to the minimum resolution needed.
[normal] If the problem is your chances against a bear, reduce the bear to a point, then add back only what seems relevant. The bear's weight can be estimated from its apparent size, which incorporates distance, rough fur density, and… oh my, saliva is flying. Nevermind, there is no need to focus on all that – at the resolution that matters, the size is very large. That is a one-dimensional, low-resolution estimate, and it converts immediately to 'much larger than you'. Do the same with speed: it is based on time and distance, which could be expressed in seconds or minutes, feet or inches, but again reduce it — very fast, especially relative to you. Now those two estimates can be multiplied in the complexity space of size and speed to give an estimated time of contact. Perhaps add in the flinging saliva, as a very low resolution “AHHH!”. The point of all this reduction is simplicity of calculation, and removal of distraction. Your legs will know they need to approximately “RUN!!” as soon as possible.
[normal] The point of perception and cognition is not precision. The point is for each element to be reduced to the right complexity level for the question at hand. And to have the math that applies at that level to be as straightforward as possible. This is the same principle for all levels of focus – dimensionality reduction isn't a loss of truth, it is a choice about which truth is load-bearing for this particular problem. The bear’s fur density still exists in its own perspective. It just isn't useful here.
[normal] This is complexity space reasoning in practice: reduce to the relevant dimensions, use the math inherent in those dimensions, and trust that important perspectives you've left out will have their say through other channels.
[Heading 1] The Math
[normal] Every perspective into a system consists of a number of elements, and how they interact. The thesis is that by describing the interactions of the elements, you are defining a complexity space, and the math of this space is inherent in the description. Simple spaces, like ordering, are so common we don’t even notice how we apply the same math in a multitude of unrelated situations. Others, like mutual attraction based on scale seem tailored for gravitational equations, yet similar math appears in economics, acoustics and electrostatic forces. This is just a problem with our intuition - we would be shocked to see linear order not be linear, but as systems get more complex we feel the math is emanating from the system, rather than inherent in the complexity space we model it with. We are able to hold many of these different perspectives in our heads at once, and by melding them together we create cognition, memory, and prediction.
[normal] Every value has a context that calibrates it. The number seven means 'seven ones'. Seven miles is calibrated against one mile. Simple versions of these spaces appear in geometry — points, lines, areas, volumes — but the idea extends much further: color, sound, social interactions, biological niches. Everything measurable is measured against some complexity space. Everything transformed is transformed within the rules of one. Mathematically they are akin to vector spaces, but with a wider net cast.
[normal] To understand this all, we need to start with a single element, and see what operations interacting with new elements allow.
[Heading 2] Datapoints
[normal] Single datapoints are the primitives of any perspective. Note that this does not mean they aren’t built of things more complex, or participating in a greater purpose. It is a choice to start the perspective at the level of the primitive. When counting cars on a road, a car is a good primitive, but when fixing a car it is not.
[normal] A geometric example would be points vs area. We know a rectangle of 2x3 has an area of 6, but it is less obvious that the area can be treated as the 0D primitive in a perspective. If you start from ‘area’ and exclude the lines and points that construct it, there isn’t even a size to it, it is just an area (it might even be a circle or other shape). With multiple areas you can sort them by size, say one is twice the size of another, and even multiply them. Area is a perfectly fine primitive unit, as are points, lines and volumes. If you want to include the side lengths and position of an area, you have to include the perspective of those lower dimensions. The position will have 2 dimensions at least (x,y), and the rectangle with have 4 elements (TB, LR) from 2 lines, along with merging operations. All of these can work together in the same space, or work in parallel in different spaces. But the key is that ‘area’, like anything else, can be a primitive in some chosen perspective.
[normal] Now notice how a single primitive, regardless how it exists in the greater reality, can not have any value attached to it without having something else to calibrate it. It can be ‘an area’ or ‘a point’ or ‘a color’, but without something to measure against, nothing more can be said about it. It is the working material, not information at this point. It is not null, it is valid, but it is uncharacterized. Mathematically we get null vs not null (not to dismiss this, it’s the mathematical equivalent of ‘let there be light’, just no information beyond that yet).
[Heading 2] Paths
[normal] A second data point means there is a difference, and therefore information. With each point on its own we get the first example of counting, but no relationship between them.
[normal] If the two are merged it implies a line, or at least some path between them. It’s best to think of this as a path, with a straight line the simplest version of it. This difference over time implies change, and the difference over samples implies category range. It is two points merged into a 1D path, and gives us our first ‘complexity space’. There is a start and end, a potential navigable space between them, and potential extrapolations beyond them.
[normal] It is worth looking closer at what a complexity space gives us. Ultimately is a frame we can use to create, categorize, understand and manipulate things that fit inside the frame. For a given path, we can add points or other paths to it, and at an even more basic level, we can say if other data can exist in that frame or not (null or not null).
[normal] As a concrete example, two temperature measures of 20 and 40 can allow other ranges in them (25->30) and single points (38). There are two possible error states - the temperature falls outside the frame (like -100, or 35->55), or the new datapoint does not belong in the frame (e.g. a distance is not a temperature, so null).
[Heading 2] Errors
[normal] Imagine the path of your drive to work. This is two datapoints interpolated (home->work) Adding a temperature datapoint to this path would not work, and would be null. A location in another city, or on another planet is also probably an error as it is so far outside the path it seems unrelated (unless you modify the ‘expected commute’ frame). Extrapolating beyond the ends of that path is also an error, but is something that has a fairly obvious fix (a pressure gradient that leads to a correct state). Someone else’s commute could be projected onto yours to compare, allowing things off the path to generate information. Even though the path is technically in 3D space, we can treat it like a line with bounds, and points further outside this range are in greater tension.
[normal] A very important point here is that the existence of error states in a complexity space is highly desirable. Where there is error/tension/surprise, there is a potential to learn. Our goal is to create a system where every perspective has intelligence, and the agency to express it. Agency is impossible with no information (I’m imagining a length, what is the length?), as well as with certainty (I’m imagining a length of 10 feet, what is the length?). With a bounded frame, we can apply ‘reasoning’ in reference to the frame. If someone says, ‘it’s hot today’ and it’s -5, you can adjust the frame (maybe yesterday was -20), or adjust the claim (that’s not hot!). It doesn’t mean the whole truth comes from this single frame of reference and data point, only that is allowed a say. The full context may be planning a picnic or snowboard trip, that is invisible to this perspective, but that doesn’t mean this perspective’s intelligence and agency isn’t an important part of a solution. We choose multiple perspectives that will help navigate a problem space in simple tractable ways. We don’t rate them by their size or complexity, but rather their utility.
[Heading 2] Paths and Proportions
[normal] The simplest complexity spaces have the fewest degrees of freedom. A segment of two fixed points can create a path, but it has no information as there is nothing to compare it to. The points are fixed, so we can’t generate a comparison of change over time either. If you properly isolate it, it isn’t even a segment, ‘like other segments’ because there are no other segments. It is just something that exists vs not exists. However! If we add a variable point to this segment, we enter perhaps the simplest complexity space there is, proportions. The point could be 30% along the path, or two segments could be created (0->point and 0->end) and the first would be 30% the length of the second. Other math is possible here, like comparing the first to second part (3/7), or starting from the end (70% from end). Any math you can think of with this information is valid in this complexity space.
[normal] THE MOST IMPORTANT THING to understand at this simple level, is that anything (anything!) that enters this space (a continuous path with two bounds, and a variable along it) will use the same math and get the same results. And all the math of this space will be valid, if not always useful or complete. There can be many spaces, and other spaces may distort the results of this simple one, and that is ok. The point is the math of this space comes from the space, not the temperatures, distances, or durations that create it. This may seem obvious at this level, but as spaces get more complex (a suspension system vs an electronic circuit) it will be tempting to think the math comes from ‘the stuff’ and not from the complexity space in inhabits. It would be absurd to think ratios of apples to oranges behave differently to ratios of apartments to houses, and this is true all the way up. If you correctly model something to enter some complexity space, the correct math will be waiting there for you.
[Heading 2] Paths and Numbers
[normal] Integers, Rationals and Real numbers all have one degree of freedom, but their complexity spaces are subtly different.
[normal] Integers are the simplest, where you have one fixed segment, the unit, and one variable segment that can only move at the resolution of the unit. The unit is by definition 0->1, and the numerator is 0->n, so one degree of freedom. More subtly there is a third segment, the numberline itself, which goes from 0->unknown for positive numbers, and -unknown->+unknown for all integers. The resolution trick to avoid decimals may seem like hand waving, but remember nothing in reality is infinite (if we choose to model reality), so the simplest system must also define resolution even if it is unknown. We define the maximum of the numberline as unknown (or as large as possible, or fixed to some value, or infinite), and the minimum resolution as the unit. We do define these lengths, and they are an important part of any numbering system. Integers in computers wrap around to negative when they overflow. That may be different in different domains, but the size of the numberline is part of the definition of an integer, even if it is defined as unknown or infinite. So in the end, integers are made of four segments (numberline, resolution, unit and value), with one degree of freedom. Unit and resolution are the same segment in the case of integers. Note, there is a lot of math you can do with integers, and as always, all integer math is valid for anything you correctly model as integers.
[normal] Real Numbers are like integers, with the only difference being the numberline and the resolution are explicitly defined as infinite. An infinite numberline matters a lot when used in computers (requiring positive and negative infinity values when things become impossible to express), but infinite resolution is the more obvious difference. Real numbers still have a fixed unit of 0->1, but now the value can use proportions to create decimals. Note that there is always a little lie happening, because technically if there is infinite resolution you can never fully express anything - you will be too busy with infinite sequences. So the reality of real number resolution is that it is variably rounded based on context, not infinite. The context is not explicit in the numbering system, so we usually default to ‘more bits that we need’. In the same way, the infinite numberline is usually just ‘bigger than we need’. This gives a unique flavor of math, the big gain is we get decimals. And again, all math valid here if valid for anything that enters this space.
[normal] Rational numbers (fractions) start with the same system as integers where the resolution is the unit, and they add a new degree of freedom with a denominator. This allows everything to work without arbitrary rounding, and it is still able to express any number real numbers can express. In theory real numbers have more precision, but in reality the numerator and denominator can both have as many digits as you want. The philosophy of the systems is quite different though, so this does lead to real differences. While rationals get around the arbitrary rounding issue of reals, they are also designed to be used by people (as they were the dominant numbering system for most of history). This explains the ‘rule’ where reduced fractions are considered to be the same number as unreduced, and the feeling is you should usually express fractions in their reduced form. You can see the issue when looking at imperial measurement wrenches. You get sequences like 3/8ths, 7/16th, and 1/2. The impulse is to have everything in 16ths, so 6/16th, 7/16th, 8/16th, however that would make 33/64th impossible if you ever want to increase precision. The metric (decimal) system doesn’t really fix that either, you have sizes like 8, 9, and 10, which is easy, but it makes the size 9.333 awkward if you ever need it. To summarize, rationals are made of 5 segments (numberline, unit, resolution, numerator, denominator) where the resolution is the unit, and there are two degrees of freedom, the endpoints of the numerator and the denominator.
[Heading 2] Other Number Systems
[normal] Are there other number systems possible on paths? Yes, of course. Many we know, and many we can invent for some specific need. Integers where the numberline starts at zero and goes to  a known endpoint with the wraparound rule create a ‘modulo’ number system of remainders, where “7 mod 3 = 1”. In science, significant figures are a rule system that tries to limit resolution issues with real numbers, at least to the nearest order of magnitude. Bases allow different notations of numbers, and while these are essentially the same as other underlying systems, you do get different intuitions with them (like how 16 is a very ‘round’ number in binary).
[normal] One of my preferences is a rational number system where you don’t simplify fractions, but use the denominator to express resolution. For example 200/500 is one hundred times more precise than 2/5. If you think of five star reviews, the first has 100 reviews and is probably pretty accurate, whereas the second implies only one person thinks it isn’t great (it might be new, or it might be unpopular, the person might be right or just cranky, so there is uncertainty). This is essentially just rational numbers without reducing, and where the denominator has to be chosen with intention. The unit is the resolution (a number of ticks that equal ‘one’), the value is the range (the extent of this number). This itself can be used as a parameterized complexity space that holds other values. Other values inside this space only need a single measure (e.g. 245 ticks), as they operate in the frame of the original unit and range. Of course if that space is 2D or complex, each element in the space will need a matching number of measures.
[Heading 2] The Lack of Error Problem
[normal] The one issue with all these number systems is they don’t allow error. Any number is considered valid, no matter how large and small, even if it can’t be fully expressed. While this seems (and is) normal in math, we always use numbers with implied and actual resolutions and bounds. If we make those implications explicit, we can mathematically detect when values are out of range or precision, and mechanically adjust things without relying on human judgement for intervention.
[normal] First, evidence that we actually do this. If we shift our definition of infinity to ‘larger than we use’ rather than infinite, we acknowledge the idea of a boundary at both large and small scales. This doesn’t lock us into anything, we can always create more capacity if we need, but it does force us to consider boundaries and their behaviors. This is how numbers in computers work, because like everything else we understand in the world, they are finite machines. We use integers, which are limited resolution, and switch to floats for decimals. We move from bits to bytes to ints to ints to ‘big numbers’ as we need more scale. There are many options, but there isn’t a universal ‘everything is true’ number, because there physically can’t be. This is just as true outside computers, albeit less obvious.
[normal] Second, evidence that we do this because it is both natural and useful. We use limits in both science and engineering, not just with significant figures, but also with units. We don’t express the distance to the sun in millimeters, nor the size of an atom in miles. There is an implied resolution to these units, and an implied scale. We express tolerance and error as ranges, and we need to make sure our units error doesn’t swamp calculations. We can’t measure a turtle’s speed over one second with a stopwatch using markers every mile). We also use ranges all the time, for example everything measured as a percent is based on a range of 0 to 100. This is the first complexity space we encountered that gave us proportions, calibrated to a round number in base 10. This doesn’t mean we must discard our regular use of numbers, you can choose the bounds to be larger than you use, and the precision 100+ decimal places if you like. It’s just that recognizing these limitations as a real thing allows us to leverage them when it is useful (which is very often once you get used to it, and all the time if you’re a machine).
[Heading 2] Adding Error
[normal] Adding error to the above number systems (complexity spaces) does not require much change. Integers already have built in resolution based on the unit size, meaning decimals are not possible. To create a range, all that needs to be done is to make the upper and lower bounds explicit, like 0 to 100 (percent), or -1000 to 1000. Proportions have the opposite problem, there are two fixed points forming a path, and a variable point inside that can move however it sees fit (no specified resolution). To specify resolution, you need a ‘tick’ size segment, or divide that path into a known number of segments. With 1000 ticks, you have a resolution of 1000, so 999/1000 is as close as you can get to maximum without being maximum. Note that these two systems now look more similar, a fixed number of divisions along a fixed length path.
[normal] Real numbers can be made finite by combining both of these systems. The unit is divided into a known number of ‘ticks’, and the scale of the numberline itself is specified. Again, this is not that unusual. If you have a tape measure, you will get 10 (or 16) ticks per division, and it will have a finite length. The decimal system itself does this, where we need to pick the number of decimal places to specify precision. The main issue is that it has to be a power of 10, which is not very precise. If you are counting 5 star reviews, it makes sense to have 5 ticks per number. 100 reviews would create a range of 100 to 500 (you can’t specify 0 stars for a review). You can see the complexity space that emerges here, it has a dynamic min and max range based on the count of reviews, and each review is a fixed number of 5 ticks. The math is inherent in that space, you don’t ‘decide’ that the lowest possible score is reviewCount/5. It is part of the space, not the review system, and is easily discoverable by both people and machines.
[normal] With this error, we can not only spot problems, but we can evolve the system to improve at the level it is at. With a little intelligence involved, one can imagine the review system discovering (over time) that expected range of 100 reviews will be 100->500 rather than 0->500. It then could adjust many of its outputs to be more accurate based on this new parameterization. The five star review system itself is usually part of a larger system that accommodates cost, seasons, reviews, country, UI, returns, self interest etc. The 5 stars still get a vote here, but other algorithms put pressure on it, and it may well adjust its range and precision over time. An intelligent local algorithm may discover that 4 stars are high praise in Japan and yet lukewarm in the US. It could then automatically adjust its output over time, with this new and improved calibration. This doesn’t have to come from a star rating algorithm looking at country data, it just feels different error pressure in each locale and adjusts itself to create more accurate predictions. This localized intelligence that can push and be pulled by external tensions (without inspecting and understanding things outside its domain) is the key to distributed intelligence, and highly capable machines.
[Heading 2] A Second Degree of Freedom
[normal] We now have a few complexity spaces that are both bounded and have a specified resolution. One aspect that we glossed over it the case where the bounds can be -1000->1000, or in the ‘100 five star ratings’ case, 100->500. This is obviously a range, which takes two numbers to encode, requiring two degrees of freedom. There are many ways to encode this, but most will lead to slightly different complexity spaces, so it does matter which you choose. Two numbers based on order works (100->500), but this does require rules to clarify ambiguity. For example, is (500->100) equivalent? Is the order min->max, or start->end? What would (500->100) imply here if order mattered? It seems to imply some kind of inversion of the numberline, and that would need to be clarified before the space could be used.
[normal] Another way to think about it is to embrace the inverting of the numberline and see what space this leads to. If a segment has a start and end, and we think of the end as ‘rightwards’ (in our positive-right system), then the start would be leftwards. What does rightwards mean? It means the unit points from zero to the right, which makes all numbers on the right of zero positive, and all on the left of zero negative. So by this logic, there would be an equivalent ‘start unit’ that points leftward (positive left), and this would encode the start point. We can call this the inverted unit (i), but it is as valid a unit as the rightward unit, it just encodes the start point, and the normal unit encodes the end point. This means that a start point at the -2 position would be encoded as ‘+2 inverted’, or 2i for short (positive is to the left for the start point). An end point of 6 would just be 6, as we normally encode numbers with this unit, as ‘positive-right’ segments from zero. We can now combine the start and end points by appending them, and we get 2i+6 to encode the segment from -2->6. Our star-rating system is -100i+500. Note that this is order invariant, 500-100i means the same thing. The start being leftward is just a choice, rotating the number line to be upside down would make the end point leftward and none of the math would change.
[normal] And speaking of the math, the math of this complexity space is pretty much the same as the math of complex numbers. Using a start point instead of a rotation means that there is a MOD option for the i variable, and of course it is parameterized differently (a numberline instead of radians). In spite of that, essentially all operations work the same even though the intuitions are very different. There are additional Boolean operations that make sense in this space, so the traditional complex number space is more a subspace of this. (I’ve written much more about this space elsewhere if you are interested).
[normal] This is not to say traditional complex numbers are wrong or misrepresented. They are a path into this type of space, one that has two degrees of freedom and two orthogonal units. Where the spaces are conceptually the same, the math is the same. There is no right and wrong here, as the truth comes from the space, not the model that brought you.
[normal] In that spirit, one can imagine the model where there is an X and Y axis and all segments start at zero being exactly the same as this segmental complex number space. And it is, as long as you choose to interpret it the same. You will discover things like positive area and negative length/area in both systems, but that isn’t too surprising when you consider 3*-2=-6 (we tend to default the area to positive, which is maybe the weird part). There are lots of things to explore in these spaces with two ‘types’ and two degrees of freedom, going forward we’ll use segments not anchored to zero as our mental model for it as this casts a pretty wide net into the space.
[Heading 2] Parameterizing Complexity Spaces
[normal] We’ve touched on parameterization of complexity spaces already, but we’ll make it explicit. If you have a proportion type space, it doesn’t matter how long it is or what the resolution is, it will be the same space. If you are 30% of the way between Fargo and Winnipeg, or 30% of the way through your coffee, it is the same space. It can be parameterized in metric, imperial, with large volumes or small frequencies. The space is structural, and the rules and relationships are what creates the structure.
[normal] The five-star review system has a dynamic range (1->5 vs 100->500) based on review count, and this can vary like the dynamic star count or sample count itself. This can still be considered one degree of freedom though, as the scale and resolution are either defined explicitly or calculated through rules. Only the number of stars on each review is not predictable. Expressed as an equation (which all complexity spaces can be), there is only one unknown that isn’t structural.
[Heading 2] Scaling Out
[normal] There are three ways to scale this system of complexity spaces discussed so far, in serial, in parallel, and increasing complexity by merging. First, flesh out more spaces by exploring things like new operations and behavior sequentially, over time. Second is to combine multiple spaces to run in parallel to give a higher conceptual understanding of what is being modeled. Third is to move to higher order spaces by adding degrees of freedom, or merging existing spaces together.
[normal] We will dive further into operations over time when discussing equations, but in short these are somewhat recursive in nature, and therefore serial rather than parallel. The simplest of these is powers, which is repeated multiplication. Sine and cosine are moving to a target over time, and logs/compound interest are recursive as well. The math is all correct here, of course, but it can be useful to think of these as calculations that happen over time, and model them that way as well. Serial and parallel is a very important distinction in nature, and we tend to blur it a little in math.
[normal] Running spaces in parallel is the key to conceptual understanding, where we have a variety of perspectives and get a ‘gist’ of the whole. Imagine seeing something moving towards a tree. We might make a size estimate along some expected size path, cat to truck. We can estimate speed with a few samples, distance over time, and that gives us momentum using speed and size. This tells us it will probably hit the tree, given the physics. However a parallel circuit tells us it is in the bear size range, and therefore is probably alive, so it will probably adjust course. Meanwhile the tree is tree size, has a large round vertical base, and is probably not moving. All this ‘negotiates’ in our mind to expect no collision, and we can use that new information to inform our ‘danger incoming’ mode to activate and adjust accordingly. This parallel execution of small commingled intelligences is the key to fast reaction and good prediction. A large model that considered the whole world for each thought would not be effective here, regardless of how well or poorly it scored in a classification metric.
[normal] Lastly we look at merging to expand into higher complexity spaces. Note first that merging is not running two in parallel. A length and a width separately can describe 2D paths, but only when they are merged can they describe an area. And no matter how many areas you use, it is only when two are merged that you get a volume.
[Heading 2] Scaling Complexity Spaces
[normal] Spaces can be scaled by adding degrees of freedom. This can be done by just adding a variable, such as we do going from 1D lines to 2D area to 3D volumes. This is always valid, as long as it can map to your problem space. We won’t go heavily into this, just to say these are all new complexity spaces, and they have the same ‘lack of error’ feature/problem as the number systems above. They work very well for certainty, and less well for uncertainty.
[normal] Note exactly what is happening to create these ‘higher order’ spaces, the values are being merged (multiplied) together. If they remain independent, like an XYZ 3D space, you can only define points in that space. The points can be merged into paths, areas or even volumes, but that will still require merges. And crucially, if you create a volume with say an area merged along a path, that volume is now 1D (it can be described with 1 degree of freedom, e.g. 30 liters). If you want to use the volume as well as the shape, you need to also preserve the line and the area it was extruded along, and use that information in that space. This is how complexity spaces naturally interact, even when there is no uncertainty – different perspectives contain different portions of the truth which work in tandem to construct a concept.
[Heading 2] Squaring Complexity Spaces
[normal] Another way to look at expanding complexity spaces is continually squaring them. So two points make a line, two lines make an area, and two areas make a volume. We usually think of three lines making a volume rather than two areas, however this binary-like progression has some interesting differences. If you think of intersections, it is easy to see that two values along X and Y will always create a valid rectangle when merged (2X and 3Y gives a rectangle with an area of 6). This is still true with non zero segments, where the segments (-2i+4)X and (-4i+7)Y intersect to create an area of 6, offset from zero in the top right quadrant. There are also invalid areas here, where anywhere outside the intersection could be considered ‘wrong’ and correctable.
[normal] The change comes when two areas combine to create a volume rather than 3 lines (an area using 4 numbers rather than 3). In this situation, two planes connect completely orthogonally at zero, tip to tip, with one plane rotated 90 degrees (see image). Now when creating areas on each of these planes you can see that it is not always possible to get an intersection. In many situations this is desirable, as it shows that increasing the complexity narrows what is valid, or even possible. For a contrived example, imagine temperature, water, and flowrate through a pump. For temperature and water, all states are possible, just not always desirable - very hot is steam, very cold is ice, and we prefer the range of liquid. Now when adding the pump speed, things outside certain ranges are just not computable - you can send ice or steam through a water pump, so the correct answer is more like null than 0. You can move parameters around to get to a non-null state by looking at valid areas where the three intersect. With three lines, you just get three numbers with flow rate being zero, but there is no way of knowing that there is error in the mechanism - that would be a valid zero if the pump was turned off for example. As you scale up in dimensions, it is generally harder to get a conceptually meaningful intersection. Every extra parameter has a chance of being an outliner, or just being incompatible with the interpretation of the system. This also gives a natural projection into much higher dimensions, where a ‘super volume’ is just two volumes merged (and of course this isn’t just about geometry, there are plenty of systems with more than ‘three’ dimensions).
[Heading 2] Modify and Synthesize
[normal] We can think of operations as synthesising new elements, or modifying existing ones. This distinction is important, but it is blurrier than it may seem, so it is worth a closer look. If you have independent horizontal and vertical lines, you can stretch or shrink either one. This is modifying existing elements, and is necessarily a sequential step, because there is a before and after. You can also group them to make an XY grid, or you can merge them to make an area. These are new elements that have been synthesized, but we must be careful with the ‘new’ word here. They are not new in the atomic variable sense, where they sever all connections with what created them.
[normal] Creating an area by merging two orthogonal lines does synthesize a new perspective, however the area we created is not independent of the original lines unless we explicitly make it so. I can measure the surface area of a balloon and then blow it up. Depending on the perspective I choose, that value will now be larger (always measure the surface area of said balloon) or stay the same (the original surface area). Either way, that value is tied to some state of the balloon, and comes from some measurements on it. We can choose to wipe all history of the measurement, and just use it as a unit of some sort, like we do with horsepower or ‘foot’, but that needs to be a choice. If my foot or horse grows, I don’t expect to change my unit, where if my balloon surface area is estimated by its diameter, I expect it to change as the diameter increases. Most ‘new’ synthesized elements retain their connection to the original source, which is why we can multiple perspectives all usefully contribute to the truth of a concept.
[normal] This goes the other way as well, modification of an existing value is also subtly a synthesis. We can take a segment of 3 units, and add 2 to make the segment 5 units. Or we can stretch it to be twice the length, to 6 units. These are both modifications of the original, but there is a merge step in there. 3+2 is 5, not two segments that add up to 5 (that would remain a grouping). Stretching a segment (multiplication) does need to specify how much it will stretch by, 2 in this case. There are many ways to mentally model this, but they all involve a second value that participates in the amount of stretching. Even along a line you have in some sense merged these two values. So while it is easy to imagine you have the same element after the operation, it is important that your process clarifies what exact modify/synthesise behaviour you are modeling. This is less important in one simple equation, but as multiple equations connect into different perspectives of the same conceptual object, these distinctions become material.
[Heading 2] Operations
[normal] Most operations are between two elements, and there are surprisingly few of them. First, for a single element, the only operation they can do on their own is create/delete. In computers, increment and decrement are considered ‘unary’ operations for chip architecture reasons, but they require the +1 or -1 so are in fact addition. Two element operations boil down to grouping, appending, scaling, and boolean operations.
[normal] Grouping is akin to a set in its most basic form, but also includes constructing complexity spaces. For example, two lines can be grouped as a set of two, but also can be partially merged by joining the zero points and making them orthogonal. They are merged but not fully solved (fully merged would be an area), in that the original lines still exist, and they can be used to calibrate other values that exist in the new complexity space. This partial grouping is similar to the difference between solvable and partially solvable equations (3*2+1 vs A*B+C). In the latter, values can be plugged in to explore the equations, where in the former there is no such flexibility.
[normal] Appending is addition, which of course includes subtraction. This is a two step process, first grouping values, and then merging in a way that preserves all their values. Thinking geometrically we need to imagine some algorithm, like sorting tip to tail, but it is better to think of something like weight, where the positions don’t play a part. You can put apples in a bag, and from that bag you can get a 1D weight that is independent of all sorting. In fact that bag could be a blender, and the addition would still be valid for the resulting applesauce. That value becomes its own thing, and the original apple attributes are not visible from its perspective.
[normal] Scaling is multiplication, which includes division. We can think of this geometrically as stretching, which is recursive in the sense of increasing or decreasing relative to its original self. And again, it does require a second value to be involved, but more as a director than ‘contributor of stuff’. Notice that scaling can be thought of as continuous over time, where addition is a two step process of group->merge.
[normal] Boolean operations are a one step operation, and have a wider variety of outcomes that can even move into higher or lower dimensions. They are under-represented in our school curriculums, but are vital to things like logic and computation. There are commonly known operations like AND, OR, and NOT, but in fact for two elements, there are 16 possible bool operations, and all of them matter. We usually do bool operations on true/false, or bits, but the same logic applies in higher dimensions. We see higher dimensions with the union and intersection of rectangles or segments, and this is probably a more useful way to develop a good intuition here.
[normal] First thing to notice is bool operations are one-shot. You have source and target segments, one operator, and the result is instant. If two segments don’t touch, the intersection (AND) of them is null. The target segment doesn’t gradually get shorter and disappear, it is immediately gone.
[normal] Second thing to notice, we had one target segment, and now we have no result segment. That is a reduction of elements from 1 to zero. In the same way, if we union the same segments (OR) we start with one target segment and end with 2 result segments. We have increased the complexity.
[normal] The third thing to notice is in simpler complexity spaces, bool operations can mimic scaling or appending, but this doesn’t mean they are the same. My favorite example is the way explain 2*3 to a child using a grid. It creates a rectangular box in the top right quadrant with an area of 6. Yet -2*-3 is also 6, and we feel less comfortable putting the same resulting box in the same position. -2*3 is even stranger, as we have no way to display negative area. Viewing this as an ‘OR’ operation, with segment intersection makes more sense here. This is explained when we leave our anchored zero point, and let both of those segments moved freely (detached segments as complex numbers). Here the intersections are still intuitive, where to get a rectangle of area 6 sitting at any position in the grid. Multiply this gives a different answer, the same answer you get when multiplying complex numbers. And yes, this is still intuitive, and it still can describe reality. It is a much more complex perspective though, and probably isn’t suitable for explaining multiplication to children.
[normal] Here are the 16 boolean operations for reference. This may seem like too much detail, and indeed they can be categorized in simpler sets, but this is the raw form of them. They are key operational primitives for combining elements, and very much worth internalizing.
[Heading 2] Decisions
[normal] Before equations can be usefully sequential, they must be able to adjust to the previous steps output. This pressure can come from the results of running the equation. Imagine stretching something to be twice as large in slow motion, there is a ‘larger’ pressure at each moment until the equation is resolved. The ‘times two’ is slowly used up as it approaches equilibrium.
[normal] We often model decisions as choices and branching, but in a dynamic system it is often more useful to resolve over time. This allows all inputs and perspectives to ‘have a vote’, meaning potential solutions can be probed and even reversed.
[normal] Imagine the example of a dog running towards a tree. We can calculate its range of motion given its speed and weight, giving some range from the left to the right of the trunk. We can ‘NOT’ the tree part, leaving a left side or right side path. At this point we might randomly pick left or right, if/then style. However in a dynamic equation with multiple perspectives we don’t need to go that far. The ‘vote’ from this level is maybe left, maybe right. Some other perspective may know about the rabbit it’s chasing, or the stream it wants to drink from, and weigh things in that direction. The goal is to allow each perspective to contribute accurate information from its view, which means not underspecifying, but also not over-specifying.
[Heading 2] The Time Dimension
[normal] Many common equations are actually dynamic adjustments over time. This allows the equation to examine the output and recycle that into new values. Every sequence works like this, as well as sign waves and most of calculus. Sometimes we can jump ahead with clever calculation, but we may not want to do that when we have many variables interacting. Not only does this allow a negotiated truth, but it allows us to narrow in on local truths as new information comes in. What starts looking like a line, may look more like a curve after a few more data points, and eventually resolve to assign wave.
[normal] This time difference can be backed into other simple equations such as multiplication, and even addition. This is, of course, the choice of the perspective, but the only truly one shot equations are creation, deletion, and Boolean operations. This allows complex dynamic operations to run in parallel over time, even if some are not considered continuous.
[normal] To reiterate, there is no ‘always correct’ way to set up a complexity space, it will depend on what you are modeling and how you choose to interpret it. Take two orthogonal unit lines. If you just add them together you get an L shaped path, and then merging them with multiplication gives an area. As you look at this over time, you can imagine a stair step diagonal that seems more continuous with finer resolution steps - the result looks like a diagonal line, but its length is always 2. Alternatively it could be continuous merging of the lines, and you end up with a diagonal, with the length being sqrt(2). These are all valid, and they have different implications. The important thing is these choices are first class properties of equations, and should not need to be inferred from notes in the margin. Or worse, not even noticed in their absence.
[Heading 2] Calibrated Expressions are Complexity Spaces
[normal] This brings us closer to the final form of complexity spaces, calibrated equations. When a full equation has no variables it is just a fact, 2*3=6. With one variable it can be solved (or not), 2*3=?, so it has use, but only in its exact context. With higher variability equations have different interpretations, which is bad for calculation, but good for exploration. Equations like 2a+2b<24 are unsolvable, but fit questions like “I have 24 inches of moulding, what sizes of picture can I frame?”. An equation like this allows you to plug in multiple values and compare them, and is more useful with other equations, like “how much space on the wall”, “what is the smallest reasonable frame”, “how are the people in the photo positioned” etc. Having resolution, ranges, and free variables allows all equations to work together to negotiate a solution through iteration. It is also true that the mathematical behavior of 2a+2b<c is not something that needs to be invented or declared, it is inherent in appending and scaling.
[normal] Choosing the correct equations to model a problem is usually the difficult part, especially for a machine. The advantage of multiple equations iterating towards a better solution is you don’t have to know the solution before starting, and the answer doesn’t need to be perfect before being useful. The machine may start modeling squares (4a<24), and while not perfect, it does give a good approximation. Later it can add an aspect ratio equation, and the two can negotiate a size. These may even merge into the single equation above over time. It also allows higher resolution adjustments, like how the inner diameter will be smaller than the outer one, how that depends on the thickness, and how a mat overlay can affect usable size. All of these are valid, but some are lower levels of detail and may only become important at later stages. Using multiple partial equations with simple math and knowable error is a much more flexible system, and is much easier for machines to learn with.

[Heading 2] Types and Mathematical Implications
[normal] // my edit: The main purpose here is to say types can often infer how calculations are done. If you add two lines on the same numberline (same type) they append, but if you add orthogonal lines you get an L shape. Multiplying the same types gives acceleration (the multiplication must happen over time), different types give area. [normal] A 'type' in a complexity space is the label that says what kind of thing you're measuring. Types are not just labels for human convenience, they are structural constraints that determine which operations are valid, and which results are meaningful. When you add two temperatures you get a temperature. When you multiply a distance by a time, you don't get a distance or a time, you get something new. This is not just dimensional analysis — it is the complexity space telling you which operations keep you inside it, and which ones create a new one.
[normal] Types create partitions in a complexity space, and those partitions have hard edges. You can add apples to apples and get apples. You can multiply apples by a scalar (dimensionless count) and get apples. But you cannot add apples to oranges and get apples, you get 'fruit', which is a different type in a different space. This is not pedantry — it is the fundamental mechanism by which complexity spaces avoid collapsing into undifferentiated noise. Maintaining type integrity is what makes a space coherent.
[normal] The mathematical implication of this is that operations must be type-aware. The addition operator for temperatures is not the same operator as the addition operator for velocities, even if both operators produce the same numeral. 5°C + 5°C = 10°C, and 5 m/s + 5 m/s = 10 m/s, but the internal logic is different because the spaces are different. Temperature is a 1D space with an absolute zero bound — you can't go below it, and the zero has physical meaning. Velocity lives in a signed space where zero is a valid midpoint, and direction matters. This is why unit systems exist, and why they are not optional bookkeeping. The type is load-bearing.
[normal] Where this gets interesting is when operations cross type boundaries intentionally. Multiplying length by width synthesizes area — a new type. This new type inherits constraints from both parents (non-negative, bounded) but also generates new constraints of its own (area scales as the square, not linearly). The new space is not reducible to either original space, and yet it would not exist without both. This is the mechanism by which all higher-order complexity spaces are constructed. Physics, chemistry, biology, and economics are all doing exactly this: composing lower-order type spaces into higher ones, where new math is waiting that could never have been anticipated from the components alone.
[normal] The practical implication for machines is that type errors are not just code bugs — they are signals. If an operation produces a value outside the valid type, that tension is information. It means either the input was wrong, the operation was wrong, or the complexity space was misspecified. All three are correctable, but only if the type violation is first class and inspectable. In a system with no type bounds, the error is silent, and no learning happens. In a system where types have real mathematical weight, error propagates back through the system and drives correction. Type integrity is not a constraint on what the system can compute — it is the engine of the system's ability to learn.
[Heading 2] Uncertainty and Randomness
[normal] Uncertainty and randomness are often treated as interchangeable. They are not, and conflating them is one of the more subtle mistakes in quantitative reasoning. Uncertainty is a property of the observer — a measure of what is unknown given available information. Randomness is a property of the system — a measure of the irreducible variability in outcomes even given complete information. In practice, distinguishing them is hard. But conceptually, the distinction matters enormously.
[normal] In the complexity space framework, uncertainty corresponds to an underspecified or uncalibrated space. You have the frame, but you don't have enough data to locate yourself in it precisely. The bounds exist, the resolution exists, but the variable is undetermined. This is a perfectly valid and useful state. It is not the same as having no space at all, which would be null. Uncertainty has a direction — you can say 'it is probably in this region' and express that as a sub-range within the full space. It has structure, and that structure can be manipulated.
[normal] Randomness, properly understood, is what you get when the complexity space itself generates values without external instruction. The complexity space still constrains the outputs — a fair die still gives integers from 1 to 6, never 3.7 or 100. The outputs are bounded by the space. But within those bounds, the specific value is undetermined not because of ignorance but because variability is inherent. A complexity space with genuine randomness produces samples, and those samples collectively populate the space in a way that reveals its structure over time.
[normal] The useful thing about separating these two concepts is that they interact differently with learning. Uncertainty decreases with information — more data, better calibration, tighter bounds on the unknown variable. Randomness does not decrease with information — it is a feature of the system, not a gap in our knowledge. What does change with information is our model of the distribution: where the random outputs are dense, and where they are sparse. This is the shape of the space, and that shape is learnable even if the individual samples are not.
[normal] In any real system, both are present at once. The weight of a dog is uncertain (we haven't weighed this particular dog) and also has genuine variability (different dogs have different weights, and the same dog's weight varies over time). A complexity space for dog weight must accommodate both: a plausible range based on type (not 2 pounds, not 500 tons), a distribution within that range that reflects observed variance, and an unknown sub-range for this specific dog that narrows as new data arrives. These three layers — type bounds, distributional shape, and point estimate — are all distinct properties of the space and need to be maintained separately for the system to reason correctly.
[normal] The most honest representation of any quantity is therefore not a number, but a complexity space with explicit bounds, a known or inferred distribution over that range, and a current estimate with a confidence window. Real numbers strip away all of this except the estimate, which is why they are so convenient and why they hide so much. A machine that operates directly on complexity spaces retains all three layers, and can therefore express not just what it thinks the answer is, but how confident it is, what would change its mind, and when it should defer to more information.
[Heading 2] Negotiating Truth
[normal] In most formal systems, truth is determined by a single authoritative resolution process. An equation is solved, an argument is validated, a classification is assigned. This works well when the problem is well-posed and the space is fully specified. It works poorly when the problem involves multiple competing perspectives, incomplete information, and real-world messiness — which is to say, it works poorly almost everywhere that matters.
[normal] Complexity spaces offer a different model. Because every perspective operates on its own subspace with its own math, and because all subspaces ultimately operate on the same substrate, truth can be negotiated rather than declared. Each perspective applies its own constraints and pushes toward its own preferred region of the shared space. The outcome is not determined by any single perspective but emerges from the tension between all of them. This is not a compromise in the pejorative sense. It is the correct epistemics for a world where no single observer has complete information.
[normal] Consider again the commute path example. Your knowledge of where you need to go constrains the path in one way. Traffic conditions constrain it another way. Your fuel level applies pressure toward certain choices. Your habit of avoiding the highway applies a soft constraint with low confidence. None of these perspectives fully determines the route, and each one has legitimate information that the others lack. The resulting path is the negotiated truth across all of them. Change one constraint — you're running late, fuel is low, road is closed — and the negotiation re-resolves to a new outcome without any perspective needing to understand the others. Each perspective just pushes harder or softer based on its own internal state.
[normal] This has a precise mathematical structure. Each complexity space defines a region of valid states. The negotiated truth is the intersection (or nearest intersection) of all valid regions across all active perspectives. When perspectives agree, the intersection is large and confidence is high. When they conflict, the intersection is small or empty, which is not a failure — it is a signal that something in the system is under tension and needs revision. An empty intersection is more informative than a false consensus.
[normal] The resolution mechanism is iterative. Each perspective samples the current state of the shared space, evaluates it against its own constraints, and pushes the state toward its preferred region. This is not sequential computation where one step completes before the next begins — it is continuous and parallel. The state evolves as a function of all the pressures simultaneously. Convergence happens when no perspective has strong remaining pressure, meaning the current state is simultaneously acceptable to all active views. This convergence may not be global truth, but it is local truth — the best available answer given the perspectives in play.
[normal] This also explains why adding more relevant perspectives generally improves the quality of the result, and why irrelevant perspectives add noise rather than harm. A perspective with no information about the current problem has a flat distribution — it applies no pressure in any direction. It doesn't corrupt the negotiation, it simply abstains. A relevant perspective with strong information applies strong pressure and meaningfully shapes the outcome. The system is naturally weighted toward informed perspectives without requiring any explicit weighting mechanism. The confidence of each perspective is expressed through the tightness of its constraints, and tighter constraints apply more pressure. This is not a design choice imposed on the system — it is a consequence of the complexity space geometry.
[Heading 2] Simple Sample
[normal] To make this concrete, take a simple worked example: estimating the height of a door from limited observations.
[normal] We begin with type bounds. A door is a vertical rectangular opening in a wall, sized for a human to pass through. Without measuring anything, we already know the answer lives in a range. It is not one inch — a human cannot pass through. It is not thirty feet — no wall in a residential building is that tall. The type itself, 'door for human passage', constrains the space to roughly 6 to 10 feet, or in our native resolution, somewhere around 180 to 300 centimeters. This is the first complexity space: a bounded range derived from type membership alone.
[normal] Now we observe. We see the door from across the room. We can estimate scale relative to the person standing next to it. That person is probably adult height, call it 170-185 cm. The door appears to be somewhat taller than the person. This gives us a proportion: roughly 1.05 to 1.15 times adult height. Running the proportions against the height estimate puts us in the range of 180-210 cm. We've narrowed the space considerably, not by measuring but by applying a second perspective — relative proportion — that operated in parallel to the type-bound estimate and intersected with it.
[normal] We now have two active complexity spaces: the type-bound space (180-300 cm) and the proportion-derived space (180-210 cm). Their intersection is 180-210 cm. The negotiated truth has become tighter. Notice that the type bounds are now fully contained within the proportion estimate — the type constraint is no longer the active limiter. If the proportion estimate had said 350 cm, we would have a conflict. Either the type bounds are wrong (maybe this is an industrial space with unusually tall doors), the proportion estimate is wrong (maybe the person is shorter than assumed), or the model is wrong in some other way. The tension is informative rather than silent.
[normal] A third perspective enters. We recall that standard residential door frames in North America are typically 6 feet 8 inches, or about 203 cm. This is a strong prior with high confidence. It applies pressure strongly toward 203 cm. The negotiated truth now sharpens around a point: somewhere in the range of 198-210 cm, with a center of mass near 203. The specific values will depend on the confidence we assign to each perspective, but the process is the same regardless of scale.
[normal] Now suppose we walk over and measure: 198 cm. Each of our perspectives was partially right and partially wrong. The type bounds contained the true value. The proportion estimate overshot by a small amount, suggesting the person we used as reference was slightly shorter than average. The standard door prior was close but off by a small amount, suggesting this door was custom-fit or slightly non-standard. All three perspectives update. The type bounds remain roughly the same. The adult height estimate should shift slightly downward. The standard door prior's confidence decreases slightly — this building may use non-standard sizes. None of these updates require any perspective to inspect the others. Each one sees only the final measured value and compares it to its own estimate, generating its own error, and adjusting its own parameters. The learning is distributed, local, and automatic.
[normal] This is the mechanism at the heart of complexity spaces applied to real inference. The simplicity of the sample belies the generality of the process. The same structure — type-bounded priors, proportional observations, distributional knowledge, iterative intersection, and local error-driven update — operates identically whether you are estimating door heights, diagnosing diseases, pricing financial instruments, or understanding natural language. The math is not tailored to the domain. It is inherent in the structure of the space. The domain only determines which perspectives are active and what the type bounds are. Everything else follows.
[Heading 2] Parsing and Training
[normal] Parsing is the act of mapping raw input onto complexity spaces. Training is the act of updating those spaces based on the error between expected and observed outcomes. They are two sides of the same process, and understanding them through the lens of complexity spaces clarifies both what machines are doing when they learn and what they could do better.
[normal] When a machine parses a sentence, it is not reading words. It is progressively narrowing a large and poorly specified complexity space down to a more specific one. The input 'the cat sat' activates type constraints immediately: there is an agent, there is an action, there is an implied location or surface. These type constraints do not wait for the full sentence — they apply pressure as soon as the relevant tokens arrive. By the time the sentence is complete, multiple parallel perspectives have been running: lexical identity, syntactic role, semantic plausibility, pragmatic context. Each perspective contributes its constraints to the shared space, and the negotiated truth is the interpretation with the best intersection of all of them.
[normal] This framing explains a phenomenon that appears mysterious in standard treatments: why context changes meaning. 'The bank was steep' and 'the bank was closed' use the same word in different senses. The reason is that surrounding context has already applied pressure from multiple perspectives before the ambiguous word is encountered. The complexity space has been pre-narrowed by prior constraints. 'Steep' enters a space already shaped by terrain-adjacent terms, and 'closed' enters a space already shaped by commercial-entity terms. The word itself does not resolve its own meaning — the negotiated truth across all active perspectives does.
[normal] Training in this framework is not about adjusting a single large model. It is about updating the parameters of each complexity space — its bounds, its distribution, its resolution — based on the local error signal each space receives. When a prediction is wrong, the error propagates back not as a uniform gradient across the entire model, but as specific tension in the specific spaces whose constraints were violated. A space whose constraints were accurate gets weak correction. A space whose constraints were badly wrong gets strong correction. The correction is proportional to the surprise.
[normal] The advantage of this over current training paradigms is locality and composability. Current large models learn from error at the global level: a single prediction is compared to a single target, and the correction ripples through billions of parameters. This works, but it requires enormous compute to do because each correction must travel through the entire model. If learning happens at the level of individual complexity spaces, each space corrects itself based on its own local error, with no need to backpropagate through all other spaces. The spaces communicate not through explicit message passing but through the shared substrate — exactly as described in the 'Communication' section. A correction in one space changes the shared state, which creates new pressure in adjacent spaces, which update in turn. The global correction emerges from local updates the same way global behavior emerges from local physics.
[normal] This also resolves the 'catastrophic forgetting' problem. In standard training, updating the model to perform better on new data can degrade its performance on old data, because the same parameters serve both. In a complexity space model, each space is responsible for its own domain. Updating the door-height space does not affect the dog-weight space. They do not share parameters. What they share is structure — the general framework of bounds, distributions, and negotiation — and that structure does not need to be updated when domain-specific parameters change.
[normal] The deepest implication is about what it means to understand something. In the current paradigm, understanding is approximated by pattern matching over a very large training set. The machine knows what 'cat' tends to follow and precede, but it doesn't know what a cat is in any structural sense. In a complexity space model, a concept is encoded not as a distribution over tokens but as a structured set of spaces with explicit bounds, types, and relationships. Understanding a cat means having active spaces for its size range, weight range, behavioral characteristics, typical environments, and the relations between all of these. Each space can be queried, updated, combined with other spaces, and used to generate novel inferences that were never explicitly in the training data. This is not a goal for some future system. It is what the complexity space framework is designed to produce, and the math for it — as we have seen throughout — is not new. It is the same math that has always been waiting in the structure of the spaces, ready to be used.

[Heading 2] Machine Strategies
[normal] The human process of building intuition for complexity spaces is slow, biological, and largely invisible to the person doing it. A child doesn't consciously construct a complexity space for temperature — they accumulate experiences, feel the boundaries, develop a sense of proportion, and eventually arrive at something that works well enough. This process takes years and is not particularly inspectable or transferable. A machine can do something structurally similar but with important differences: it can be systematic about it, it can run many attempts in parallel, and it can be explicit about what it is doing at each stage.
[normal] The key insight is that given a set of known variable parameters, a machine can automatically generate candidate complexity spaces and test them. If you know that some system involves temperature, pressure, and volume, those three are variable parameters of known type. The machine does not need to know which complexity space they form — it can try the candidates. Three independent 1D spaces. A 2D space merging two of them. A 3D space merging all three. Various combinations of bounds and resolution. For each candidate space it tries, the math is not invented — it is waiting. The rules of a 1D bounded space are fixed by the structure of that space. The rules of a merged 2D space follow from the merge. The machine is not choosing the math, it is choosing the space, and the math comes along automatically. This is the computational leverage: the search space is over structural configurations, not over arbitrary functions.
[normal] This means the machine's job at each step is narrow and well-defined. Pick a configuration. Run the math inherent in it. Measure the error against observed outcomes. Adjust. The adjustment has three distinct levers, and they operate somewhat independently. The first lever is the parameters themselves — the specific values assigned to bounds, resolution, and distribution within a given space. These are the cheapest adjustments, equivalent to fine-tuning within a frame that already fits. The second lever is the rules of the space — the operations allowed, the type constraints, the topology. These are more expensive changes, because altering the rules changes what all the parameters mean, not just their values. The third lever is the structure itself — removing a space that adds noise, combining two spaces that turned out to be redundant, splitting a space that was carrying two independent signals that interfere with each other. These are the most expensive adjustments, but also the ones that produce the largest improvements in representational accuracy.
[normal] A machine that can move all three levers — tuning parameters, updating rules, and restructuring the space topology — is doing something qualitatively different from a machine that can only adjust weights. It is not just fitting a model to data. It is discovering which model structure the data is most consistent with. The outer loop is structural search; the inner loop is parameter fitting given a structure. Both loops generate error signals. The inner loop generates measurement error within a fixed space, which is the familiar kind. The outer loop generates structural error — the residual tension that remains after the inner loop has converged, which indicates that the space itself is a poor fit, not just its parameters. Both kinds of error are real, both are learnable, and keeping them separate prevents the common failure mode where parameter overfitting masks a fundamentally wrong structure.
[normal] Over time and across many domains, this process produces something that looks like taste — a trained sense for which configurations tend to work and which don't. A space that keeps producing null intersections in practice is a bad space regardless of whether it is mathematically valid. A space that consistently converges quickly and produces low residual tension is a good space. This accumulated record of which configurations have worked, and in what contexts, is the machine's structural intuition. It is not stored as rules but as weighted priors over the configuration space. New problems are approached not by searching blindly but by sampling configurations that have historically been productive for problems with similar parameter signatures.
[normal] This is not hypothetical — the mechanism is already partially visible in how large language models work. LLMs are trained on text and code, and both of these are dense with implicit complexity space information. When a programmer names a variable temperature_celsius and constrains it between -273 and 1000, they are encoding type, bounds, and resolution in plain text. When a physicist writes that pressure and volume are inversely proportional at constant temperature, they are describing the topology of a two-dimensional space. When a novelist describes a character as 'somewhere in their mid-forties', they are expressing a bounded estimate with low resolution. Every well-written piece of human text is, among other things, a description of complexity spaces and how they interact. LLMs absorb this incidentally while learning to predict tokens, and the result is that they develop something resembling structural intuition — they know that pressure and temperature tend to appear together, that ages are bounded, that financial quantities have a sign. This is real and useful, but it is also incomplete, because it was never the explicit training objective.
[normal] A machine trained with complexity spaces as a first-class target — not just absorbing them from text incidentally, but actively constructing, testing, and refining them as part of the learning process — would develop structural intuition more directly and more robustly. Language and code remain valuable sources, not merely because they contain facts but because they encode, in recoverable form, the complexity space judgments of everyone who has ever written carefully about anything. The indirection through text is actually an asset: human language has been pressure-tested across billions of domain-specific problems, and the structural choices that survive in common use are the ones that work. Reading that record carefully is one of the fastest ways a machine can bootstrap its prior over which configurations tend to be productive — and then it can go beyond what the text contains by exploring configurations that no human has named, using the same math that was always waiting in the structure of the spaces.
